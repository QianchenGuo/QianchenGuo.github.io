"On Advanced Algebra" is a response of mine that I am quite proud of, where I lay out my understanding of the logical thread of advanced algebra. Recently, as the new semester begins and freshmen arrive, some classmates have approached me for help with linear algebra questions. Reflecting on my own experience back then, and given the current state of linear algebra teaching in China, I cannot help but feel a deep sense of frustration and helplessness. This has inspired me to write this article, sharing my personal understanding of engineering-oriented linear algebra and my approach to learning it. Since it is aimed at engineering students, I will avoid setting the bar too high, focusing instead on what an engineering student should grasp.

## The Problem

Let's start by cutting to the chase and identifying the flaws in the domestic [Linear Algebra course](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=212935194&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0%E8%AF%BE%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity). These deep-seated issues have been festering for a long time and may have an impact on students in the future. Engineering linear algebra textbooks in China are basically centered around the Tongji University linear algebra textbook (the purple one), with various schools potentially creating their own adapted versions, and some schools even teaching linear algebra together with analytic geometry. The biggest feature of this book is that it is overly concise, almost like lecture notes or a formula handbook, severely lacking the introduction and connections between knowledge points that a proper textbook should provide. Moreover, the content has some unreasonable aspects.

This leaves students lacking motivation in their studies, unsure of what they are doing in linear algebra—just feeling that they are computing determinants and matrices. Countless strange definitions, concepts, properties, and operational rules make you wonder: why does solving a system of equations have to be this elaborate? Then you face all kinds of exam question types, each with its own solution methods, just to survive the final exams and then the graduate entrance exam. Cramming for exams is manageable; even if you don't understand, you can just use your brain a bit more and memorize it. But once linear algebra is applied in other disciplines—such as in calculus, professional engineering courses, or future research and work—all that rote memorization suddenly feels futile, requiring you to start over or fill in the gaps. Once linear algebra is muddled through, you end up on a path of increasing confusion.

The causes of these problems are multifaceted and complex. First, most textbooks are inevitably flawed, and teachers often simply read from the materials. Even teachers with genuine insights have to rush through the curriculum given the limited class hours, let alone delve deeply enough for students to truly understand. On a deeper level, the flaws in the textbooks have historical reasons.

Although I haven't thoroughly verified this myself, it goes without saying that domestic engineering linear algebra textbooks originated from Soviet models. The reason they are flawed isn't that Soviet textbooks were bad, but rather that Soviet-style textbooks were designed for a specific teaching approach, and times have changed over the decades. First, our linear algebra class hours have been continuously compressed, and the curriculum has been reformed and trimmed, leading to difficulties in comprehension. The teaching quality of domestic instructors varies widely, and in Soviet-style textbooks—structured as "definition, theorem, proof, corollary, proof, exercises"—the motivation for introducing concepts and deeper understanding were meant to be conveyed by the teacher, with the textbook serving more like lecture notes. Additionally, the development of the times has made the integration of computers with linear algebra more essential, but domestic textbooks haven't done this well. Some may include computer-related content, but it feels disjointed from the main text, like two pieces of rag poorly sewn together into a single piece of clothing.

Teaching linear algebra alongside analytic geometry isn't inherently wrong (although many linear algebra teachers see analytic geometry as serving calculus, and putting it into linear algebra is just for convenience, especially given linear algebra's limited class hours), but the integration in domestic textbooks generally suffers from the same sense of patchwork, without any feeling of true intellectual unity.

## Learning Methodology

Among the people I know, some schools actually don't use those "traditional" linear algebra textbooks. Instead, they use Strang's book (introduced later), which starts with vectors and transitions better from high school knowledge, making the subsequent content more intuitive and easier to understand.

For those of you struggling with your textbooks, you can selectively follow the approach I offer: (If some of the things I say are unclear, refer to the materials I provide, and I'm confident you'll soon understand what this article is about.)

1. Don't be intimidated by determinants, especially the definition using inversion numbers. The inversion number definition comes from the mathematical study of permutations; permutation groups are an important example in group theory, but for engineering purposes, it's quite redundant. The inversion number simply indicates that determinants are oriented—swapping two rows (or columns) changes the sign. So I prefer to understand the geometric meaning of the determinant—it's an oriented scaling factor (where "oriented" means it can be positive or negative; a negative value means the corresponding matrix transformation "flips" the space over). For those with greater ambitions, I'd encourage you to understand the determinant as an antisymmetric multilinear function.

2. Emphasize geometric intuition as an aid to understanding, which means building up the concept of vector spaces. You can simply think of a vector space as Euclidean space (our real life is three-dimensional Euclidean space) with the metric (length, angles, etc.) removed. You'll find that many things become obvious once you have geometric intuition—like properties of determinants, the associative law of matrices (AB)C = A(BC), the structure of [solutions to systems of linear equations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=212935194&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B%E7%BB%84%E8%A7%A3&zhida_source=entity), and so on.

Here, I'd like to recommend a great book: *The Geometric Meaning of Linear Algebra*. The motivation for writing this book is similar to why I'm writing this article, and it was also written by someone in engineering.

Recently, I also came across some materials used by a senior student at my school; the author is now a teacher at the university, and these materials are also worth consulting.

There's also the Bilibili channel 3Blue1Brown, which pairs well with general courses.

If you find what I'm saying hard to grasp, go check those out. I suggest reading them side by side, and I'm sure you'll have an "aha" moment soon.

## Understanding Engineering Linear Algebra (No need to grasp it immediately; the methodology is above)

This is just a bird's-eye overview; it certainly doesn't explain everything, but I hope it sparks some inspiration.

For engineering majors, the perspective on linear algebra is naturally quite different from that of mathematics majors. As the saying goes, in mathematics, linear algebra is the study of linear mappings between linear spaces and the linear transformations on them. For engineering, we mostly focus on low-dimensional spaces (four dimensions and below). Therefore, what we study is naturally linear mappings and linear transformations in low-dimensional linear spaces. These things, especially in three dimensions and below, are intrinsically linked to geometric intuition in low dimensions—that's why I said it makes sense to merge linear algebra with spatial analytic geometry. My teacher also said that teaching geometry in linear algebra serves multivariable calculus, which also makes sense, since multivariable calculus does need geometry.

Now let's talk about the close connections among the various knowledge points. A standard engineering linear algebra textbook covers six major sections: determinants, matrices, vector groups, systems of linear equations, eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and quadratic forms. Linear spaces, however, are only a supplementary topic in engineering textbooks, sitting on the periphery and usually not covered in class.

Similar to advanced algebra in math departments, from a historical perspective, linear algebra as a subject originated from the study of systems of linear equations. While studying the solutions to linear systems, people invented determinants. At the time, a determinant was just a notation, a way to represent a certain calculation. But as research deepened over time, concepts like matrices and rank emerged, and linear algebra gradually became an independent discipline. Many things—such as determinants and linear systems—gained new perspectives and deeper meanings.

What is a vector space? A space isn't actually "empty"—every space is filled with things. A vector space consists of infinitely many vectors, all starting from the origin O, pointing in various directions.

A matrix is a linear transformation on a vector space—like a "function" that takes one space as input and outputs another, essentially "moving" the vector space. However, this transformation must satisfy linearity, meaning homogeneity and the superposition principle.

Since every vector in a vector space can be represented in terms of a basis—in other words, the basis vectors span the space—transforming the vector space is equivalent to transforming the vectors in it, which is in turn equivalent to transforming the basis vectors of the space (which is why some people say linear algebra is a subject about "bases" (with a pun intended)). A matrix is precisely formed by arranging the transformed basis vectors column by column—that is, a matrix is formed by a vector group.

The determinant of a matrix (note I say "determinant of a matrix," because the determinant is a property of square matrices) is the scaling factor of the matrix transformation. Some of you may have heard the joke about a 3×4 determinant (laughs). But then, why do only square matrices have determinants? Actually, if we assume that the matrix transformation acts on the column space (the space spanned by the column vectors)—that is, viewing the matrix as composed of column vectors—a 3×4 matrix corresponds to taking four basis vectors (meaning they come from a four-dimensional space) and each basis vector now has only three components (meaning they go into three-dimensional space). Going from four dimensions to three is a dimensionality reduction—what would you say the scaling factor of this dimensionality-reducing transformation is? Naturally, it's 0, so discussing a determinant for such a matrix is meaningless. Similarly, a 4×3 matrix goes from three dimensions to four. You might think, going from three to four dimensions is like going from zero to one to two to three dimensions—points moving to lines, lines to surfaces, surfaces to volumes. But that's not how it works: dimensionality reduction loses information (vector components), while dimensionality increase cannot recover those missing components. So dimensionality increase can be simply understood as placing three-dimensional space into four-dimensional space in some way; studying its determinant is equally meaningless because you don't know how the three-dimensional space was embedded into four dimensions or what intermediate steps occurred. That determinant is essentially ill-defined. If the transformed basis vectors are linearly dependent, the determinant is 0; if they're linearly independent, it could be any value, depending on the situation of the transformed basis.

Thus, it's quite obvious that the rank of a matrix is the dimension of the transformed space, which is naturally a manifestation of the linear dependence of the vector group forming the matrix—the transformed basis vector group. We gradually move on to studying matrix equations as more abstract linear equations, where both the unknowns and constants become vectors in vector spaces.

For example, our object of study, systems of linear equations, becomes the matrix equation Ax = b or Ax = 0. It represents a matrix acting on a vector space, meaning it acts on vectors in the space, turning one vector into another. No solution means there is no vector x that, when acted on by A, becomes vector b or 0; a unique solution means there's only one such x; infinitely many solutions means there are infinitely many x's.

Here, you might wonder: why can't there be a finite number of solutions? Some might proudly say: because the textbook's case-by-case discussion of the ranks of the coefficient matrix A and the [augmented matrix](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=212935194&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A2%9E%E5%B9%BF%E7%9F%A9%E9%98%B5&zhida_source=entity) (A | b) has already covered all possibilities! But I'd say that still doesn't touch the essence. The essence lies in the fact that the solutions of a linear system still form a space. Note that only the solution space of a homogeneous system is a vector space, called the null space, because it consists of vectors that become 0 after transformation, and it contains the origin O. The solution space of a non-homogeneous system is an affine space over a vector space. These spaces also have their own bases—this is the concept of the fundamental system of solutions. So it's quite obvious: a homogeneous system has a unique solution, which is the zero solution (though this is also obvious algebraically), and if it isn't unique, there must be infinitely many solutions, including the zero solution. As for non-homogeneous systems, depending on the transformation A, there may be no solution, a unique solution, or infinitely many solutions. These are all geometrically obvious. The structure of these spaces essentially reveals the structure of the solutions to linear systems.

In the study of matrices, we find that within the "change" of matrix transformations, there lies something "unchanged." This kind of invariance within change is usually a key focus of mathematical research (for example, differential form invariance, topological invariants, homomorphisms, isomorphisms, etc.). Hence, we have the following concepts:

1. The equivalent canonical form of a matrix: different matrices can share the same rank. Different transformations may yield the same dimension of the image space, which also means they can be transformed into one another through finitely many elementary operations. This is like grouping matrices of the same shape with the same rank into one class.

2. The concept of eigenvalues and eigenvectors: eigenvectors of a matrix undergo only stretching or compression but no rotation; eigenvalues are the corresponding stretching/compression factors for those eigenvectors. Naturally, matrices with the same eigenvalues can be grouped into a class—this is the similarity canonical form. Also, similarity can simplify our computations, because diagonal matrices are very easy to compute, and similarity lets us focus only on the diagonal matrix similar to the matrix we're dealing with.

3. In particular, when studying quadratic forms, we encounter the concept of orthogonal similarity—that is, similarity to an [orthogonal matrix](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=212935194&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AD%A3%E4%BA%A4%E7%9F%A9%E9%98%B5&zhida_source=entity). The reason this deserves special mention is that orthogonal matrices have excellent properties—specifically, angle preservation (called preserving the metric of the space in mathematics), which can be simply understood as no stretching or compression, only translations, rotations, reflections, etc. This property is quite important when studying quadratic forms, because some quadratic forms are "crooked," and we can use orthogonalization to "straighten" them.

(Also, as a side note, these equivalent canonical forms and similarity canonical forms both provide a classification of matrices; in mathematics, this is called an equivalence class under a certain equivalence relation.)

Quadratic forms are the final highlight of general linear algebra textbooks. The reason we study them is their importance in various applications. As an automation major, I know at least that quadratic forms are applied in engineering, control theory, convex optimization, probability and statistics—the uses are truly numerous. Furthermore, from the main thread of linear algebra, quadratic forms also provide an example of pure geometry within a vector space. They can be said to originate from quadratic functions. Although we always rely on our real-life three-dimensional Euclidean space for intuition, in reality we use linear algebra methods—that is, matrices—to study them, and this part is also highly geometric.

Careful readers may have noticed that I've been talking about vector spaces when I said linear algebra studies linear spaces. First, let me say up front: the two concepts can be treated interchangeably, but some professors prefer to point out that a linear space is a further abstraction of a vector space. We're used to calling the elements of a vector space "vectors," but after further abstraction, the elements of a linear space are not necessarily traditional vectors. For instance, a [Hilbert space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=212935194&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B8%8C%E5%B0%94%E4%BC%AF%E7%89%B9%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) is a function space, where functions serve as elements—these are "vectors" or points, one by one (since the starting point is always O, the endpoint can represent the vector). In calculus, careful students will also notice that differentiation and integration are linear operations and satisfy the definition of a linear space, so these functions can form a linear space.

From this, we can see that linear algebra studies linear problems. As long as something satisfies linearity, it inherently contains the structure of linear algebra. Knowing this, it's no surprise that linear algebra is everywhere, because mathematics is essentially linear or nonlinear, and one approach to nonlinear problems is to linearize them. Linear things are just too common.

From the overview above, you might have noticed that I keep talking about vector spaces, and the method of study is to represent transformations using matrices. A matrix is truly a fantastic tool for studying linear transformations.

As mentioned earlier, a matrix is like a "function," so it's not limited to just the four basic arithmetic operations—it also has calculus. We can take derivatives of matrices and integrate matrices. The determinant at the beginning of typical textbooks is just a property of square matrices; from a macro perspective, it's not the main focus. The calculus of matrices is also a part well worth knowing.

Going a step further, as disciplines, linear algebra and matrix theory study different things.

Linear algebra studies linear spaces and linear transformations, centered around vector spaces as its main object. Matrices serve as a concrete representation of linear transformations, but the subject doesn't go deep into matrix theory; indeed, general linear algebra courses don't really incorporate much of matrix theory.

Matrix theory centers on matrices themselves. Just as mathematical analysis studies calculus, matrix theory involves matrix analysis—the calculus of matrices as transformations. In fact, it has many similarities to multivariable calculus. Additionally, it covers matrix decompositions, [matrix norms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=212935194&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9F%A9%E9%98%B5%E8%8C%83%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity), complex matrices, and things like the Jordan canonical form, which aren't covered much in linear algebra. In a math department, these topics are scattered across courses like advanced algebra, ordinary differential equations, and functional analysis. For engineering, all that matrix analysis is covered at the graduate level. For engineering students, both linear algebra and matrix theory are extremely important foundational math courses.
