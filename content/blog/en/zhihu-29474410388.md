After studying the Kalman filter and, more generally, optimal estimation and optimal control theory, from the perspectives of both learning optimal control and probability and statistics, and then delving into information divergence and variational inference, I've found that there are two implicit logics for handling sequential problems. Regardless, the shared core tightly revolves around **sparsity**. So, it's entirely feasible to combine the two: leveraging techniques from statistical inference to better adapt state-space models to more general scenarios—whether Markov or non-Markov, deterministic or probabilistic—by simply holding fast to the core of sparsity. This opens up a vast and promising landscape.

Moreover, in optimizing sequential problems, one almost always faces two difficulties: the complex optimization of cost functions and the challenge of aligning cost function construction with optimization outcomes. The latter is a problem common to all optimization—namely, the task-oriented construction of energy functions/Lyapunov functions—which I've already discussed in my previous article [1](#ref_1), but there's no truly good solution. The former, however, stands to benefit greatly from the tailwinds of **zeroth-order optimization**.

In short, although the author hasn't yet delved deeply into this area, still racing against time and fighting hard, based on my mathematical intuition, behind this lies a beautiful expanse.

## I. Planning Control and Optimization

### (1) Optimal Control

The logic from the control and optimization side is: if you formulate an optimal control problem as a nonlinear optimization problem and then apply sequential second-order approximations, you're essentially taking Newton steps on the KKT system. This confronts you with the Hessian inversion problem, driving the complexity up to O((n+m)³H³). Of course, from an optimization standpoint, we needn't directly use Newton steps; there are many ways to reduce the cost of inversion and accelerate convergence.

However, in optimal control, the most fundamental logic is the recursive optimal substructure—the principle of dynamic programming arising from the Markov property (lack of aftereffects). The sequential structure imparts sparsity, which in turn yields the Riccati iteration, reducing complexity to O((n+m)³H). This result dates back to 1998. Yet, it still falls short in the face of the curse of dimensionality; the issues posed by dimensionality have led many to turn to ADP (better known as reinforcement learning, RL), using function approximation to handle high-dimensional, unknown cost-to-go or value functions, or action-value functions.

### (2) Motion Planning and MPC

Many practitioners in planning don't actually realize that planning and optimal control are essentially no different. In fact, motion planning also optimizes some cost based on a sequential problem—a perspective I've elaborated in my earlier article [2](#ref_2). The difference lies in the far more complex constraints in planning; if discrete constraints are crudely incorporated, the problem can even become a mixed-integer or combinatorial optimization.

For problems of manageable dimensionality, MPC provides an elegant theoretical framework for solving "optimal control problems in more general settings," or "motion planning problems." That is, under complex non-convex constraints, nonlinear dynamics, and complex cost functionals, we opt for a sliding window (receding horizon) coupled with some approximation techniques. This effectively simplifies the mixed-integer optimization into a more tractable problem, though a crude formulation often yields a non-convex or even non-smooth optimization. Thus, exploiting problem structure for convexification becomes an art—for instance, in optimization, we discuss convex relaxation (a rare gem, such as the 2012 paper behind SpaceX's rocket recovery technology), Lagrange relaxation, sequential convex programming, and Majorization-Minimization. For various engineering treatments, see [3](#ref_3).

So, claims that MPC has high algorithmic complexity or is slow are often irresponsible. Without employing more sophisticated techniques to dissect problem structure and resorting only to the crudest formulations, clumsiness is inevitable. One could even argue that the crux of nonlinear programming and general planning lies in identifying problem structure, rather than seeking universal information like gradients to solve everything.

### (3) Robustness

I still know too little about robust control to say much.

When faced with uncertainty in a system, if the disturbances are bounded (whether random or not), in simple cases, one can often rely on the inherent robustness of a designed controller to cover it (ensuring stability, i.e., bounded disturbances yield input-to-state stability). If one insists on some probabilistic notion of optimality, then the statistical properties of disturbances or noise must be incorporated into the design. However, for nonlinear systems, achieving semi-global stability is often already a good outcome; demanding probabilistic optimality via statistical properties is too much to ask when nonlinearities are riddled with approximations.

Beyond treating perturbed systems in an expectation-minimization framework, minE[·], there's also the minimax {·} game-theoretic viewpoint, which yields a two-player zero-sum equilibrium between disturbance and control input. Stochastic optimal control, on the other hand, treats various system quantities as stochastic processes, giving rise to stochastic dynamic programming and tools from stochastic analysis. I have limited familiarity with stochastic control, knowing only the basic Itô lemma and a few applications—essentially that the first-order expansion accuracy in stochastic calculus is insufficient.

## 2. Statistical Inference

### (1) Approximate Inference and Robustness

For a Bayesian inference problem, the posterior distribution $p(x|z)=\frac{p(z|x)p(x)}{\int p(z|x)p(x)dx}$ typically encounters three issues:

1. Mismatch due to inaccurate priors; 2. Mismatch in the likelihood $p(z|x)$; 3. The integral in the denominator is computationally intractable, due to high dimensionality and the absence of closed-form solutions for complex distributions.

Among these, prior mismatch is quite natural—we habitually resort to conjugate priors to simplify computation. As for the likelihood, which serves as a "sensor model," mismatch largely stems from substituting the full likelihood with partial samples (finite time). Lastly, any Bayesian method inevitably faces the intractable integral problem, at which point statisticians turn to approximate inference. Flexible optimization and sampling theory give rise to two strongholds—VI and MCMC—corresponding to deterministic approximation and stochastic approximation, respectively.

For high-dimensional problems, pure sampling methods fall short, and thus the well-scaling optimization method—variational inference—has become the method of choice. The problem of variational inference similarly centers on optimization over the space of probability distributions. From a high-level perspective, this is an information-geometric viewpoint: we are optimizing on an infinite-dimensional manifold. From a more general viewpoint, it is the minimization of a probability metric (or divergence). In general, the cost functional takes the ELBO form

$$
J=\int q\left( z|x \right) \left[ aD\left( q\left( z|x \right),p\left( z \right) \right)+b\sum_{i}{l_{i}\left( x,\theta \right)} \right]dx
$$

where $q\left( z|x \right)$ is the chosen variational distribution; $D\left( \cdot,\cdot \right)$ is a probability divergence or metric, which must at least be positive definite, with a metric additionally required to be symmetric and satisfy the triangle inequality; and $L=\sum_{i}{l_{i}\left( x,\theta \right)}$ is a function serving the same role as the likelihood, where the summation representation reflects a decoupling—the total likelihood can be decomposed into a sum of per-component likelihoods.

From the form of the cost, the problems we face include the selection of the variational family, the choice of divergence and likelihood, and how to perform optimization. The divergence term addresses the prior mismatch problem; robustness to prior mismatch superficially calls for robust divergences or metrics. The likelihood term corresponds to likelihood mismatch. Given the usual mechanisms behind likelihood mismatch, several strategies are available—for instance, using variational inference to construct the relationship between $p\left( z_{1:k}|x \right)$ and the true likelihood $p\left(z|x \right)$. In many cases, the negative log form serves as the best likelihood.

### (2) Factor Graph Inference

For sequential problems, statistics formulates them as Bayesian probabilistic graphical models. Sequential problems typically become sequential decision-making or statistical inference problems. A Bayesian inference problem can be represented as a factor graph, and performing inference is vividly described as "message passing" on the factor graph. For an ordinary single system with Markovian properties, the factor graph takes a chain-like structure.

In my view, the factor graph model is arguably the third great representation in control theory, following the transfer function of classical control and the state space of modern control. From a discretization perspective, it expresses both Markov and non-Markov systems in a unified, structured manner (compared to differential equations, or the representation within state-space equations).

Compared to the Riccati iterations mentioned earlier, or the direct use of various optimization theories, factor graphs—or more generally graphical state models—are more general when handling sequential problems. They naturally account for temporal structure without making the Markov assumption.

## 3. A Unified Perspective—Global Optimization & Sparse Sequential Processing

With the above foundations in place (despite the lack of technical details), I believe any mathematically inclined researcher in related fields would be quite excited. Our mathematical intuition points to a highly general—and by no means infeasible—framework. Unlike previous encounters where we were ultimately constrained by fundamental difficulties such as nonlinearity or combinatorial explosion, this time we are better equipped.

### (1) Optimal Estimation: "A Bridge Spanning North and South"

Optimal estimation is arguably a remarkable field. From a control perspective, it involves designing observers using optimal control. From a statistical perspective, the general Bayesian filter is a hidden Markov model. But why is it precisely the observer—rather than control itself—that bridges the two disciplines? I suspect it is because the tasks coincide: statistics inherently seeks to extract regularities from samples, which aligns naturally with the observer's goal of estimating states from measurements.

![](https://pica.zhimg.com/v2-d72e5fc2d723b65d8c94ad67647118ae_r.jpg)

The connection is this: the construction of the cost functional in optimal control translates, under the statistical viewpoint, into some criterion (MMSE, MAP, MLE, etc.) combined with assumptions on probability distributions. This readily explains why the standard Kalman filter pairs the LQR of a linear system (via duality) on one side with recursive least-squares variance estimation (essentially MMSE) plus a Gaussian assumption on the other: both are quadratic optimizations under a linear sequential structure. For more general nonlinear systems and complex functionals, what corresponds is the minimization (or explicit computation) of some statistic—or the entire distribution—of a complex multimodal distribution.

### (2) Optimal Sequential Decision-Making and Inference

It becomes clear that the two perspectives above are essentially no different at their core—both reduce to finding the optimum over a time series.

The key problem is that, to achieve good scalability for high-dimensional problems while avoiding the constraints of conjugate distribution assumptions, we need black-box optimization for inference. On black-box optimization, also known as zeroth-order optimization, see my article:

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1903595957171513009?share_code=RVaDDjUIOmkP&utm_psn=1906824119783236396](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1903595957171513009?share_code=RVaDDjUIOmkP&utm_psn=1906824119783236396)

Once the problem is formulated as statistical inference, probabilistic graphical models can represent motion planning problems, and the inference problem becomes "message passing" on a factor graph. Due to NP-hard complexity, message-passing algorithms on general (loopy) factor graphs require approximation, and variational inference provides a powerful approximation framework. Within the variational inference framework, message passing includes Belief Propagation, Variational Message Passing, and other approaches.

The advantages of using factor graphs as a representation for distributional inference are: first, factor graphs are not constrained by the Markov property and can handle game-theoretic interaction problems more naturally [\[4\]](#ref_4)—though this is not the only formulation for such interactions. Second, for complex systems, factor graphs hold strong potential for efficient computation, with the core lying in exploiting sparsity.

## 4. Motion Planning

### 4.1 The Challenge of Planning

For the difficulties faced by the current classical motion planning framework, refer to Section 3.5 "Remaining Challenges" in [\[5\]](#ref_5).

**1. Non-convexity and non-smoothness arising from the complex nature of motion planning**

In planning, casting problems into convex forms actually limits our ability to solve the problems we care about. This is reflected in how we construct cost functions and constraints. Without the shackles of convexification, we could more naturally adopt task-oriented approaches, design more natural cost functions, and handle more complex constraints.

**2. Uncertainty from multimodality and interactive games**

When other dynamic obstacles or [agents](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=254917378&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%99%BA%E8%83%BD%E4%BD%93&zhida_source=entity) are present, both dynamic [environment perception](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=254917378&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8E%AF%E5%A2%83%E6%84%9F%E7%9F%A5&zhida_source=entity) and the behavior of other agents introduce uncertainty. First and foremost is the uncertainty in perception and prediction, which gives rise to the multimodality problem. Motion planning should handle multimodal problems more naturally rather than simply selecting among trajectories, which requires planning to work with probability distributions. A more fundamental approach is to tightly couple the [prediction and decision-making](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=254917378&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%A2%84%E6%B5%8B%E4%B8%8E%E5%86%B3%E7%AD%96&zhida_source=entity) modules with the planning component. Interaction and human-like costs may not even be [additive](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=254917378&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%AF%E5%8A%A0%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity), and their forms can be complex.

[How can one become an excellent decision and planning engineer for autonomous driving? Please share your insights, experts?](https://www.zhihu.com/question/458735768/answer/3625130888?utm_psn=1898315702668067414)

**3. Dynamical complexity**

Linearizing dynamic constraints is also an approximation. Moreover, for underactuated and nonholonomic dynamics, simple polynomial trajectory optimization is insufficient—this point needs little elaboration.

## 5. A True "End-to-End" Approach?

Starting from the above framework, one can center on motion planning while integrating prediction, decision-making, and the SLAM backend. This could even potentially achieve a white-box "end-to-end" system, which is fundamentally different from the current black-box "end-to-end" approaches. Principle-based algorithms built on axiomatic deduction offer stronger interpretability and [generalization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=254917378&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B3%9B%E5%8C%96%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity). Furthermore, through state graphs, one can extend to [swarms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=254917378&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9B%86%E7%BE%A4&zhida_source=entity), whether for swarm coordination or game-theoretic settings. Even for dynamically simple systems such as [quadrotors](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=254917378&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9B%9B%E6%97%8B%E7%BF%BC%E6%97%A0%E4%BA%BA%E6%9C%BA&zhida_source=entity), this can be viewed as a special case of global optimization MPC.

When integrating with modules such as prediction and decision-making, neural networks are not placed in a central role (of course, neural networks and other learning-based methods can still serve as auxiliary components within this framework), eliminating the need for neural network design and large model training.

### 5.1 Integration with Decision-Making [\[11\]](#ref_11)

The current fundamental tools for handling stochasticity in scenario evolution for decision-making and planning are MDP, POMDP, and scenario-based MPC. Black-box MPC can also perform scenario evolution inference given trajectory information, thereby fusing decision-making with planning. Considering planning information during decision-making facilitates natural handling of game-theoretic interactions with other agents. Moreover, Markov assumptions are not required.

### 5.2 Integration with Prediction

When predicting the trajectories of other agents, there is no need to rely solely on a fully black-box NN. One can instead incorporate their dynamics and use planning-based methods to generate more reasonable predictions—an approach known in the prediction literature as physics-based prediction. Diffusion models are well suited for multimodal generation, and preference can be incorporated through various techniques. Additionally, based on the relationship between surrounding agents and our own agent (adversarial vs. cooperative), one can compute objectives and generate trajectory predictions for other agents via optimization. Combining both approaches should yield better results in terms of information utilization than pure NN prediction. Meanwhile, data can also be used to optimize parameters in planning or control; existing directions such as [IRL](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=254917378&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=IRL&zhida_source=entity) and iterative learning control already address similar problems.

### 5.3 Coupling with SLAM

The SLAM backend—whether based on (nonlinear) filtering or graph optimization—is essentially solved via factor graph optimization. If the SLAM backend factor graph and the motion planning factor graph could be coupled and jointly handled with unified graph-theoretic techniques, better results should follow. This is especially relevant for active SLAM: in the existing active SLAM literature, likely because the researchers come from the SLAM community, the planning algorithms are usually quite trivial. Moreover, planning and SLAM are almost completely decoupled in many cases, and the task is not framed as planning being subservient to SLAM, so this standard paradigm does not apply. The graph-coupling approach strikes a better balance between the two.

### 5.4 Control, Robustness, and Safety

A critical question for motion planning is: under model mismatch, prediction inaccuracy, and environmental uncertainty, how can we guarantee the robustness and safety of predictive control used as the planning/control law? [\[12\]](#ref_12) At the control level, this comes for free—using MPC for motion planning naturally provides a trajectory-tracking controller. However, the real question is: under model mismatch, environmental disturbances, and inaccurate predictions, how can we ensure that such probabilistic inference remains robust? Classical robust MPC is built on the theory of [invariant sets](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=254917378&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%8D%E5%8F%98%E9%9B%86&zhida_source=entity) and admissible sets, whereas current probabilistic frameworks can only employ heuristic measures to cope with unknown parameters or distribution mismatch without quantitative analysis. How, then, should the robustness of such distributional inference be defined and treated at a theoretical level? This is a question worth pondering.
