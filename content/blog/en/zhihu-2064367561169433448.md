## I. A Brief Introduction to Classical Symplectic Geometry

Assume the reader has some familiarity with [Riemannian geometry](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Riemann%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity). Symplectic geometry is then the *geometry of conservation* and the *geometry of softness*.

### 1. Ruler vs. Area

The core of Riemannian geometry is a ruler: a symmetric, positive-definite metric tensor $g$. It tells you the length and angle of every vector in the [tangent space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%87%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity), from which arc length, geodesics, and curvature all follow.

The core of symplectic geometry is an area net: a **nondegenerate, skew-symmetric closed 2-form** $\omega$. On a two-dimensional surface it measures area; on the [cotangent bundle](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%99%E5%88%87%E4%B8%9B&zhida_source=entity) it takes the form $\sum dp_i \wedge dq_i$. It is precisely this skew-symmetry that forces [symplectic manifolds](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BE%9B%E6%B5%81%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity) to be even-dimensional—a nondegenerate skew-symmetric bilinear form exists only on even-dimensional spaces.

### 2. No Local Geometry: [Darboux's Theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Darboux%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity)

Riemannian geometry is locally rich: the [curvature tensor](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9B%B2%E7%8E%87%E5%BC%A0%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity) exists everywhere, telling you how space bends, and it cannot be eliminated by [coordinate changes](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9D%90%E6%A0%87%E5%8F%98%E6%8D%A2&zhida_source=entity) unless the space is flat to begin with. Riemannian geometry is thus locally rigid: the [isometry group](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%AD%89%E8%B7%9D%E5%8F%98%E6%8D%A2%E7%BE%A4&zhida_source=entity) is a finite-dimensional Lie group.

Symplectic geometry is the opposite: **Darboux's theorem** asserts that every symplectic manifold is locally equivalent to the standard form $\sum dp_i \wedge dq_i$. This means that locally all symplectic manifolds are identical—there are no local invariants like curvature. This is the fundamental source of what you called "softness"—locally there is no geometry at all. From this arises an enormous symmetry: every [smooth function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%89%E6%BB%91%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) $H$ generates a [Hamiltonian vector field](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Hamilton%E5%90%91%E9%87%8F%E5%9C%BA&zhida_source=entity) that preserves $\omega$. The symplectomorphism group is infinite-dimensional; one can arbitrarily knead space without changing the symplectic structure—a flexibility utterly unimaginable in Riemannian geometry.

### 3. Rigidity of Global Topology

If it were merely soft locally, symplectic geometry would be too simple. Gromov and others discovered that once one looks globally, symplectic geometry exhibits striking rigidity. The most famous example is the non-squeezing theorem: one cannot symplectically embed a symplectic ball into a symplectic cylinder of smaller radius, even though volume-wise the ball fits inside. This reveals the existence of global symplectic capacity invariants.

"Softness" should be understood as: local flexibility + topological rigidity. Riemannian geometry, by contrast, has local rigidity (curvature) driving global geometry. The two form a remarkable inversion.

### 4. [Configuration Space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%8D%E5%BD%A2%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) vs. [Phase Space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%B8%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity)

Riemannian geometry is naturally the geometry of configuration space: the spacetime curvature of [general relativity](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B9%BF%E4%B9%89%E7%9B%B8%E5%AF%B9%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity), or the position space of a constrained system, where geodesics are the trajectories of inertial motion. Lagrangian mechanics is indeed described in this geometric language.

Symplectic geometry is the geometry of phase space: it incorporates not just position but also momentum, forming the even-dimensional cotangent bundle. [Hamiltonian mechanics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Hamilton%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) manifests here as a flow—[Hamilton's equations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Hamilton%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) are precisely the "gradient flow" of the symplectic form. "Conservation" originates here: $\omega$ is preserved by the flow ( $L_{X_H}\omega = 0$ ), and consequently the volume form $\omega^n/n!$ is conserved—this is [Liouville's theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Liouville%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity); energy $H$ is also conserved along the flow. All of this is [automatically encoded](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%87%AA%E5%8A%A8%E7%BC%96%E7%A0%81&zhida_source=entity) in the closedness of $\omega$, i.e., $d\omega = 0$.

The two meet in the duality of the tangent and cotangent bundles: given any manifold with a [Riemannian metric](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Riemann%E5%BA%A6%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity) $g$, the cotangent bundle carries both the canonical symplectic form and a rewritten kinetic energy as a [Hamiltonian](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Hamilton%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity), so the geodesic flow becomes a [Hamiltonian flow](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Hamilton%E6%B5%81&zhida_source=entity).

## II. Almost Complex Structures and [Kähler Geometry](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=K%C3%A4hler%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity): The Origin of Modern Symplectic Geometry

Symplectic geometry $(M,\omega)$ is "soft locally"; Riemannian geometry $(M,g)$ is "rigid locally." When the two coexist compatibly, they give rise to a complex structure.

Definition (Almost Complex Structure) If there exists a [tensor field](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BC%A0%E9%87%8F%E5%9C%BA&zhida_source=entity) $J \in \Gamma(T^*M \otimes TM)$ satisfying $J^2 = -\mathrm{Id}$, compatible with both $\omega$ and $g$: $g(X,Y) = \omega(X, JY) \quad \forall X,Y$ then $(M,\omega,g,J)$ is said to be an almost Hermitian structure. In this case $\omega$ is $J$-invariant $（\omega(JX,JY)=\omega(X,Y)）$, and any two of $(g,\omega,J)$ determine the third.

Kähler condition: if $d\omega = 0$ (the symplectic condition) and $J$ is integrable (the Nijenhuis tensor vanishes), then the manifold is a Kähler manifold. Equivalently, there exist local [holomorphic coordinates](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%A8%E7%BA%AF%E5%9D%90%E6%A0%87&zhida_source=entity) $z^i$ such that:

$$
\omega = \frac{i}{2} \sum_{i,j} g_{i\bar{j}} dz^i \wedge d\bar{z}^j, \quad g = \sum_{i,j} g_{i\bar{j}} dz^i d\bar{z}^j
$$

and $g_{i\bar{j}} = \partial_{z^i}\partial_{\bar{z}^j} K$, where $K$ is the Kähler potential. Then $\omega$ is a closed (1,1)-form.

I shall not presume to elaborate further on the deeper theory.

## III. Basic Formulation of Geometric Control

### **1. Holonomic and Nonholonomic Dynamics**

Suppose the system is subject to a Pfaffian constraint $\omega(q) \cdot \dot{q} = 0$ (equivalent to the [system dynamics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity)), where $\omega$ is a 1-form. Frobenius' theorem tells us that the distribution $\Delta = \ker \omega$ being involutive implies integrability, which means there exists a function $h(q)$ such that $\omega = dh$, or an [integrating factor](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86%E5%9B%A0%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity) $\mu(q)$ with $\mu\omega = dh$. The constraint is then equivalent to $h(q)$ being constant.

- The constraint restricts positions, not velocities.
- The configuration space of the system reduces to a lower-dimensional submanifold $\{q \in M\mid h(q) = c\}$, on which the unconstrained dynamics can be rewritten.
- Such constraints do not fundamentally alter the geometric structure of the system—they merely change the domain of the problem.

If the system is subject to a non-integrable velocity constraint $\omega(q,\dot{q})=0$, where $\omega$ is not the differential of any function, then the state space is not the entire tangent bundle. One can still write the dynamics in [affine form](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%BF%E5%B0%84%E5%BD%A2%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity) on this submanifold, but the drift and control vector fields are restricted to the constrained tangent bundle. This is called a [nonholonomic constraint](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E5%AE%8C%E6%95%B4%E7%BA%A6%E6%9D%9F&zhida_source=entity) or nonholonomic dynamics.

- The configuration space does not reduce in dimension. One can still reach any point on $Q$.
- But the paths one may take are restricted—some directions cannot be traversed directly and must be circumnavigated. This gives rise to all the richness of nonholonomic geometry: Lie bracket reachability, sub-Riemannian metrics.

A general class of controlled dynamics is the affine control system, typically nonholonomic:

$$
\dot{x} = f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m g_i(x) u_i
$$

- The state $x$ may include configuration $q$, velocity $\dot{q}$, and even disturbances. All quantities of interest in the evolution are contained therein.
- The control $u$ can arise from any physical quantity; it acts on the state manifold through the control vector fields $g_i(x)$.

Define the control distribution $\Delta = \operatorname{span}\{g_i\} \subseteq TM$. The directions in which control directly affects the state evolution lie only within this distribution. However, the drift term $f$ can generate additional directions of motion. By alternately employing control vector fields and drift, the overall reachable set is generated by iterated Lie brackets $[f, g_i], [g_i, g_j], [f, [f, g_i]], \dots$. This is precisely where [Chow's theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Chow+%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity) and [Frobenius' theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Frobenius%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity) come into play.

### **2\. Fully Actuated and Underactuated Dynamics**

For a mechanical system described by the original Lagrange dynamics:

$$
M(q) \ddot{q} + C(q,\dot{q}) \dot{q} + G(q) = B(q) u.
$$

Converting to a first-order affine form: define the state $x = (q, \dot{q}) \in TM$, with a state-space dimension of $2n$. The dynamics can then be written equivalently as:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} \begin{pmatrix} q \\ \dot{q} \end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \dot{q} \\ -M(q)^{-1}[C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q} + G(q)] \end{pmatrix}}_{f(q,\dot{q})} + \sum_{i=1}^m \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ M(q)^{-1} B_i(q) \end{pmatrix}}_{g_i(q)} u_i.
$$

-   Here $B_i(q)$ is the $i$-th column of $B(q)$.
-   The drift vector field $f$ captures the free dynamics (motion without control, such as a pendulum under gravity);
-   The control vector fields $g_i$ span a distribution lying in the "vertical" subspace of the tangent bundle (since their first component is identically zero), with dimension equal to $\operatorname{rank} B(q)$.

In this setting:

-   **Fully actuated:** $\operatorname{rank} B(q) = n$, so $\dim \Delta = n$. Combined with the Lie brackets $[g_i, f]$ producing changes in velocity direction, the system is fully reachable on $TM$.
-   **Underactuated:** $\operatorname{rank} B(q) < n$, so $\dim \Delta < n$. However, through the drift $[g_i, f]$, the system may or may not be reachable.

From a geometric viewpoint, the physical identity of $u$ is not what matters; what matters is that it spans an admissible direction subspace (a distribution) on the state manifold and how we assign a cost to it. The control $u$ can be hidden inside $g(x)$. For instance, for the dynamical system $\dot{q} = v, \quad \dot{v} = M(q)^{-1}(u - C(q,v)v - G(q))$, one can still define a metric on the distribution through the relationship between $u$ and $(\dot{q},\dot{v})$. Although this now involves state-dependent velocities, the geometric classification remains unchanged. Thus, regarding $u$ as $\dot{q}$ entails no loss of geometric generality, because as long as the distribution is given, we can always transform the system into an equivalent velocity [control form](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E5%BD%A2%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity) via coordinate changes or state extension. This is the idea of "[feedback linearization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%8D%E9%A6%88%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity)" in geometric [control theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity). From a practical engineering standpoint, however, this is not done due to considerations of stability margins and [robustness](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%B2%81%E6%A3%92%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity).

## III. The Language of Optimal Control

Optimal control starts from [sub-Riemannian geometry](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=sub-Riemannian%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity), can be further generalized to sub-Finsler geometry, and ultimately reaches the realm of [metric geometry](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BA%A6%E9%87%8F%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity), all stemming from increasingly complex cost structures. But we will eventually hit a wall somewhere: complex costs strip away geometric structure. At that point, we should recall the symplectic geometry that has been lurking in the background all along.

For more detailed exposition, see this article, which delves directly into [Floer homology theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Floer%E5%90%8C%E8%B0%83%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity) in symplectic geometry, essentially studying Lagrangian submanifolds (also briefly touched upon later in this article).

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/141844086?share_code=nBaFrzGx3fqV&utm_psn=2064631772386682157](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/141844086?share_code=nBaFrzGx3fqV&utm_psn=2064631772386682157)

### 1\. Starting from Sub-Riemannian Geometry

A typical problem is: on the state space of a system (such as a wheeled robot, with position, orientation, etc.), given nonholonomic dynamical constraints (wheels cannot slip sideways; velocity directions are restricted) and a control cost, find the optimal trajectory.

**(1) The $L^2$ Norm**

When the cost is a [quadratic form](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BA%8C%E6%AC%A1%E5%9E%8B&zhida_source=entity), this optimal trajectory is identified with a geodesic on a certain sub-Riemannian manifold.

Given the system (already raised to first order on the tangent bundle): $\dot{x} = f(x) + G(x) u, \quad x = (q, \dot{q}) \in TM,$ where 

$$
f(x) = \begin{pmatrix} \dot{q} \\ -M^{-1}(C\dot{q}+G) \end{pmatrix}, \quad G(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ M^{-1}B \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Cost: $J = \int \frac{1}{2} u^{\mathsf{T}} R u \, dt, \quad R \succ 0.$ Control distribution: $\Delta_x = \operatorname{im} G(x) \subseteq T_x(TM),$ a vertical subspace corresponding to acceleration directions.

Pushed-forward metric: for $v \in \Delta_x$, define $\|v\|_x^2 := \min_{u: G(x)u = v} u^{\mathsf{T}} R u.$ This is a positive definite quadratic form, whose solution is $u = R^{-1} G^{\mathsf{T}} (G R^{-1} G^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger} v$, hence $\|v\|_x^2 = v^{\mathsf{T}} (G R^{-1} G^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger} v.$

Here 

$$
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
$$

 denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. We thus obtain a Riemannian metric defined on the distribution:

$$
\langle v, v \rangle_x^{\Delta} = v^{\mathsf{T}} (G R^{-1} G^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger} v
$$

In this way, the original optimal control problem becomes equivalent to finding geodesics perturbed by the drift field $f$ on the sub-Riemannian manifold $(TM, \Delta, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^\Delta)$.

-   The nonholonomic constraints define a distribution $\Delta$ on the state manifold $M$ (the admissible subspace of velocity directions).
-   The [cost function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E4%BB%B7%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) $∫u^2 dt$ is equivalent to defining an inner product (metric $g$) on $\Delta$, endowing it with a notion of "length." On the control input space $\mathbb{R}^m$, there is a natural inner product. Through the map $G(x) : \mathbb{R}^m \to T_x M$, this inner product is pushed forward to an inner product $(GG^T)^{\dagger}$ on the tangent subspace $\Delta_x$ (deriving this requires a case-by-case discussion of whether $G$ is bijective). This is the sub-Riemannian metric.
-   Consequently, the problem is strictly transformed into finding the shortest path—a geodesic—connecting two points in the space $(M, \Delta, g)$. It satisfies $∇_{\dot q}\dot q ⊥ Δ$ (under $g$), whose local coordinate form is $\ddot q_i + Γ^{jk}_i \dot q_j \dot q_k = λ^A ξ_A^i$, where $ξ_A$ are the annihilating vector fields of the distribution $\Delta$ and $λ_A$ are multipliers.

**(2) General Norms**

If the cost is no longer the control energy $\int \|u\|^2 dt$ but rather a more general $L^p$ norm or any positive definite [homogeneous](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%BD%90%E6%AC%A1&zhida_source=entity) Minkowski norm (possibly asymmetric and non-smooth), then what you define on the state space is no longer an inner product but a pointwise family of norms $\mathcal{F}_q$ (i.e., a Finsler structure). The Riemannian metric is the quadratic, elliptic case; the Finsler metric is the general first-order homogeneous case. From a [dynamical systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) perspective, homogeneity implies time-scaling invariance, with trajectories determined solely by the direction field. When this norm is defined only on a nonholonomic distribution $\Delta$, it becomes sub-Finsler geometry. Pulling back as above yields the sub-Finsler metric $\|v\|_x = \min_{Gu=v} \|u\|_p$.

-   The "length" of a curve is defined by $\int \mathcal{F}_q(u) dt$, which need not be quadratic. The unit ball can be a [convex polygon](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%87%B8%E5%A4%9A%E8%BE%B9%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity), allowing geodesics to have polygonal segments and cusps; however, the cost is a convex (so that the metric defined via optimization is well-posed) norm (satisfying first-order homogeneity).
-   Shortest paths still exist and satisfy the generalized Pontryagin [maximum principle](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9E%81%E5%A4%A7%E5%80%BC%E5%8E%9F%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity), except that the Hamiltonian now changes from a quadratic form to the extremal condition involving the [dual norm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AF%B9%E5%81%B6%E8%8C%83%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) (support function), meaning there is no self-duality as in the $L^2$ norm.

**(3) Including Potential Terms: [Homogenization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%BD%90%E6%AC%A1%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity)**

If the cost function includes terms depending on the state $q$, it indeed cannot be directly processed as a standard metric. A Riemannian metric $g$ defines an inner product on tangent spaces, and curve length is $L = \int \sqrt{g_{ij}(q)\dot{q}^i\dot{q}^j}\,dt$. Note that the integrand is 1-homogeneous in velocity, so length is independent of the speed of [parameterization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%82%E6%95%B0%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) and depends only on the geometric path. The dependence on $q$ enters only through the metric tensor $g_{ij}(q)$, but this dependence must appear in inner-product form. Consider first the cost:

$$
J = \int \left( \frac{1}{2} \|\dot{q}\|^2 + V(q) \right) dt
$$

This is equivalent to a "kinetic energy + potential energy" integral; it is an action, not a length. Its minimizer depends on the time parameterization of the path: you can move faster in high-potential regions to reduce potential energy accumulation, which is fundamentally different from the "shape-optimal" geometric path. The potential term destroys parameterization invariance.

[Mathematical physics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%95%B0%E5%AD%A6%E7%89%A9%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity) solved this problem in the 19th century, precisely using a geometrization approach. Consider a conservative mechanical system with Lagrangian $L = T - V$, where kinetic energy $T = \frac{1}{2} g_{ij}\dot{q}^i\dot{q}^j$ and total energy $E = T + V$ is conserved along the motion. Maupertuis' principle states: among all paths with fixed energy $E$, the extremal trajectories of the action $J = \int (T-V)dt$ are equivalent to geodesics of the following metric:

$\tilde{L} = \int \sqrt{ 2\big(E - V(q)\big) \, g_{ij}\dot{q}^i\dot{q}^j } \, dt$

By introducing the conformal transformation $\tilde{g}_{ij}(q) = 2\big(E - V(q)\big) \cdot g_{ij}$, the potential $V(q)$ is perfectly absorbed into the metric itself, becoming the [conformal factor](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%B1%E5%BD%A2%E5%9B%A0%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity) $2(E-V)$ of the original metric. This new metric is called the Jacobi metric.

Now consider a more general cost: $J = \int \left[ \frac{1}{2} u^{\mathsf{T}} R u + V(x) \right] dt.$ Here there is no conserved mechanical energy, since $u$ is an external control that can inject energy arbitrarily. The Jacobi metric can still be used; this construction is known as Finsler geometrization in optimal control geometry. The resulting "metric" depends not only on velocity magnitude but possibly also on velocity direction, making it well suited for asymmetric costs and those containing state-dependent potentials.

The integrand is no longer 1-homogeneous under time scaling, so it cannot be directly written as the length on some distribution. We introduce auxiliary variables for homogenization, lifting the original problem to a purely geometric problem on an extended state space. First introduce a time-scaling variable, setting $dt = \lambda ds$, where $\lambda > 0$ is a new control variable (representing the rate of time flow). Define the new parameter $s \in [0,1]$ as the arc-length parameter. Both state and control are reparameterized: $\frac{dx}{ds} = \lambda \dot{x} = \lambda [f(x) + G(x) u], \quad \frac{dt}{ds} = \lambda.$

The original cost becomes:

$$
J = \int_0^1 \left[ \frac{1}{2} u^{\mathsf{T}} R u + V(x) \right] \lambda \, ds.
$$

Next define new control variables, treating $(u, \lambda)$ as new controls. The cost is now 1-homogeneous in $(dx/ds, dt/ds)$:

$J = \int_0^1 \mathcal{F}\left( x, t, \frac{dx}{ds}, \frac{dt}{ds} \right) ds$

where $\mathcal{F}$ is a Finsler function (on the extended tangent space). More precisely, we want to eliminate $u, \lambda$ and obtain the cost directly in terms of $(x', t')$. Let $x' = dx/ds, t' = dt/ds = \lambda$. Then $x' = t' f(x) + G(x)ut'$. Define the new control $\tilde{u} = u t'$, so that $x' = t' f(x) + G(x) \tilde{u}$. The cost can then be written as

$$
J = \int_0^1 \left[ \frac{1}{2} \frac{\tilde{u}^{\mathsf{T}} R \tilde{u}}{t'} + V(x) t' \right] ds,
$$

Finally, construct the Finsler metric: for fixed $(x, t)$ and [tangent vector](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%87%E5%90%91%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity) $(x', t')$, minimize the cost integrand. That is, solve

$$
\mathcal{F}(x, t; x', t') = \min_{\tilde{u}} \left\{ \frac{1}{2} \frac{\tilde{u}^{\mathsf{T}} R \tilde{u}}{t'} + V(x) t' \;:\; x' = t' f(x) + G(x) \tilde{u} \right\}.
$$

This is a convex optimization problem. Let $v = x' - t' f(x)$ (for $t'>0$), then $\tilde{u} = (G^{\mathsf{T}}G)^{-1}G^{\mathsf{T}} v$ (assuming injectivity). Substituting gives: $\mathcal{F} = \frac{1}{2t'} v^{\mathsf{T}} (G R^{-1} G^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger} v + V(x)t'$

Note that $v = x' - t' f(x)$, so 

$$
\mathcal{F}(x, t; x', t') = \frac{1}{2 t'} (x' - t' f)^{\mathsf{T}} P (x' - t' f) + V(x) t'\\ = t'\left[\frac{1}{2} \|x' - t' f\|^2_{P} + V(x)\right]
$$

where $P = (G R^{-1} G^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}$ is the metric inverse on $\Delta$. This $\mathcal{F}$ is a Finsler metric (strictly speaking, of Randers or Kropina type, depending on the form of $V$) on the extended state space $(x, t)$. It is positively 1-homogeneous in $(x', t')$. The original optimal control problem is now exactly equivalent to: on this Finsler manifold in the extended space, find the geodesic from the initial state to the terminal state.

-   The role of the potential is to modify the "local ruler" at each point in space: where the potential is low ($E-V$ large), the ruler lengthens and the geometric distance increases; where the potential is high, the ruler shortens. Thus, shortest paths naturally tend to avoid low-potential regions (where the ruler is long), perfectly reproducing the bias of physical trajectories.
-   The cost becomes a true metric depending only on velocity! It just depends on the chosen energy level $E$—different total energies yield different geometries.

From the symplectic geometry perspective of phase space, without introducing virtual time, one can obtain the Jacobi metric via a generalization of Maupertuis' principle. For the cost $L = \frac{1}{2} u^{\mathsf{T}} R u + V(x)$, the maximum principle yields the optimal Hamiltonian: $H(x, p) = p^{\mathsf{T}} f(x) + \frac{1}{2} p^{\mathsf{T}} G(x) R^{-1} G(x)^{\mathsf{T}} p - V(x).$

Since the system is autonomous and the terminal cost is zero, we must have $H = 0$ along optimal trajectories. On the zero-energy [hypersurface](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B6%85%E6%9B%B2%E9%9D%A2&zhida_source=entity) $\Sigma = \{H=0\}$, consider the variation of the action $J = \int p \, dq$. In the spirit of Maupertuis' principle, optimal trajectories are geodesics of some sub-Riemannian metric on $\Sigma$.

Define the symmetric matrix $A(x) = G R^{-1} G^{\mathsf{T}}$ (positive semidefinite, rank $m$). The Hamiltonian is $H = p^{\mathsf{T}} f + \frac{1}{2} p^{\mathsf{T}} A p - V = 0.$

To obtain a metric depending only on $x'$, we perform the inverse Legendre transform, noting that $A$ is not invertible. For tangent vectors satisfying $x' - f \in \operatorname{im} A$, define the Lagrangian:

$$
\mathcal{L}(x, x') = \sup_{p} \{ p^{\mathsf{T}} x' - H \} = \frac{1}{2} (x' - f)^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\dagger} (x' - f) + V(x).
$$

The action $J = \int \mathcal{L}(x, x') dt$ is not parameterization-invariant. However, we can construct a length by multiplying by a factor. The classical Jacobi metric formula is $ds^2 = [E - V(x)] \, g_{ij}(q) dq^i dq^j$. In the $H=0$ case, this generalizes to $ds^2 = 2[ - V(x) ] \, (x' - f)^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\dagger} (x' - f)$.

Thus, for motion on the zero-energy surface, there exists a degenerate Jacobi metric:

$$
\langle x', x' \rangle_{\text{J}} = 2( - V(x) ) \cdot (x' - f)^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\dagger}(x' - f), \ \  x' - f \in \operatorname{im} A
$$

If $G$ is surjective (fully actuated) and the full state feedback linearization gives $\dot{x} = u$, then $f=0$, $A = I$, $V(x) = V(q)$, recovering the classical Jacobi metric.

### 2. Jet Bundles: The Nature of Higher-Order Inputs

**(1) Motivation**

The cost in optimal control may also explicitly depend on acceleration (e.g., minimizing jerk). We need a geometric framework that systematically handles the relationships among position, velocity, acceleration, and their higher-order derivatives. Geometers invoke *jet bundles*—essentially, the tangent bundle of the tangent bundle. An optimal control problem:

$$
\min_{u} \int L(t, q, \dot{q}, u) dt, \quad \text{s.t. } \ddot{q} = f(q, \dot{q}, u)
$$

can be cast on the 1-jet bundle $J^1(\mathbb{R}, M)$ (with coordinates $(t, q, \dot{q})$) by defining a generalized "metric," known as the variational quadratic form or the Helmholtz condition. This structure is richer than the Sub-Riemannian one.

**(2) Jet Bundles and Contact Forms**

Let $M$ be an $n$-dimensional configuration manifold. Consider all smooth curves from $\mathbb{R}$ (the time axis) to $M$.

**Definition (First-order jet bundle $J^1(\mathbb{R}, M)$):** Two curves $\gamma_1, \gamma_2: \mathbb{R} \to M$ are said to be *first-order equivalent* at $t_0$ if $\gamma_1(t_0) = \gamma_2(t_0), \quad \dot{\gamma}_1(t_0) = \dot{\gamma}_2(t_0).$ The first-order jet bundle $J^1(\mathbb{R}, M)$ is the set of all such equivalence classes. An equivalence class is denoted by $j^1_{t_0}(\gamma)$.

In local coordinates $(q^i)$, $j^1_{t_0}(\gamma)$ is completely determined by $(t_0; q^i(t_0), \dot{q}^i(t_0))$. Hence, $J^1(\mathbb{R}, M)$ is locally isomorphic to $\mathbb{R} \times TM$, with coordinates $(t, q^i, v^i)$, where $v^i$ represents velocity.

**Definition (Second-order jet bundle $J^2(\mathbb{R}, M)$):** Two curves are *second-order equivalent* at $t_0$ if their first two derivatives coincide at $t_0$. The second-order jet bundle has local coordinates $(t, q^i, v^i, a^i)$, where $a^i$ represents acceleration.

**Key Property:** A curve $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to M$ naturally lifts to a curve in $J^2(\mathbb{R}, M)$: $j^2\gamma(t) = (t; \gamma^i(t), \dot{\gamma}^i(t), \ddot{\gamma}^i(t)).$

This lifted curve is called the *prolongation*. Jet bundles carry an intrinsic geometric structure that distinguishes "prolongations of genuine curves" from arbitrary curves.

**Definition (Contact forms):** On $J^1(\mathbb{R}, M)$, define the 1-forms: $\theta^i = dq^i - v^i dt, i = 1, \dots, n.$ On $J^2(\mathbb{R}, M)$, additionally define: $\Theta^i = dv^i - a^i dt, \quad i = 1, \dots, n.$

These forms define a distribution on the tangent space—the *Cartan distribution* $\mathcal{C}$: $\mathcal{C} = \ker \{\theta^i, \Theta^i\}.$

**Geometric Meaning:** A curve $\Gamma(t) \subset J^2(\mathbb{R}, M)$ is the second-order prolongation of some original curve $\gamma(t) \subset M$ if and only if $\Gamma(t)$ is everywhere tangent to the Cartan distribution. That is: $\theta^i(\dot{\Gamma}) = 0, \Theta^i(\dot{\Gamma}) = 0 \quad \forall i.$ The contact condition is precisely the kinematic relationship. Thus, the Cartan distribution is the geometric embodiment of a second-order differential equation: a second-order ODE system is a vector field on $J^2(\mathbb{R}, M)$ satisfying the Cartan distribution constraint.

**(3) Variational Principles and the Variational Biform**

How do we describe the calculus of variations and optimal control within this framework? First, the Lagrangian is a function $L: J^1(\mathbb{R}, M) \to \mathbb{R}$, i.e., $L = L(t, q, \dot q)$. The action functional is: $S[\gamma] = \int L(t, q, \dot{q}) dt$.

To express the variational principle geometrically, we introduce the *Cartan form* (standard construction for the second-order case):

On $J^1(\mathbb{R}, Q)$, define the Poincaré-Cartan 1-form: $\Xi_L = L dt + \frac{\partial L}{\partial v^i} (dq^i - v^i dt) = L dt + p_i \theta^i,$

where $p_i = \partial L / \partial v^i$ is the conjugate momentum. Note that $\Xi_L$ is a 1-form on $J^1(\mathbb{R}, M)$.

**Definition (Variational biform):** Taking the exterior derivative of $\Xi_L$ yields a closed 2-form on $J^1(\mathbb{R}, M)$:

$$
\begin {aligned}   \Omega_L &= -d\Xi_L \\ &= -d(L dt + p_i \theta^i) \\ &=dp_i \wedge dq^i - dp_i \wedge v^i dt - p_i dv^i \wedge dt - dL \wedge dt. ( d\theta^i = -dv^i \wedge dt) \end {aligned}
$$

Further using

$$
dL = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}dq^i + \frac{\partial L}{\partial v^i}dv^i + \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}dq^i + p_i dv^i + \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt
$$

and substituting, we get:

$$
\Omega_L = dp_i \wedge dq^i - \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i} dq^i + \frac{\partial L}{\partial t} dt \right) \wedge dt.
$$

A more concise form is: $\Omega_L = dp_i \wedge dq^i - dH \wedge dt$, where $H(t,q,v,p) = p_i v^i - L(t,q,v)$ is the Hamiltonian given by the Legendre transform. This 2-form is precisely the symplectic form on the time-extended phase space.

**Geometric Formulation of the Variational Principle:** A curve $\Gamma \subset J^1(\mathbb{R}, M)$ is an extremal curve of the Euler-Lagrange equations (i.e., a stationary point of the action) if and only if the tangent vector field $X$ of $\Gamma$ satisfies $X \lrcorner \, \Omega_L = 0$ (on the Cartan distribution). This condition is equivalent to the Euler-Lagrange equations: $\frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L}{\partial v^i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i} = 0.$

**(4) The Helmholtz Problem**

Now, bypass the variational principle and directly consider a system of second-order ODEs: $\ddot{q}^i = f^i(t, q, \dot{q}), i = 1, \dots, n.$ This is equivalent to giving a section on $J^2(\mathbb{R}, M)$. **The inverse problem (Helmholtz problem):** When can this system be derived from a variational principle? That is, does there exist a Lagrangian $L(t,q,v)$ such that

$$
\frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}^i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i} = 0 \iff \ddot{q}^i = f^i(t,q,\dot{q})
$$

The *Helmholtz conditions* are the integrability conditions for this problem in local coordinates. Geometrically, this is equivalent to: there exists a closed 2-form $\Omega$ on $J^1(\mathbb{R}, M)$ whose "restriction" to the given second-order equation field satisfies a specific non-degeneracy condition. More concretely, we want to associate $f^i$ with a semi-basic closed 2-form on $J^1(\mathbb{R}, M)$.

Define a suitable set of 1-forms: $\psi^i = dv^i - f^i(t,q,v) dt.$ These forms define constraints on $J^2$. The standard form of the Helmholtz conditions (Douglas's classical result, 1941) seeks a non-degenerate matrix $g_{ij}(t,q,v)$ such that

$$
g_{ik} (dv^k - f^k dt) = \frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L}{\partial v^i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}
$$

is integrable. This leads to a set of conditions that $g_{ij}$ must satisfy, including:

1.  Symmetry: $g_{ij} = g_{ji}$
2.  The Hessian condition with respect to velocity: $\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial v^k} = \frac{\partial g_{ik}}{\partial v^j}$
3.  A set of complex compatibility conditions on $f^i$, essentially requiring that $\frac{\partial f^i}{\partial v^j}$ satisfies a certain self-adjointness property.

On jet bundles, a variational principle corresponds to a *Lepage equivalence class*—an equivalence class of closed 2-forms $\Omega$ that induces a regular symplectic structure on the Cartan distribution. The Helmholtz condition is the integrability obstruction to the existence of such a closed 2-form.

Our earlier reduction of second-order mechanical system dynamics to a first-order affine system is essentially a projection from $J^2$ to $TM$. The control $u$ in the cost $\int L(t,q,\dot{q},u)dt$ can be viewed as an acceleration variable in $J^2$, linked to the dynamics and control. The Hamiltonian system arising from PMP is naturally defined on $T^*(J^1(\mathbb{R}, Q))$ or higher-order cotangent bundles. The variational biform $\Omega_L$ is the precursor to the symplectic form there.

## IV. Turning to Symplectic Geometry

At this point, the geometry underlying our problems seems quite complete, yet we also find ourselves hitting walls in certain places. Consider a scenario where we are not solving a control problem but a decision-planning problem. The task-oriented cost function becomes far more complex, making it difficult to write as the generalized "potential plus kinetic energy" terms above. Even if it could be written, the potential $V$ might be non-convex. Furthermore, the complexity of obstacle avoidance in planning problems pushes the constraints toward mixed-integer levels; once everything is converted to penalty functions, the non-convexity of $V$ becomes even more pronounced. So even invoking jet bundles or homogenization techniques seems insufficient. Methods like Finsler geometrization in homogenization rely heavily on defining a metric via an optimization problem; when the cost is non-convex or even non-smooth, defining the metric becomes problematic.

### 1. Complex Costs

The boundary of mainstream geometry remains at "smooth quadratic forms" or "smooth strongly convex norms." Crossing this boundary, the mathematical tools become exceptionally arduous, and the engineering returns are unclear, creating a blank region driven by research inertia. Yet precisely in this blank region, operations continue quietly in the background under the more abstract guises of dynamical systems and non-smooth analysis.

**(1) Theoretical Perspective**

Mainstream geometry is built upon differentiable manifolds + smooth tensor fields. This framework boasts a powerful toolbox: tensor analysis, curvature, geodesic equations, comparison theorems... everything supports local computation. Even Finsler geometry, which generalizes the metric, still requires a baseline regularity to ensure the well-posedness of the geodesic equation and to discuss concepts like flag curvature.

If the cost function goes to extremes—non-convex, even non-smooth (e.g., not a norm at all)—then the pointwise norm ceases to exist, and an Finsler structure cannot be directly constructed on the state space. Geometry still exists, but the phrase "geometry of non-convex non-smooth costs" is almost entirely absent from mainstream mathematics and applied literature. This is because the structure is too difficult to study, and the very definition of "geometry" changes. Such objects are diverted to specialized branches like non-smooth analysis, variational analysis, metric geometry, and optimal transport. The results are relatively fragmented and fail to coalesce into a unified and grand theoretical system like Riemannian geometry.

Regardless of how pathological the cost is, as long as it defines a positive functional on curves (e.g., $J = \int L(t,q,u) dt$), we can attempt to define the minimum cost between two points as a distance $d(q_0, q_1) = \inf J$. If this satisfies the triangle inequality, we obtain a (quasi-)metric space. This enters the realm of metric geometry, where differentiable geodesics are not required; non-smooth concepts like cuts and shortest paths can be used. Examples include Alexandrov spaces, Carnot-Carathéodory spaces (sub-Riemannian limits), etc., which require tools from real analysis, measure theory, and metric geometry rather than classical differential geometry. The research community is smaller.

-   Once the cost becomes non-smooth or non-convex, geodesics are no longer described by ODEs. Instead, we encounter differential inclusions or variational inequalities. We lose the pointwise metric and must contend with "subdifferentials" or the "support functions" of reachable sets.
-   Non-convexity destroys local uniqueness and can even lead to discontinuous dependence on the endpoints, making it extremely difficult to establish a global "geometry" (such as homotopy or curvature bounds). Mathematicians favor elegant structures, and non-convexity often breeds pathological counterexamples.
-   The "geometry" in state space transforms into the geometry of level sets and reachable sets. For instance, a non-convex cost may cause optimal paths to jump between extremal points under control constraints, and the corresponding geodesic in state space might exhibit "refraction" or "winding" phenomena. This is no longer a sub-Riemannian geodesic but rather an extremal curve in some sub-Riemannian cone structure or non-smooth length space.

**(2) Applied Perspective**

In simpler problems, practical applications deliberately choose tractable cost forms. Quadratic, L1, and similar costs combined with linear(ized) systems yield convex optimization that can generally be solved efficiently. Even with a generalized "kinetic + potential" form, practitioners rarely perform explicit geometrization, because there is another powerful tool: the maximum principle. Everything is lifted to phase space (the cotangent bundle), where the Hamiltonian automatically handles all non-convexity and non-smoothness. At this point, there is no need to force a metric in state space; the Hamiltonian flow on the symplectic manifold already encodes optimality. Moreover, the maximum principle is equivalent to the KKT conditions in numerical optimization.

Furthermore, what is often called a "geometric planner" in robotics is typically based on the known geometry of environmental obstacles, not the differential geometry of the cost. In motion planning, the cost is usually an add-on, adjustable quantity. A common practice is hierarchical: use the environment geometry (obstacles in configuration space) to generate path candidates, then evaluate and optimize them with the cost function, rather than condensing the cost function into a fixed geometric structure in state space. This further reduces the urgent need for a "geometry of non-smooth costs."

It is only when investigating the deep geometric structure of a system (e.g., analyzing reachability, singularities) that scholars invoke Jacobi metrics or Finsler structures. These appear more in geometric mechanics or mathematical physics than in mainstream engineering applications. When geometric structures fail to yield efficient, specialized algorithms, no one is inclined to explore the obscure realm of "non-smooth geometry."

### **2\. The Advantages of Symplectic Geometry**

On the surface, symplectic geometry only has area, not length, and thus cannot define a cost. All Hamiltonian flows are "equivalent" in the sense that they only preserve the area element; the geometry does not care "how far the trajectory travels in space." A rotation and a translation are identical in symplectic geometry as long as they preserve the symplectic structure. Locally, all symplectic manifolds are standard, and using the symplectic structure to define a distance yields only a trivial quantity that is locally constant and featureless. However, symplectic geometry plays a central role behind the scenes; it simply does not stand in the foreground to directly define trajectories. No matter how pathological the cost, the maximum principle still applies. When you solve using the maximum principle, you introduce [costate variables](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%8F%E6%80%81%E5%8F%98%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity)$p$, lifting the problem to the cotangent bundle. On the cotangent bundle $T^*M$, we still have the canonical symplectic form $\omega$, and the Hamiltonian is $H(q,p) = \sup_{u \in U} \left( \langle p, f(q,u) \rangle - L(q,u) \right)$, which may be nondifferentiable or even nonconvex. Nevertheless, the concept of Hamiltonian flow on a symplectic manifold can be generalized via [viscosity solutions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%B2%98%E6%80%A7%E8%A7%A3&zhida_source=entity), differential inclusions, and nonsmooth analysis, and the topology and symplectic structure of the level sets of the Hamiltonian can still be studied. In other words, the rigid framework of symplectic geometry carries the essence of optimality, even when the cost makes the metric structure of the state space singular.

Mathematicians prefer to analyze systems with nonsmooth Hamiltonians using smooth symplectic geometry in the higher-dimensional phase space (the symplectic manifold), rather than attempting to build a nonsmooth "metric geometry" on the state space. The former remains within mainstream geometry (symplectic geometry, contact geometry, dynamical systems), while the latter would fall into a swamp lacking mature tools.

### 3\. The HJB Equation

The maximum principle is only the local symplectic geometry along an optimal trajectory, whereas the HJB equation is a PDE that characterizes the global optimal value function and feedback control.

**(1) The HJB Equation and the Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP)**

The [HJB equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=HJB+%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) is a first-order nonlinear PDE whose characteristic curves are precisely the Hamiltonian system given by PMP.

Given an optimal control problem (cost $L$, dynamics $\dot{q}=f(q,u)$), with Hamiltonian $H(q,p)$. Consider the time-dependent value function $S(q,t)$; by the [principle of dynamic programming](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E6%80%81%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity), it satisfies the HJB equation:

$$
\partial_t S+ H(q, \partial_q S) = 0
$$

For infinite-horizon or terminal steady-state problems, or when the value function does not depend on time, we have $H(q, dS) = 0$, where $H:T^*M \to \mathbb R$.

Consider a [first-order nonlinear PDE](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%80%E9%98%B6%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E5%81%8F%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity)$F(x, u(x), \nabla u(x)) = 0, \quad x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, where $u: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is the unknown function and $\nabla u = (u_{x_1}, \dots, u_{x_n})$. We seek "characteristic curves" of $u$ along which the PDE reduces to a system of ODEs.

Introduce a parameter $s$, suppose $x(s)$ is a curve, and we want to simultaneously track $u(s) = u(x(s))$ and $p(s) = \nabla u(x(s))$, treating them as independent variables that are compatible. $\dot{u} = \sum_i u_{x_i} \dot{x}_i = \sum_i p_i \dot{x}_i.$ $\dot{p}_i = \sum_j u_{x_i x_j} \dot{x}_j.$

Now, differentiating the PDE $F(x,u,p)=0$ with respect to $x_i$ gives $F_{x_i} + F_u p_i + \sum_j F_{p_j} u_{x_i x_j} = 0.$ We want the evolution of $p$ to close along the curve. If we choose the tangent of the curve to satisfy $\dot{x}_j = F_{p_j}$, we obtain $\dot{p}_i = -F_{x_i} - F_u p_i$ and $\dot{u} = \sum p_i F_{p_i}$.

$$
\dot{x}_i = \frac{\partial F}{\partial p_i},\dot{p}_i = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i} - \frac{\partial F}{\partial u} p_i,\dot{u} = \sum_i p_i \frac{\partial F}{\partial p_i}
$$

This is the [characteristic equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%89%B9%E5%BE%81%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) of the PDE, and its integral curves are called characteristic curves.

For the steady-state Hamilton–Jacobi equation $H(x, d S(x)) = 0$, the characteristic equations are $\dot{x}_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},  \dot{p}_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i}$, which is precisely the Hamiltonian system derived from the maximum principle.

An introduction to the [method of characteristics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%89%B9%E5%BE%81%E7%BA%BF%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) can be found here:

**(2) Lagrangian Submanifolds**

Take a smooth function $S: M \to \mathbb{R}$. Its differential graph is $\Lambda_S := \{ (q, p) \in T^*M \mid p = dS_q \}.$ In local coordinates, this is written as $p_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial q^i}(q)$. Clearly, it is an $n$-dimensional submanifold, since $q$ can serve as coordinates on it, with the embedding map $q \mapsto (q, dS_q)$.

$$
dp_i = d\left( \frac{\partial S}{\partial q^i} \right) = \frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} dq^j.
$$

Therefore, the symplectic form restricted to $\Lambda_S$ is:

$$
\omega|_{\Lambda_S} = dq^i \wedge dp_i = dq^i \wedge \left( \frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} dq^j \right) = \frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} dq^i \wedge dq^j.
$$

Since $\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} = \frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial q^j \partial q^i}$ while $dq^i \wedge dq^j$ is antisymmetric, their contraction is identically zero. Hence $\omega|_{\Lambda_S} = 0$. A manifold of half the symplectic dimension on which the symplectic form vanishes is called a Lagrangian submanifold. If $S$ is not defined on the entire base manifold but only locally, this yields a Lagrangian immersion.

Clearly, the differential graph of the value function $S$, $\{(q, dS(q))\}$, is precisely such a Lagrangian submanifold. It satisfies $\omega|_\Lambda = 0$, so locally there exists a generating function $S$ such that $p_i = \partial S/\partial q^i$. Studying the information in this value function is equivalent to studying the differential graph.

The steady-state HJB equation $H(q, dS) = 0$ is precisely the equation constraining this Lagrangian submanifold. Geometrically, it states that $\Lambda$ is contained in the [zero level set](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9B%B6%E6%B0%B4%E5%B9%B3%E9%9B%86&zhida_source=entity) of the Hamiltonian and is invariant under the Hamiltonian flow. Consider the Hamiltonian vector field $X_H$ generated by $H$, satisfying $i_{X_H} \omega = -dH$. Its flow gives the Hamiltonian flow on phase space (the characteristic equations), which is a symplectomorphism and naturally preserves Lagrangian submanifolds. One can also compute this directly: along the Hamiltonian flow, $\frac{d}{dt} (p_i - \partial_i S) = -\partial_i H - \partial_i \partial_j S \cdot \partial_{p_j} H.$

Since $H(q, \nabla S(q)) = 0$, we have $\partial_i H + \partial_{p_j} H \cdot \partial_i \partial_j S = 0$. Combining these yields

$$
\frac{d}{dt}(p_i - \partial_i S) = - (\partial_{p_j} H) (\partial_i \partial_j S - \partial_j \partial_i S) =0
$$

That is, the [Hamiltonian vector field](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%93%88%E5%AF%86%E9%A1%BF%E5%90%91%E9%87%8F%E5%9C%BA&zhida_source=entity) $X_H$ is tangent to $\Lambda_S$, and the flow preserves $\Lambda_S$. This Lagrangian submanifold is "woven" by the family of characteristic curves: by varying the initial point $q_0$, these curves sweep out the entire $\Lambda_S$, and their projections onto $M$ are the optimal trajectories.

**(3) The Time-Dependent Case: [Contact Geometry](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%87%E8%A7%A6%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity) and Legendrian Submanifolds**

Consider the non-stationary case, or the case where the value function depends on time $S(t,q)$. Consider the extended phase space $T^*M \times \mathbb{R}$ with coordinates $(q,p,t)$ and introduce the contact form $\theta = dS - p\,dq + H\,dt$ (or similar). The time-dependent HJB equation is:

$$
∂_tS+H ⁣(t,q,∂_qS)=0
$$

This is a [first-order PDE](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%80%E9%98%B6%E5%81%8F%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity). On the contact manifold $(T^∗M×\mathbb R,θ)$, the 1-graph of the equation, $\Lambda=\{ (t,q, \partial_t S, p=\partial_q S) \}$, is a Legendrian submanifold—that is, a maximal integral submanifold with $θ∣_\Lambda=0$ everywhere. Essentially, this is the contact-geometric counterpart of a Lagrangian submanifold. By promoting time to a state variable, the cases can be treated uniformly. Any symplectic manifold can be realized as a reduction of a contact manifold via "contactification."

**(4) Facing Complex Costs**

When the cost is nonconvex and nonsmooth, the value function may be nonsmooth, but its geometric essence remains powerful:

Viscosity solutions and Lagrangian submanifolds with boundary graphs: points where the value function is non-differentiable correspond to the Lagrangian submanifold no longer being a [single-valued graph](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%95%E5%80%BC%E5%9B%BE&zhida_source=entity), but rather developing "vertical walls" (submanifolds with vertical parts). This typically arises where characteristic curves overlap (shocks). One can define reachable cones and subdifferentials of the graph, which still form a kind of Lagrangian set in phase space, albeit no longer a smooth submanifold but a set with "singular edges." In [control theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity), the "frontier of the reachable set"—the boundary of the level set of the minimum-time value function—often corresponds to these singular sets. Geometrically, one must study them using generalized Lagrangian submanifolds or singularity theory.

Consider the standard projection $\pi: T^*M \to M，(q,p) \mapsto q$, and restrict it to $\Lambda$ as $\pi|_\Lambda: \Lambda \to M$. Lagrangian [singularity theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A5%87%E7%82%B9%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity) (Arnold, Hörmander) tells us that, for a generic Lagrangian submanifold $\Lambda$, the set of critical points $\Sigma(\Lambda)$ of the projection $\pi|\Lambda$ consists of points where the tangent map $d(\pi|\Lambda)$ is degenerate (a tangential intersection rather than a transverse one). At these points, there exists a nonzero tangent vector $v \in T_{(q,p)}\Lambda$ with $d\pi(v) = 0$, meaning it has only a $p$-component and no $q$-component. This implies that, locally, $\Lambda$ "stands up" vertically and can no longer be represented as $p = dS(q)$, because singularities or multivaluedness appear. The caustic surface (caustic) is defined as the set of these critical values: $\pi(\Sigma(\Lambda)) \subset M$. This is precisely where the value function is non-differentiable in state space—the frontier of the reachable set (or the envelope of optimal trajectories). Starting from an initial point $x_0$, optimal trajectories advancing with different initial costates $p_0$ may intersect (or self-intersect) in state space. At an intersection point, multiple characteristic curves with different values of $p$ arrive at the same point. At the boundary where the single-valued graph is about to break, we have critical points of the projection, whose image is the frontier of the reachable set. For an intuitive understanding of the difference between tangential and transverse intersections of submanifolds and why they form fronts or envelopes, see the figure below.

If a Lagrangian submanifold $\Lambda$ (the differential graph of the value function) intersects the zero section ($p=0$) transversely, then the intersection points correspond to critical points of the optimal solution. Lagrangian [intersection theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%B8%E4%BA%A4%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity) in symplectic geometry (e.g., the Arnold conjecture, Floer homology) studies lower bounds on such intersection points. This provides [topological constraints](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279802250&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8B%93%E6%89%91%E7%BA%A6%E6%9D%9F&zhida_source=entity) on the multivaluedness and singularities of solutions to the HJB equation: even if the value function develops folds (shocks) in state space, its Lagrangian submanifold in phase space must obey a certain Maslov-index-type invariance, which guarantees the global structure of the optimal solution.

Thus, the task of symplectic geometry is no longer to trace a single curve but to weave an entire surface, and to handle the folds, cusps, and topological transformations that the surface may undergo. Seeking a global optimal feedback = studying the HJB equation = finding, on the cotangent bundle, that submanifold contained in the zero energy surface and invariant under the Hamiltonian flow, including its existence, uniqueness, and singularities. Geodesics (optimal trajectories) are characteristic curves on it, fibers over the Lagrangian submanifold.

### 4\. Applications

Direct numerical solution of the HJB equation suffers from the curse of dimensionality. However, modern research offers many ideas and different techniques:

**(1) Method of characteristics (ray-tracing)**

The PMP is used to generate a family of trajectories via shooting. These trajectories form the skeleton of the Lagrange submanifold, from which the value function or a local feedback law can be reconstructed. Essentially, this is an indirect method for solving the HJB equation using symplectic geometric tools (generating functions). Moreover, in many cases we are only interested in a single optimal trajectory.

**(2) Model reduction and symplectic reduction**

If the system possesses symmetries, then the value function must inherit these symmetries as well. The HJB equation can then be solved on a reduced phase space—this is precisely Marsden-Weinstein symplectic reduction and Hamilton-Jacobi reduction. Consider a symplectic manifold (phase space); if it admits a symmetry action of a Lie group $G$ that preserves the symplectic form, we can do two things:

1.  Fix the value of the conserved quantity (momentum map). For example, set the angular momentum to zero.
2.  Treat the orbits under the group action as equivalence classes and quotient them out.

The resulting new space automatically inherits a reduced symplectic form and remains a symplectic manifold, but its dimension is reduced from $2n$ to 

$$
2(n - \dim G)
$$

. The HJB equation $H(q, dS) = 0$ can be reduced to an HJB equation on this lower-dimensional space, and the value function on the original space can be "lifted" back up via the symmetry.

**(3) Geometric integrators**

Constructing symplectic- or contact-preserving numerical schemes to track Lagrange submanifolds over long times, rather than directly solving the PDE by brute force. Each step exactly preserves the symplectic form of the phase space. This means that even with discretization errors, the discrete map remains a symplectic transformation. These methods are highly regarded in orbital mechanics, where extreme precision and long-term stability are required.

**(4) Static HJB in path planning**

Consider the shortest-time problem in an isotropic medium. Your speed depends only on position $c(q) > 0$, the control input is the direction, and the cost is time. The value function $T(q) = \text{shortest time to reach the goal from } q$ satisfies the HJB equation

$$
\max_{\|u\|=1} \left( \nabla T(q) \cdot u \right) - \frac{1}{c(q)} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \|\nabla T(q)\| = \frac{1}{c(q)}
$$

This is the Eikonal equation. Its characteristics are the shortest-time paths (light rays). The Fast Marching Method (FMM) essentially tracks the wavefront of this Lagrange submanifold at the discrete level. Once a grid point's value is "activated," it never changes, mimicking the wavefront sweeping through. The algorithm uses upwind schemes to strictly enforce causality: information only flows from points with smaller values to points with larger values. This precisely corresponds to the unidirectional flow of characteristics in phase space. It does not track regions where multiple characteristics entangle (where multi-valuedness may arise, but FMM selects the viscosity solution, i.e., the earliest arriving wavefront).

At the HJB level, when people attempt to handle potentials, obstacles, and non-convex costs using numerical methods (such as level set methods for Hamilton-Jacobi equations or generating function methods), they are unwittingly stepping into a geometric realm governed by Lagrange submanifolds.

Meanwhile, several directions are bringing the geometry of non-smooth, non-convex costs to the surface:

-   **Optimal transport and Wasserstein geometry**: This field deals with non-smooth, non-convex cost functions. Its geometric structure (Otto's Riemannian structure, McCann interpolation) is entirely metric geometry and has permeated the frontiers of sampling, optimization, and learning-based methods.
-   **Hybrid systems and non-smooth analysis**: Rigid contact problems in robot locomotion and friction involve phase space geometries that are non-smooth (impacts, jumps). A community of mechanicians is addressing these using non-smooth contact geometry.
-   **Non-smooth generalizations of sub-Riemannian geometry**: Studying geodesics when the distribution has singularities or when costs are non-quadratic—this is slowly developing into a full-fledged theory within mathematics.
