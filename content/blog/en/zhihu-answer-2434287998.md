Between 1880 and 1920, the modern analysis textbook genre produced many masterpieces in English, French, and German. In Britain, there were Whittaker's *A Course of Modern Analysis* and Hardy's *A Course of Pure Mathematics*; in France, Jordan, Picard, Hermite, and Goursat each wrote their own *Cours d'analyse*; and in Germany, Carathéodory's celebrated *Vorlesungen über reelle Funktionen* (still styled as a calculus course at the time, modeled on Euler's treatises and not yet incorporating the foundational rigor that emerged in the mid-to-late nineteenth century).

Before 1930, analysis courses were divided only into real analysis, complex analysis, Fourier analysis, functional analysis, and the like.

In 1951, authors of Rudin's generation, following institutional curricula, extracted mathematical analysis from real analysis to serve as an introductory course in analysis, making it a standalone subject.

In 1960, the field of analysis produced a classic: *Foundations of Modern Analysis* by Dieudonné, a leading figure of the Bourbaki school. The French original was titled *Fondements de l'analyse moderne*, and the English edition renamed it *Foundations of Modern Analysis*. Dieudonné regarded this work as the "modern analysis" of the postwar era, relegating earlier analysis treatises to the category of "classical analysis." Dieudonné's stated purpose in writing the series is made clear in the preface to the first volume: "...I have tried to write for the mathematicians of the 1970s a treatise on analysis comparable to those that Jordan, Picard, and Goursat wrote for the students of mathematics between 1880 and 1920." That is, a comprehensive treatise that, once mastered, would allow students to begin original research in their respective fields. (All of these earlier authors were nineteenth-century mathematicians. There was a French tradition, lasting at least until the early twentieth century, in which mathematicians wrote textbooks as massive tomes, covering everything from the fundamentals to the research frontier.) However, the development of mathematics in the twentieth century far surpassed that of the nineteenth, so early-twentieth-century analysis treatises, such as Goursat's, ran to only three volumes and roughly a thousand pages. Dieudonné's series, by contrast, swelled to nine volumes, each between 300 and 500 pages (though some appear to be around 200 pages), and even then it realized only a fraction of his original plan—he had intended to cover every branch of analysis known at the time, following the example of his predecessors. When this textbook appeared, its novel conceptual framework—for instance, replacing countability with filters, treating the Riemann integral merely as useful computational exercise, and viewing mappings as variables—immediately triggered a global rewriting of analysis textbooks.

By 1969, Dieudonné decided to expand and revise the work. *Foundations of Modern Analysis* was lightly revised and republished almost unchanged as the first volume of the new *Treatise on Analysis*, subtitled *Foundations of Modern Analysis, Expanded and Revised*. The expanded second volume became the second volume of the *Treatise on Analysis*, subtitled *Expanded and Revised*. These two volumes can be regarded as an extended edition of *Foundations of Modern Analysis* and studied independently. The remaining six volumes cover real analysis, complex analysis, calculus on manifolds, topological spaces, functional analysis, Lie groups, and other major topics in analysis. The English edition no longer carries the subtitle *Foundations of Modern Analysis* (the French edition, which I have not seen, may differ). According to the announcement on the original title page, the series was planned for ten volumes; only eight volumes are now circulating online, so it may never have been completed.

In the 1980s, China translated the first two volumes of the *Treatise on Analysis*, using the English subtitle *Foundations of Modern Analysis*. The 1960 edition has no electronic version online, though the Chinese translation circulates as scanned old books and PDFs. It is no exaggeration to say that, to this day, the teaching of analysis has yet to move beyond the Bourbaki era represented by Dieudonné. The influence of Dieudonné's modern analysis course on global analysis teaching is evident from the boastful blurb on the back cover of the English edition.
