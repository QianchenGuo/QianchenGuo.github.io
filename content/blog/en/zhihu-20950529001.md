## 1. Motion Planning Task Requirements

In essence, solving the problem of an [autonomous agent](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%87%AA%E4%B8%BB%E6%99%BA%E8%83%BD%E4%BD%93&zhida_source=entity) is equivalent to a [feedback control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%8D%E9%A6%88%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity) problem for an abstract system, except that the feedback loop is built between the environment and the system. This assertion has long been firmly established in fields such as reinforcement learning.

![handwritten note](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-35fcfb40485eccf0ce7355dc8d6a6157_r.jpg)

However, for a specific physical object—such as a robot or an autonomous vehicle—the complexity of the entire system is not comparable to Atari games or various board games. In such cases, the whole system is typically solved in a hierarchical manner, and this layering is essentially a recursive [design method](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%AE%BE%E8%AE%A1%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) in control theory, which can be divided into [backstepping](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=backstepping&zhida_source=entity) and forwarding, where the former is more common and has a familiar alias—[cascade control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%B2%E7%BA%A7%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity). The intuitive way to understand it is that the inner loop reduces the burden on the outer loop (with the inner loop present, the outer loop only needs to generate a reference for the closed-loop dynamics without facing the open-loop dynamics directly), and the [inner loop](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=3&q=%E5%86%85%E7%8E%AF&zhida_source=entity) only needs to track the outer-loop reference. This way, a large system and a large task are tackled via divide-and-conquer. As a side note, there is a dedicated branch in control theory to study this kind of layering—recursive construction of [Lyapunov functions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Lyapunov%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) based on passivity theory.

Specifically in the field of [decision-making and planning](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E5%86%B3%E7%AD%96%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity), regardless of the underlying method, planning algorithms in a broad sense (including decision-planning-control) are also solved hierarchically, though the boundaries between layers may vary. What remains consistent is that, in the end, different layers must collectively achieve multiple levels of task objectives.

### 1.1 Feasibility and Optimality

First, it is clear that the robot needs a trajectory that is safe, smooth enough to be executed, and of low energy consumption. This is called the feasibility of the solution trajectory. At the same time, real-time requirements should be satisfied as much as possible (which imposes constraints on the resource demands of the planning algorithm), especially in complex [dynamic environments](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E6%80%81%E7%8E%AF%E5%A2%83&zhida_source=entity), where real-time performance partly equates to safety. Furthermore, the optimality of the solution trajectory typically includes requirements such as short path length, short time duration, and low energy consumption. In essence, optimality serves as a [regularization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AD%A3%E5%88%99%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) for the ill-posed problem of motion planning, since motion planning clearly has multiple solutions depending on the requirements.

These ensure that the robot-executed trajectory is physically consistent, while also eliminating the multiple solutions caused by ill-posedness, so that the solution trajectory meets basic needs.

### 1.2 Trajectory-Level Robustness

[Robustness](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E9%B2%81%E6%A3%92%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) is a concept well established in [control theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity), and the definition can be extended to planning. Trajectory-level robustness means that, for a set of disturbances, the family of trajectories shares common stability and feasibility properties. The disturbance set acts on the dynamics of the agent and [environment evolution](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8E%AF%E5%A2%83%E6%BC%94%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity), ultimately reflecting its impact on the output trajectory of the planning algorithm.

### 1.3 Simple Interaction

In different environments, many tasks depend on interactions between the agent and the environment. For instance, in [robotics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%BA%E5%99%A8%E4%BA%BA%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity), the most fundamental tasks—navigation, locomotion, and manipulation—each have distinct key aspects: navigation centers on obstacle avoidance, [locomotion](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=locomotion&zhida_source=entity) focuses on contact between the body/feet and the ground or other surfaces, and manipulation involves contact between the [end-effector](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=end-effector&zhida_source=entity) and other objects.

In obstacle avoidance, interaction with the environment is not "contact-based" but relies on perception in this "non-contact" mode. Contact interaction, however, involves theories such as contact models and [hybrid dynamics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B7%B7%E5%90%88%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B&zhida_source=entity). In some tasks, [compliant control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9F%94%E9%A1%BA%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity) may also appear at lower levels, and the higher-level planner should account for compliance.

### 1.4 Behavior and Task Generalization

If the above aspects resemble the "skills" of a large model, the next consideration is how to combine these capabilities to accomplish more complex tasks. This means broadening the scope to decision-making and planning rather than just motion planning (from the cerebellum to the cerebrum), where the environment and the planned object itself become more complex, imposing higher-level requirements on planning:

-   The concept of behavior generalization lies in the fact that the behaviors generated by behavior planning must be complete with respect to the tasks to be accomplished: the set of behaviors required by the task and the set of behaviors covered by the planner must be equivalent. The hardest part here is that we often neither know how to define behaviors nor what the behavior set for a task looks like. This lack of scientific rigor in design has given rise to the so-called "corner cases" in [autonomous driving](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E8%87%AA%E5%8A%A8%E9%A9%BE%E9%A9%B6&zhida_source=entity) in recent years;
-   Interaction becomes more complex: not only interaction with other objects, but also interaction with other agents. For example, in autonomous driving, the ego vehicle interacts with other vehicles; in home robotics, the robot must interact with family members. This kind of interaction differs from simple interaction—it spans longer time horizons, involves complex evolution, and simultaneously affects trajectory, contact, and other levels;
-   For the solution trajectory or behavior produced by planning, a "risk" assessment should be provided, starting from the [physical properties](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%89%A9%E7%90%86%E6%80%A7%E8%B4%A8&zhida_source=entity) of the trajectory to describe capability and behavioral reasonableness. Behaviors must align with intuition and human rules (e.g., autonomous vehicles operate on human-built lanes, and highway and urban scenarios differ in rules and thus in task structures), as well as human expectations (e.g., overly aggressive autonomous driving behavior is unacceptable to users, the Three Laws of Robotics, etc.) [[1]](#ref_1). This implies that even with interaction, the solution is not a rational game-theoretic equilibrium.
-   Achieving task generalization involves handling multiple scenarios and multiple tasks (e.g., autonomous driving covers parking, various urban intersections, and highways as distinct scenarios, while robots exhibit even more pronounced multi-task characteristics). In this case, the decision-planning algorithm must have task-scenario [coverage capability](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%A6%86%E7%9B%96%E8%83%BD%E5%8A%9B&zhida_source=entity)—how many target scenarios can be covered given certain computational resources. Most planners can only accomplish goals that meet certain conditions within limited resources. Motion planning itself is [NP-hard](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=NP-hard&zhida_source=entity), and the set of tasks does not appear to be countable, so complete coverage is inherently impossible; the only option is to shrink the scope of task-level "corner cases" as much as possible. In short, different tasks reveal more problem structures to exploit... And for many tasks, we have not even properly investigated their essence or even formulated the problem well.

### 1.5 Task-Level Robustness

When executing long-horizon tasks, not only do behaviors compose tasks, but tasks themselves also have scheduling relationships. During task execution, [disturbances](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=3&q=%E6%89%B0%E5%8A%A8&zhida_source=entity) acting on the agent and the environment affect not only the trajectory but also the completion of the tasks. At this point, decision-making and planning must continuously readjust the behavior sequence or even task scheduling in response to disturbance-induced changes, and temporarily integrate new subtasks, among other things. These are not merely interaction and game-theoretic issues—humans continuously experiment while also proactively avoiding risks, and in long-horizon tasks, this manifests as the branching and recombination of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and scenario trees.

## 2. From Feasibility to Optimality: Coarse-to-Fine, No Fixed Method

Given the task requirements above, and due to the inherent difficulty of the motion planning problem (high-dimensional, non-convex [combinatorial optimization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BB%84%E5%90%88%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity)), achieving feasibility and optimality relies on the "**coarse-to-fine**" hierarchical idea I have mentioned in my earlier articles.

[/en/blog/zhihu-708362997](/en/blog/zhihu-708362997)

Layering decouples the search for feasible solutions from that for optimal solutions by using different scales, and for subproblems at each scale, appropriate methods are chosen based on the problem's characteristics. In past articles, I simply divided motion planning into [global and local](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%A8%E5%B1%80%E4%B8%8E%E5%B1%80%E9%83%A8&zhida_source=entity) levels, but in reality, there are more details due to differences in problem scale and the stages where problems are best addressed. We can summarize some classic schemes for mobile robots in different environments. Nevertheless, for this lowest-level task, existing [algorithm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=5&q=%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) solutions have already matured considerably.

### 2.1 Global Path Planning - Local Smoothing - Trajectory Optimization

Front-end path planning relies on a global prior map and primarily considers [topological](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8B%93%E6%89%91&zhida_source=entity) road connectivity information to generate a geometric path, deliberately ignoring smoothness and dynamic feasibility to reduce complexity. However, feeding the path planning result directly as an initial guess into the back-end optimization yields poor quality, so a lightweight local path smoothing algorithm becomes highly useful at this stage. Such algorithms typically avoid search or optimization altogether, instead performing lightweight operations such as node removal and [interpolation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8F%92%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity), before passing the smoothed path as an initial guess to the back-end optimizer.

### (II) (Global Path Planning—) Dynamics-Based Path Planning—Trajectory Optimization

Sometimes global planning cannot reflect changes in the robot's nearby environment in real time, so the local planner also bears some responsibility for obstacle avoidance. In such cases, simple interpolation cannot satisfy obstacle-avoidance requirements, and dynamics-based path planning becomes relevant. Kinodynamic path planning satisfies dynamic constraints and possesses a certain obstacle-avoidance capability. However, as a predominantly global path planning method, once a trajectory is computed, it is difficult to also address time allocation, and dynamic limits may sometimes be violated. Local trajectory optimization methods further improve solution quality, considering not only dynamic constraints but also time parameterization, energy savings, actuator limits, and task-level requirements that give rise to task planning. Of course, global path planning is sometimes unnecessary as well.

### (III) Trajectory Generation—Trajectory Optimization

Sometimes the robot dynamics are relatively simple, allowing direct planning at the trajectory level; sometimes the robot's perception range is extremely limited and there is no global notion of the environment. In such cases, a simple approach (straight-line segments, ordinary polynomials, etc.) can be used to generate a simple trajectory (which, unlike a path, includes time parameterization). These are typically closed-form solutions to two-point boundary-value optimal control (BVOC) problems, and sometimes a near-optimal trajectory is generated via fast BVOC solvers. With boundary conditions specifying initial and goal poses, and intermediate conditions through waypoint poses (either manually specified or obtained from path planning), a certain degree of smoothness must also be satisfied.

### (IV) Global Motion Planning

The first three categories all rely on setting up various "global–local" and "path–trajectory" mechanisms before the final optimization problem, providing an initial solution for warm starting the backend optimization. This is essentially a form of problem hierarchy: the front-end outer loop and the back-end inner loop address different levels and difficulties of the problem. The fundamental purpose of the front-end warm start is to reduce the burden on the backend optimization while preventing the optimizer from falling into an excessively poor local minimum. If a sufficiently powerful optimization algorithm exists that is both global and real-time, then global optimization can be performed, solving the motion planning problem in a holistic manner.

## III. Simple Interaction

The interaction between a robot and its environment actually touches upon the essence of many robotics tasks. Environmental interaction involves modeling the environment (related to computational geometry), which is embedded in the information transfer from the perception layer to the planning layer.

### (I) Non-Contact

This type of non-contact interaction is built upon sensors, which is why I refer to it as weak interaction. Naturally, one faces the situation of limited sensor perception resources. Problems with limited conditions and resources are referred to as partially observable environments, where the exploration & exploitation (E&E) tradeoff is ever-present. A typical scenario is the unknown environment, where tasks usually involve exploration or simple point-to-point planning (with static or dynamic obstacle avoidance).

In theory, such partially observable problems can be approximately modeled using POMDP theory to provide conceptual or methodological guidance. Since online perception range is limited, the range over which reliable obstacle information (depth) can be obtained is also limited. Local re-planning must therefore be based on local maps. The key lies in the coupling between SLAM (the observer) as the upstream outer loop and the planner as the downstream inner loop. At the local level, coupling involves incorporating local localization information during planning, typically formulated as a Fisher information field that enters the planning cost; at the global level, planning must consider constructing loop closures for SLAM.

### (II) Contact

Contact-based interaction currently falls roughly into two categories: friction models and contact models. The former is common in autonomous driving and legged robotics, while the latter is unavoidable in foot-step planning for legged robot locomotion and in manipulator manipulation tasks.

For highly dynamic scenarios in autonomous driving (such as autonomous racing), friction must be exploited finely to pursue peak performance: approach the friction limit as closely as possible without exceeding it, to obtain maximum grip. Maintaining balance near the friction limit—that is, in a nearly slipping condition—is difficult. Moreover, over time, temperature rises and friction itself causes the friction coefficient to vary dynamically across space and time. Given our ignorance of the friction coefficient distribution and its trend of variation, the E&E balance here involves exploring the friction limit with dynamic adaptation. Humans can continuously sense friction changes through hearing and feedback from the steering wheel. Classical algorithms, however, can only operate slightly below the online-estimated limit. In this partially observable scenario, stochastic control laws (planning solutions) are not certainty-equivalent; using certainty-equivalent solutions yields only suboptimal results.

Contact models, in turn, are roughly divided into three types, each with its own characteristics and corresponding drawbacks. In many applications, the choice or improvement of the model must be tailored to task requirements—the goal being to simulate as fast as possible while ensuring that physical laws are correctly respected during the task.

## IV. Complex Interaction and Task Generalization

### (I) Consistency Issues

For motion planning algorithms, if consistency issues between modules are not handled well, optimality will inevitably be lost due to anomalous behavior. "Between modules" here refers both to the relationship between perception and decision/planning in the overall mobile robot algorithm, and to the relationship between the front end and back end within motion planning itself. Consistency implies change, and to such change there are naturally two responses: the "robust" approach of remaining unchanged in the face of change, and the "adaptive" approach of changing in response to change. These correspond respectively to what is currently called multimodal planning and re-planning—the former essentially generalizing to produce multiple options, and the latter using event triggering for appropriate adaptation.

**1. Dynamics Issues**

Regarding the front-end/back-end relationship within motion planning: since the front end's fundamental task is to provide topologically connected information in space, its results are not guaranteed to satisfy dynamics. This often leads to situations where the initial path or trajectory and the actual dynamically feasible optimal trajectory lie in different homotopy classes. Two solutions exist: topological trajectory planning, or incorporating dynamics-aware modules (kinodynamic path planning, smoothing, or trajectory generation).

**2. "Painting the Dragon" Issues**

These arise from temporal inconsistency in perception results.

This inconsistency may, on one hand, be a natural consequence of dynamic environments—time-varying environment information indeed causes time-varying topological information, and re-planning can also lead to poor consistency between successive trajectories, resulting in oscillatory behavior. On the other hand, even in static environments, such jumps can still occur. This inconsistency fundamentally stems from perception outputs that are not probabilistic but instead rigidly defined by certain rules. The end result is that perception outputs jump discontinuously over time.

In this regard, the most fundamental development would be a (stochastic) dynamic topology theory. However, such a theory—somewhat like combinatorial planning relative to planning algorithms—would only provide a complexity bound for the algorithm, with high practical computational difficulty. More operational, more technical problem-reduction techniques would be used for approximation. For example, topological trajectory planning attempts to plan multiple trajectories simultaneously.

In unknown environments, exploration strategies can also be attempted: sometimes the absence of a feasible solution is due to mapping errors caused by visual sensor noise. In such cases, a more aggressive approach can be taken—generating safe, short trajectories in the surrounding region to observe the environment and achieve coverage.

Along similar multimodal lines, the field of contingency planning has emerged. Handling scenario-based stochasticity in the evolution of scenes theoretically requires MDP and POMDP tools, but in practice the toolkit extends well beyond these. A common approach is to plan a trajectory tree rather than a single trajectory—or even to plan a trajectory tree for a multi-agent system. The branches of the tree represent different scenarios, and the branching process is essentially one of information increasing from sparse to rich. This naturally motivates the necessity of decision-making: in a practical sense, decisions choose between different homotopy classes, or equivalently, they enter different local minima; in an abstract sense, decisions select among multimodal divergences.

The root of the trajectory tree uses less information; additional information causes the tree to branch. Thus, the decision process proceeds from coarse to fine. At this point, decision-making combined with joint optimization can produce consistent results.

**3. Association Issues**

The "painting the dragon" problem just described concerns perception results varying in the time domain; correspondingly, perception results varying in the spatial domain are referred to as association. Spatial variation means that results from position sensors at different locations are inconsistent. These sensors may be on a single agent, or on different individuals in a cooperative swarm task.

How to handle consistency and accuracy well under sensor noise interference is key to achieving safe obstacle avoidance. This is especially true for optimization problems with hard constraints, where noise interference has a significant impact.

### (II) Complex Interaction Issues

First, consider cases where the inherent feasibility of the original solution is broken during interaction; two examples follow.

A common problem is the "frozen robot" problem. Specifically, this is a situation where a robot in a complex (dynamic) environment fails to plan continuously because no feasible solution exists. In this case, one typically needs to consider a new problem structure: dynamic obstacles are agents, and the behavior of other agents is also influenced by our own behavior. This interaction reflects fundamental multi-agent characteristics, touching on the core of some difficult problems in mixed-autonomy systems. Another type of problem concerns constraint relaxation in special circumstances. For instance, in extreme situations, relaxing different constraints represents different levels of risk; sometimes, when no feasible solution exists, one must find a "least violation" or "minimum risk" solution, which places higher demands on the formulation of the optimization problem.

Moreover, complex interaction often requires multiple systems to evaluate, predict, and game-theoretically interact over each other's behavior within a certain time window (including the past, present, and future). Clearly, such systems are inherently acausal and non-Markovian, requiring game-theoretic reasoning as well. Furthermore, complex interaction decisions are inherently uncertain, hence multimodal, and involve probability.

### (III) Other

Predictably, beyond the above, there are many other factors affecting complex tasks and generalization. Beyond non-Markovianity, beyond interactive game theory, the environment should offer richer information to be exploited. One important kind is semantic information.

At this level, planning algorithms truly transcend the motion planning layer and reach a new level: performing information transfer and inference in a latent space—what many refer to as the transition from "cerebellum" to "cerebrum." However, at present, too much work remains at the stage of using neural networks for function approximation in latent spaces, without finding suitable structures for representation. For semantic information, I personally believe that graph structures are a potentially effective approach. One would naturally need to introduce graph-based state spaces, and consequently, directions such as graph neural networks that have cooled off may well experience a resurgence in the future.

This is also a direction I have attempted to explore in some of my previous articles; I believe that the so-called embodied intelligence, which is currently more popular in manipulation, should focus on this type of task.

Looking at the current state of affairs, although "planning as inference" enjoyed a few years of popularity in the past, there is still no good formulation. The target distribution remains a major problem, though progress in black-box optimization is gradually addressing these issues.

## V. Real-Time Performance

### (I) Static Known Environments

If the environment is static and known, then real-time performance is not of paramount importance; many environmental quantities can be precomputed and pre-stored offline. In this case, the online algorithm merely serves to relieve offline pressure (for example, quantities estimated offline need not be perfectly accurate, since online robustness can absorb the slack). Conversely, offline computation also relieves the pressure on the online stage: if the entire problem were left to online computation or storage, the requirements would be prohibitive, so the problem naturally decouples across time scales (a long-horizon offline method first produces a reasonable solution, and online, the offline result serves as a rough guide, providing pruning effects). The two are complementary. This can be clearly observed in agents such as AlphaGo and AlphaZero, which employ deep reinforcement learning: an offline value function is trained from existing game data, and online, MCTS is used for search. Because board games fully conform to static rule-based environments with only a few-player game requirement, and the criteria for evaluating a position are rather fuzzy, a value function serving as a prior for the online algorithm proves highly effective.

### (2) Static Unknown Environments

In static unknown environments, the lack of prior knowledge means that, if a solution exists, the worst case is visiting every reachable state—only after exhaustive traversal can planning terminate. A slightly better approach is to explore a bit more, just enough for the observer to satisfy the observability condition, which outperforms exhaustive traversal. The key issue becomes how to quickly obtain a solution; otherwise, the agent will freeze. Since no prior environmental information is available, offline computation cannot amortize much of the environmental complexity. What can be amortized instead is some of the task-level difficulty. For example, in navigation, planners within the local planning horizon must collect localization information while avoiding obstacles; in manipulation, challenges arise from perceptual occlusion, the non-Markovian nature of [state estimation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1&zhida_source=entity) (observers), and inaccuracies in modeling contact friction. All of these create strong coupling between planning and perception-localization.

Many difficulties emerge here, the simplest being that the intrinsic complexity of the problem formulation itself increases. In this setting, offline work ideally serves as a form of [heuristic search](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%90%AF%E5%8F%91%E5%BC%8F%E6%90%9C%E7%B4%A2&zhida_source=entity): through offline training, the system can find a feasible solution faster online—or even an infeasible one that provides some acceleration online—because, especially for manipulation problems with contact-rich interactions and multi-task objectives, defining clear evaluation criteria for behavior quality is also difficult. Consider that in the robotic arm domain, many tasks have traditionally relied on demonstration or sampling-based methods (e.g., RRT variants); if all tasks were delegated to online search-optimization, real-time performance would be a major hurdle.

### (3) Dynamic Environments

In dynamic environments, the moving obstacles are typically other agents, and the task is usually navigation, tracking, or pursuit-evasion—these are merely different game-theoretic models (if the dynamic obstacles were non-agentic, randomly moving objects, the planning difficulty, especially real-time requirements, would be even higher). Here, the primary bottleneck shifts to interactive game-theoretic reasoning among agents. In dynamic environments, the significance of offline computation abruptly diminishes. The reason is that offline algorithms require a "digital twin" of the real environment (i.e., a [simulator](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%BF%E7%9C%9F%E5%99%A8&zhida_source=entity)), but the actual interactive game dynamics are currently difficult to embed in simulation. This is not merely a matter of solving a multi-player game; it also involves, at least potentially, human psychological expectations (e.g., overly aggressive driving behavior is unacceptable to users), agents not fully adhering to game-theoretic solutions (let alone equilibrium solutions), and various irrational behaviors. Thus, offline computation cannot amortize difficulties in environmental representation, nor can it amortize task-level difficulties. Fortunately, current planning algorithms in dynamic environments no longer pursue multi-task objectives either (for household robots, due to low-speed scenarios, navigation, whole-body obstacle avoidance, and manipulation can be tentatively treated as decoupled).

## V. Summary

Overall, motion planning has evolved toward greater intelligence—from the motion itself, to interactions during motion, to learning to reason. Higher-level tasks may fundamentally reshape or overturn lower-level ones, but this is invariably accompanied by deeper exploration of task scenarios and advances in mathematical formulation. For a sequential decision-making problem, we have progressed from [graph search](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9B%BE%E6%90%9C%E7%B4%A2&zhida_source=entity), to convex MPC, to non-convex optimization—with solution methods evolving from single shooting to multiple shooting—and further to game theory, zeroth-order optimization, and a range of related branches of [applied mathematics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=253212430&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BA%94%E7%94%A8%E6%95%B0%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) (as seen in the opening of the article below). Along the way, our understanding of problem structure has become clearer, and our grasp of intelligence more concrete. Standing where we are today, we can readily look toward the future.
