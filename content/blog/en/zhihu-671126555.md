## History of Control Theory Development

1. The Naive Period:

- Pre-17th century and earlier: various simple automatic control devices from ancient China and abroad, omitted (easy to look up), e.g., Aristotle, Archimedes, etc.;
- 1687: Newton published *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, applying axiomatic-deductive mathematical systems to many aspects of natural science, establishing systematic calculus-based modeling ideas;
- 1765: Watt improved the steam engine and invented the centrifugal governor (details also findable on Zhihu);

2. The Germination Period

- 1770–1780: Lagrange and Laplace constructed positive definite functions to analyze system stability;
- 1868: Maxwell studied the problems Watt encountered. Due to advances in manufacturing, damping in steam engines decreased, making governors more sensitive but prone to unstable runaway behavior and reduced reliability; the fix was to increase damping. Maxwell used modeling to derive stability conditions for the system, becoming the first to systematically analyze the stability of feedback control;

3. Classical Control

Frequency-domain analysis is the core of classical control theory, supplemented by root locus and classical time-domain analysis.

- 1877: Routh proposed an algebraic criterion for determining positive real-part roots of polynomial equations, later known as the Routh criterion;
- 1886: Poincaré took a qualitative, geometric approach to the stability of planar dynamical systems, emerging from ordinary differential equations and founding the mathematical branch of dynamical systems;
- 1892: Lyapunov published *The General Problem of the Stability of Motion*, introducing Lyapunov stability and his first and second methods;
- 1898: Hurwitz gave the Hurwitz zero theorem, leading to a root criterion for equations—the Hurwitz criterion;
- 1932: Nyquist proposed a frequency-domain stability criterion while working at AT&T and Bell Labs; during this period, frequency-domain analysis tools developed rapidly and became the core of classical control theory;
- 1940: Bode redrew the Nyquist plot using logarithmic coordinates to obtain the Bode plot, and correspondingly proposed phase margin and stability criteria for minimum-phase systems; these results, being intuitive, were more convenient for engineering applications;
- 1948: Wiener published *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*, introducing the fundamental concepts of cybernetics and marking its birth;
- 1954: Tsien Hsue-shen published *Engineering Cybernetics*, the first book to systematically summarize control theory in abstract engineering problems, highlighting the difference between cybernetics and engineering control theory. It was the first to use "engineering cybernetics" to describe the control theories, concepts, and methods applied in engineering design practice. It was subsequently translated into Russian (1956), German (1957), and Chinese (1958), among others.

4. Modern Control

The development of aerospace was a major driving force. State-space methods, dynamic programming, the maximum principle, and the Kalman filter are the core of modern control.

- 1957: Bellman published *Dynamic Programming*, converting optimization problems over multi-stage decision processes into a sequence of single-stage problems, solved sequentially using inter-stage relationships. This gave rise to the idea that, under certain conditions, global optimality implies local optimality. This even later led to the theory of viscosity solutions for nonlinear PDEs, for which Lions received the Fields Medal;
- 1958: Pontryagin proposed the maximum principle, providing necessary conditions and a synthesis method for optimal controls and trajectories of performance indices when initial states, target states, and control variables are constrained;
- 1958–1960: Swerling and Kalman independently published papers proposing the Kalman filter; after S. Schmidt first implemented it, Kalman applied it to the Apollo spacecraft navigation system and subsequently published a paper. This is an algorithm that uses linear system state equations and input–output observation data to perform optimal state estimation. It provides a recursive minimum-variance estimate for the state of nonstationary stochastic systems;
- 1961: Pontryagin published *The Mathematical Theory of Optimal Processes*, which also stimulated the development of modern calculus of variations and differential games; after introducing randomness, it further led to theories of backward and forward–backward stochastic PDEs;
- Year uncertain: LQ (linear quadratic) optimal control, solving controllability and quadratic optimal control problems for finite-dimensional linear systems. Its nonlinear generalizations led to theories such as Sussmann's orbit theorem and sub-Riemannian geometry in several complex variables (see J. Merker and E. Porten's long survey *Holomorphic Extension of CR Functions, Envelopes of Holomorphy, and Removable Singularities*, 2007, which has a chapter on Sussmann's orbit theorem).

5. Contemporary Control

The modern control theory above mainly targeted relatively simple deterministic finite-dimensional linear systems. Since the 1970s, however, control theory has developed rapidly, with a steady stream of new results, methods, and ideas.

- Adaptive control, traceable back to the 1950s;
- Robust control: research on robustness originated in 1981, when J. Doyle, then a graduate student, published his first paper proving that an LQG system composed of LQR and KF has no robustness whatsoever. From then on, (linear) robust control developed;
- Model predictive control (MPC), originating in the 1970s, can be viewed as a numerical (receding-horizon) optimal control method. It first emerged in the chemical industry (where process control has large time constants and slow dynamics), and later, with advances in computing, it became possible to solve large-scale optimization problems at high frequency, bringing MPC into robotics;
- Distributed parameter system control;
- Stochastic system control;
- Nonlinear system control;
- Intelligent control

These developments in control theory have relied on corresponding advances in mathematics, such as PDE theory, stochastic differential equations and stochastic analysis, dynamical systems, and nonlinear analysis.

They have also relied on corresponding advances in engineering applications; without these applications, theory would not have been driven forward. For example, the combination of optimal control theory and artificial intelligence gave rise to reinforcement learning. Aerospace, robotics—including autonomous driving and drones—systems with highly nonlinear or strongly coupled complexities that demand high control performance and even bear on major production and life safety, push us to develop more and more reliable nonlinear control theories.
