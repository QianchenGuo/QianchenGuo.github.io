## Optimization and Optimal Control

Alright, let's get back on track. Let me first talk about the direction with the most gains, since this is also my research area—motion planning—and most likely my future career path, so I've devoted the most effort to it. Moreover, this direction is also the necessary path toward noncausal control, as opposed to feedback control.

### (I) Optimization Theory

This year I continued working through part of the foundational optimization theory I hadn't finished in 2024. On the main track, I carefully finished reading **[Yinyu Ye](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=762201659&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%B6%E8%8D%AB%E5%AE%87&zhida_source=entity)'s *Linear and Nonlinear Programming***, and completed **[Dr. Zhepei Wang](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=762201659&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=%E6%B1%AA%E5%93%B2%E5%9F%B9&zhida_source=entity)'s "Numerical Optimization in Robotics"** course. ### (II) Optimal Control—The Grand Unification!

Finally, onto the actual control-related content! On this front, my main track was completing **[CMU](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=762201659&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=CMU&zhida_source=entity)'s Zach's "Optimal Control and Reinforcement Learning"**. I was quite excited while studying it, though I also noticed that the course content seemed a bit "abbreviated" [\[12\]](#ref_12). I then turned to Zhihu again, and after struggling painfully through the platform, I found that the Chinese internet is somewhat lacking in optimal control theory, while solution methods are all over the place. Some people just parrot content from Zach's course, and no matter how much you look around, you can't piece together a systematic compendium of solution methods. That bothered me, so I began compiling and organizing, and eventually made some progress, forming a preliminary systematic framework. Later, through a stroke of luck and guidance from some experts, I discovered **Moritz's courses and lecture notes**, which were a treasure trove (though I still haven't finished them).

At the same time, I started reading **Bertsekas's *Reinforcement Learning and Optimal Control***. Compared to his other work [\[13\]](#ref_13), this book is much more concise; if you don't read too carefully, you can easily grasp the main thread, and sometimes I could read half a chapter or a full chapter in one sitting. I mainly studied this book to understand the DP and approximate DP frameworks for solving optimal control problems, and incidentally to understand the success of the [MPC framework](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=762201659&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=MPC%E6%A1%86%E6%9E%B6&zhida_source=entity) from a DP perspective—namely, multi-step lookahead and policy rollout.

In the end, consolidating everything, I [\[14\]](#ref_14) produced a **systematic summary of optimal control and its computational methods**. I'll continue updating it later; for now let me give a rough overview. Broadly, the methods can be divided into three categories: indirect methods, dynamic programming (DP), and direct methods.

**Indirect methods** optimize first and discretize second. They express the KKT conditions through Pontryagin's maximum principle, reducing the problem to a boundary value problem. For that BVP, simple cases admit analytical solutions, while complex cases can be solved numerically via shooting methods—this is the indirect shooting method. Of course, Pontryagin's maximum principle itself is an important milestone in the development of the calculus of variations, introducing the needle variation technique.

**DP** is based on the [Bellman optimality principle](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=762201659&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=Bellman%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E5%8E%9F%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity). Leveraging the Markov property yields a recursion, from which value iteration and policy iteration naturally follow. Approximate value iteration simplifies the problem by approximating the cost-to-go, approximating the expectation, and approximating the min. Classic cost-to-go approximations can be achieved through problem approximation, policy rollout (the basic idea behind MPC), parametric approximation, and aggregation. Typical [DRL](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=762201659&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=DRL&zhida_source=entity) uses DNNs as tools for parametric approximation. Meanwhile, a host of search-based and sampling-based algorithms in path planning can each be viewed as finite-horizon deterministic problems on finite/countable state spaces with finite control spaces, formed by aggregating the problem or implicitly constructing it through random sampling... For the aggregation view, A\* is naturally a special cost-to-go approximation method. The so-called kinodynamic path planning is nothing more than an attempt to aggregate one side of the state/control space.

**Direct methods** discretize first and then treat the problem itself as a nonlinear optimization problem with equality and inequality constraints, possibly using dynamics rollout to compress the problem, thereby converting a simultaneous strategy into a sequential one. These different discretization strategies naturally yield the three main approaches: single shooting, multiple shooting, and collocation.

The first two are collectively called direct shooting methods: ultimately, inequality constraints are handled by active set, gradient projection, penalty function, interior point methods, or ALM and ADMM. Then you're faced with some nonlinear system of equations—most likely a primal-dual KKT system or the KKT system of some subproblem—and you solve Newton steps [\[15\]](#ref_15), at which point you encounter block-diagonal sparse structures. These sparse structures can be handled by sparse Gaussian elimination, Riccati recursion, sparse Cholesky, Schur complement recursion, and various other methods. A special case is that the indirect shooting method turns out to be precisely a simplified version (without constraints) of the direct shooting method. And DDP/iLQR, viewed from a DP perspective, turns out to be an explicit Gaussian elimination procedure on a KKT system with only equality constraints, yielding a Riccati recursion; other sparse processing techniques would achieve the same effect.

As for **direct collocation**, together with its important subclass, the shape method: the former originates from spectral methods in numerical ODEs, using basis functions to represent the state and control input locally in a piecewise manner, then converting the original constraints into collocation-point-related constraints. The problem dimension can be adjusted by the number of collocation points, ultimately yielding an extremely sparse [NLP](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=762201659&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=NLP&zhida_source=entity). The shape method, also known as polynomial trajectory optimization, applies when globally parametrizing the trajectory with spline curves incurs little performance loss—in that case, one can directly use global parametrization, ultimately obtaining a dense optimization over control points. This is typically used for differentially flat systems whose inverse dynamics are explicit; otherwise, polynomial-parametrized trajectories can only yield suboptimal solutions.

Additionally, within direct methods there is a special class: **derivative-free optimization**. These methods are essentially based on sampling in state space or control space for optimization. Their value lies in enabling more powerful Lyapunov function construction, and even more powerful generators of task-level behavior (see later).

Over the course of the year, as my understanding of control deepened, my understanding of motion planning also kept rising. At the beginning of the year, I consolidated some scattered things I'd read on Zhihu, together with what I'd systematically studied in 2024 on "mobile robot motion planning" and some piecemeal reading of LaValle, into the following article.

Later, I began advancing the construction of my motion planning **"the rising sea"** [\[16\]](#ref_16), with the intent of not being confined by any particular course or book, but rather starting entirely from existing planning frameworks, centered on my own understanding and reflection. In each case I strive to dissect the essential problems and, through deeper understanding, attempt to push forward the resolution of hard problems. Furthermore, maintaining a systematic mindset, I organize the current solutions surrounding hard problems from a macroscopic perspective. In the end, I produced this column:

[/en/blog/zhihu-1922973164197122321](/en/blog/zhihu-1922973164197122321)

Among other things, I attempted to extend general motion planning to probability distributions, aiming for better performance in multimodal problems or probabilistic systems.

[/en/blog/zhihu-29474410388](/en/blog/zhihu-29474410388)

There, I grasped two core points. First, when the structure does not involve more complex issues (robustness, task-level considerations, etc.), sparsity is the most essential element for real-time motion planning. In deterministic systems, after carefully formulating the problem, sparsity manifests as the Riccati recursion brought by the Markov property; stochastic systems are more flexible—using probabilistic graphical models, the problem's sparsity is translated into graph structure, which also handles non-Markov systems naturally. Second, for complex cost optimization, one can ride the wave of derivative-free optimization to achieve powerful solutions.

The former leads to **planning as inference**: the motion planning problem, as an optimal sequential decision problem, becomes a statistical inference problem of "message passing" on a factor graph, which pushed me to continue learning probabilistic inference. This technique even allows front-end/back-end coupling, making it a very powerful framework. The latter also shows tremendous promise, because in essence many problems in general control stem from the difficulty of constructing Lyapunov functions corresponding to desired behaviors, and MPC provides a method of "implicitly" constructing them via optimization [\[17\]](#ref_17)—that is, allowing coarser cost-to-go approximations as described above. In a certain sense, the stronger one's ability to optimize nonconvex, nonsmooth, even non-Markov problems, the more powerful the "implicit" construction methods become. Furthermore, this grants us greater capability for complex task-level planning—I believe this is where the true value of derivative-free optimization lies.

At the same time, I also studied **Wenchao Ding's "Prediction and Decision Planning for Autonomous Driving"** course, moving from robotic motion planning to understand the decision planning side of autonomous driving. I learned about common concepts in autonomous driving planning such as spatiotemporal separation/joint formulation and longitudinal-lateral decoupling/coupling. Subsequently, I also came to the industry for an internship and, along with casual discussions in a chat group (see later), experienced the unevenness of the industry. Taking advantage of this opportunity, I also sorted out the different decision-planning architectures in the autonomous driving industry and offered some pointed critiques of Apollo—all of which can be turned into articles later [\[18\]](#ref_18).

## The Probabilistic World

In last year's answer, I already mentioned that in the confluence of sequential Bayes theory and optimal estimation, and in studying optimal control and motion planning, I naturally began to think about how to solve multimodal problems—which essentially seems to call for probabilistic results. At that point, a voice seemed to guide me naturally: planning as inference.

So I began studying probabilistic inference. I first looked into various **information divergences and metrics**, studied **variational inference**, and then moved on to information geometry, SVGD, and [Wasserstein geometry](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=762201659&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=Wasserstein%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity). I started reading **Amari's *Information Geometry and Its Applications***, but to this day I still haven't finished it in bits and pieces. I attempted to learn **SVGD**, which combines MCMC particle methods with variational inference methods. But the geometry behind it is quite deep: the Stein operator is actually related to the infinitesimal generator of the Langevin dynamics operator semigroup; one could of course use other SDE generators, but the special feature of the Langevin–Stein operator is that its corresponding Stein discrepancy can be kernelized conveniently by introducing an RKHS. Then I finished **Otto's short course at MPI, "Wasserstein Gradient Flow"** [\[19\]](#ref_19), studied **Otto Calculus** [\[20\]](#ref_20), and also "reviewed" Riemannian geometry. Later, I'd like to explore more Wasserstein geometry, especially to understand Wuchen Li's work—how to connect the two probabilistic geometries, and how to introduce phase space on the Wasserstein manifold to obtain accelerated algorithms.

These topics naturally connect with derivative-free optimization. However, these nonparametric methods all fix the target distribution, which is fundamentally different from the nonparametric approach in IGO. At present, the latter is more applicable to the problems I'm currently facing.

## Miscellaneous Bits and Pieces

### (I) What Else Can We Dig Out of the Control-Observation Duality?

The conclusion is that there isn't much, haha. I started to get triggered just seeing this duality[\[22\]](#ref_22). After poking around, I found Todorov's 2008 paper[\[23\]](#ref_23). After reading it, my first impression was that I had underestimated the control-observation duality—it turns out that extending the linear quadratic problem to more general settings requires extra conditions. This led me to explore **linearly solvable systems**, which is the most general setting where the duality can be extended with structure. But if we ignore the internal structure and just look at the optimal control problem from the surface, as long as we have similar cost forms and constraints (dynamics, blah blah), we can obtain solutions of the same type. So in a sense, this duality doesn't have much deeper content to it (?) [\[24\]](#ref_24). This is why the Kalman filter has so many interpretations[\[25\]](#ref_25)—essentially because it is just too special, and such a special case can be explained from any angle. Furthermore, the sigma-point filters[\[26\]](#ref_26) and particle filters that followed seem to be, in essence, zero-order optimization-based filtering methods, especially the latter, which is essentially MCMC—a zero-order optimization method[\[27\]](#ref_27).

BTW, linearly solvable systems are quite interesting. Their corresponding [HJB equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=762201659&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=HJB%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity)—a backward nonlinear PDE—can be naturally linearized through an exponential transformation to yield a linear parabolic PDE, and then we can use the Feynman-Kac formula to obtain an explicit solution—a solution in terms of trajectory cost (known as path integral). This is one angle that leads to path integral control[\[28\]](#ref_28). And I can boldly conjecture that the reason this bound holds in linearly solvable systems with so many different perspectives is that the cost function takes the form of "potential energy + some quadratic term," which shares the same symplectic geometric structure as Newtonian mechanics.

### (II) Noncausal Control—The Future of Control

I have to admit, this year I paid relatively little attention to feedback control and devoted most of my effort to predictive control. The so-called noncausal control refers to what I mentioned in last year's article:

[/en/blog/zhihu-698176373](/en/blog/zhihu-698176373)

Namely, having both the predictive capability of predictive control or planning, and the robustness of feedback control. I have been pursuing this direction all year long. This is also the original intention behind my series "**Motion Planning: The Rising Sea**"—to keep digging into problem structures and find solutions to the core problem or simplified versions of it.

From the perspective of task-level planning, we can attempt to construct interaction games in certain special tasks to model behavior more reasonably. At the same time, we should also try to map topological or other spatial relations to behaviors—this is achievable in some applications—so that we can use control theory to construct more sensible costs for desired behaviors. Additionally, we have powerful optimization tools; optimization is currently the most accessible way to handle task-level planning, after all, explicitly constructing Lyapunov functions remains out of reach (or rather, it's an impossible task unless we combine predictive control with feedback control—otherwise we would essentially be giving feedback control predictive capabilities, and then the problem would be solved).

But how much further can we go? Looking around, I find myself surrounded by "data-driven," "learning-based," "[VLA](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=762201659&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=VLA&zhida_source=entity)," and "large models." People hype up Scaling Law and then try to issue warnings, but it seems nobody is listening. In this restless, explosive, short-sighted era where everyone is frantic and on edge, I sense a crisis. All I can do is keep my own counsel, like a moral guardian patiently waiting for the age of science to arrive, feeling that the road ahead is still long and arduous...
