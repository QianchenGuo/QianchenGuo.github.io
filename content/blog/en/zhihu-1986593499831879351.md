Just finished answering the 2025 control-circle question of the year, and on a whim, I decided to consolidate some of the classic, oft-repeated topics. First, as everyone knows, the Kalman filter can be viewed from many angles, and there has been a lot of debate about this point. I also felt dissatisfied with my previous articles, so I'm going to synthesize several of my earlier posts along with some good articles on Zhihu to make this as comprehensive as possible (and **kill this topic once and for all 😜**). I'll also share some of my new insights.

Let me start with the big picture: the reason the Kalman filter has so many interpretations is simply because it's so special. It's as common as QP in optimization, PID in control, or [LQR](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity) in [optimal control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity). The simpler such things are, the more extensions they have across different fields.

## 1. Optimal Estimation

Minimum variance estimation, [linear minimum variance estimation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%9C%80%E5%B0%8F%E6%96%B9%E5%B7%AE%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1&zhida_source=entity), maximum likelihood estimation, [maximum a posteriori estimation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9E%81%E5%A4%A7%E5%90%8E%E9%AA%8C%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1&zhida_source=entity), and Bayes estimation can be equivalent under certain conditions. When these five estimation methods are applied to a first-order Markov [state-space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) model, with certain conditions and assumptions, the Kalman filter can be derived from five perspectives within the probabilistic framework. Note, however, that the Gaussian assumption is not necessary—[white noise](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%99%BD%E5%99%AA%E5%A3%B0&zhida_source=entity) is what matters. ## 2. Probabilistic Graphical Models

### (1) Bayes Networks

In fact, if we simply draw the Bayes network corresponding to the Bayes-filter-form Kalman filter from Section 1, we find that we obtain a chain-like hidden Markov model.

BTW, the so-called Markov model is essentially a sliding window of size one. Due to its [covariance](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%8F%E6%96%B9%E5%B7%AE&zhida_source=entity) being recursively maintained automatically, the state is updated only once based on the independence assumption. So it is fundamentally memoryless: each step's observation of a [landmark](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B7%AF%E6%A0%87%E7%82%B9&zhida_source=entity) is "new," and there's no need for explicit [marginalization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BE%B9%E7%95%8C%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) (unlike MSCKF). Furthermore, under the Gaussian assumption, if we set the window to one and use single-step Gauss-Newton with marginalization, the optimization becomes equivalent to the extended Kalman filter.

### (2) Factor Graphs

A factor graph specifically refers to a bipartite probabilistic graphical model that uses independent measurement assumptions. States (landmarks, poses, etc.) serve as nodes for the [quantities to be estimated](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity), connected by [probability distributions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A6%82%E7%8E%87%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83&zhida_source=entity) as factor constraints. A factor graph, once fully marginalized, becomes a Bayes network, and every Bayes network can be represented as a factor graph (but not vice versa). Factor graphs separate variables from probability distributions, making them more flexible. Clearly, factors need not be Gaussian; the Gaussian assumption is only made to accelerate computation, turning the batch MAP into a [weighted least squares](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A0%E6%9D%83%E6%9C%80%E5%B0%8F%E4%BA%8C%E4%B9%98&zhida_source=entity) problem. If a Gaussian prior is assumed, then MLE is the same (see the article in Section 1).

Factor graph inference benefits from the Gaussian assumption to obtain a Gaussian [approximate posterior distribution](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%91%E4%BC%BC%E5%90%8E%E9%AA%8C%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83&zhida_source=entity). Factor graph optimization essentially reduces to [nonlinear least squares](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%9C%80%E5%B0%8F%E4%BA%8C%E4%B9%98&zhida_source=entity); the key is also to obtain a sparse structure via the Gaussian + independence assumptions, then use better ordering and elimination methods to accelerate the solution of [linear systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B%E7%BB%84&zhida_source=entity) for the MAP estimate. A factor graph can be seen as a special Bayes filter with incremental inference and speedup properties; the Bayes filtering approach, by contrast, makes strong assumptions but can also infer the posterior distribution rather than just the MAP.

The Kalman filter is a recursive [least squares](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=268044291&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E5%B0%8F%E4%BA%8C%E4%B9%98%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) method, while factor graph optimization is large-scale sparse least squares. For a loop-free hidden Markov model with fully marginalized recursive form, factor graph optimization degenerates into the Kalman filter.

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/661895443?share_code=BlgNLSpcqm4M&utm_psn=1988386395849524236](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/661895443?share_code=BlgNLSpcqm4M&utm_psn=1988386395849524236)

### (iii) $H_2$ Filtering (Optimal $L_2$ Gain)

Consider $G(s)$ as the transfer function from the system disturbance to the estimation error; let $k(t), t \geq 0$ be the system's impulse response. Let the disturbance $X(t), -\infty < t < +\infty$ be a zero-mean, mean-square continuous stationary stochastic process. Assuming the system is asymptotically stable, we first compute the correlation function of the estimation error output $\{ Y(t), -\infty < t < +\infty \}$:

$$
\begin{aligned} B_Y(\tau) &= E[Y(t+\tau)Y(t)] \\ &= E\left[ \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} X(t+\tau-\eta)k(\eta)d\eta \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} X(t-\lambda)k(\lambda)d\lambda \right] \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} k(\lambda)k(\eta) B_X(\tau+\lambda-\eta) \, d\lambda d\eta \end{aligned}
$$

By the Wiener–Khinchin theorem, the power spectral density is given by

$$
\begin{aligned} S_Y(\omega) &= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} B_Y(\tau) e^{-j\omega\tau} d\tau \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} k(\lambda)k(\eta) B_X(\lambda+\tau-\eta) e^{-j\omega\tau} \, d\lambda d\eta d\tau \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} k(\lambda) e^{j\omega\lambda} d\lambda \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} k(\eta) e^{-j\omega\eta} d\eta \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} B_X(\lambda+\tau-\eta) e^{-j\omega(\tau+\lambda-\eta)} d\tau \end{aligned}
$$

Letting $\lambda+\tau-\eta=\tau^*$, the third integral on the right-hand side becomes $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} B_X(\tau^*) e^{-j\omega\tau^*} d\tau^* = S_X(\omega)$, while the first and second integrals are respectively

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} k(\lambda) e^{j\omega\lambda} d\lambda = G(-j\omega),\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} k(\eta) e^{-j\omega\eta} d\eta = G(j\omega)
$$

Substituting these results yields $S_Y(\omega) = G(-j\omega) G(j\omega) S_X(\omega) = |G(j\omega)|^2 S_X(\omega)$. The estimation error variance is:

$$
\sigma_Y^2 = E[Y^2(t)] = B_Y(0) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} S_Y(\omega) d\omega = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} |G(j\omega)|^2 S_X(\omega) d\omega
$$

When the disturbance is white noise, minimum-variance estimation is equivalent to minimizing the $H_2$ norm (which, for stable systems, coincides with the frequency-domain $L_2$ norm). Therefore, a Kalman filter that achieves minimum-variance estimation also possesses the optimal $L_2$ gain, or equivalently, is an $H_2$ filter.

## VII. On the Deterministic vs. Stochastic Debate

### (i) The Kalman Filter

Looking back at history, when Kalman published *A New Approach to Linear Filtering and Prediction Problems* in 1960, the filter was derived using minimum mean-square error and orthogonal projection methods, without heavily relying on stochastic or Bayesian perspectives. It was not until 1964, when Yu-Chi Ho published *A Bayesian Approach to Problems in Stochastic Estimation and Control*, that the Bayesian framework was formally integrated with the Kalman filter.

The debate essentially centers on deterministic versus probabilistic frameworks. Let me settle this issue by explaining the Kalman filter **bottom-up** thoroughly. From the discussion in Perspective 3, we can see that in essence:

1. In terms of stability and disturbance rejection, the Kalman filter is no different from the Luenberger observer;

2. In terms of least-squares estimation error, the Kalman filter employs an optimal time-varying gain compared to the Luenberger observer;

3. Furthermore, the least squares in Section III above was not weighted—or rather, only a single norm was written. If that norm is explicitly a weighted norm rather than a simple 

$$
L^2
$$

 norm, then refer to the following two articles:

[/en/blog/zhihu-694145140](/en/blog/zhihu-694145140)

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/42390886](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/42390886)

Note that the weighting matrices must satisfy the persistent excitation condition together with the uniform observability condition (which can be relaxed to detectability) to guarantee stability (the unweighted case 

$$
Q=I,U=O
$$

 trivially satisfies these).

Now, we finally begin to venture into the probabilistic framework. The most purist probabilistic framework models the system with an SDE, but for simplicity, a discrete-time model is often used instead (as in the models in Sections I or II). For the SDE framework, see:

[https://www.zhihu.com/question/47559783/answer/1992277654385210743?share_code=1iQgg3d00WbzH&utm_psn=1992955301578692414](https://www.zhihu.com/question/47559783/answer/1992277654385210743?share_code=1iQgg3d00WbzH&utm_psn=1992955301578692414)

In the weighted least-squares formulation, we encounter the weighting matrices $(Q,U)$: the former weights the output error, while the latter weights a slack term in the observer dynamics. This slack term represents the fact that the estimate $\hat x$ cannot evolve exactly according to $\dot x=Ax$; essentially, $\dot x=Ax+\delta$, and the slack variable introduces a quadratic penalty on the violation. After computation, we find that this slack term is precisely the output-error linear feedback term in the Kalman filter. Now, if we assign stochastic meaning to this slack term $\delta$ and to the additive term $n$ in the measurement equation, we obtain:

4. The slack term becomes process noise, and the additive term becomes measurement noise. For white noise, we can prove that the Kalman filter achieves linear minimum-variance estimation (note that least squares and minimum variance are not the same). If we further impose Gaussian white noise, then the Kalman filter achieves minimum-variance estimation (which also corresponds to the optimal $L_2$ gain). Meanwhile, according to Section I, under Gaussian conditions, minimum variance, linear minimum variance, and maximum a posteriori estimation are all equivalent. At this point, our $(Q,U)$ becomes the more familiar $(R,Q)$, acquiring the additional interpretation of intensity matrices (or covariance matrices in the Gaussian case). Moreover, the same procedure generalizes straightforwardly to nonlinear systems with an additive perturbation term of a certain form; see this answer:

[https://www.zhihu.com/question/641137647/answer/3406204532](https://www.zhihu.com/question/641137647/answer/3406204532)

5. Thus, in step 4, by restricting certain terms and imposing additional conditions, we arrive at the probabilistic framework. The stability conditions must then also change from Lyapunov asymptotic stability in the uniformly convergent sense to Lyapunov asymptotic stability in the mean-square sense. This is the fundamental reason why, in steps 1–3, we have disturbance rejection for *bounded* slack and additive terms, yet in step 4 we find that white/Gaussian white noise can be unbounded while the entire framework remains sound. At this point, convergence in the mean-square sense, together with exponential decay guaranteed by large deviation theory, ensures everything remains well-posed;

6. For colored noise with low-frequency components, the essential issue is its strong temporal correlation—that is, the noise itself should be modeled with its own dynamics. We typically model it as Markovian, which is commonly referred to as a shaping filter. Clearly, the persistent excitation condition plus uniform observability that hold for the original system do not necessarily hold for the augmented system consisting of the original system plus the colored-noise dynamics. This is the essential reason why the system may no longer remain stable after adding colored noise (and it has nothing to do with the probabilistic characteristics of the colored noise itself). Recalling undergraduate modern control theory, even if we perform an observability decomposition and design a filter for the observable subsystem, the system dynamics will be coupled through the unobservable part, so the observer dynamics are not at all of the form written above... If that unobservable part is itself unstable, the conclusion diverges even further;

7. For non-Gaussian noise, based on the above analysis, stability conditions are unaffected, but the gain becomes suboptimal (degrading from minimum-variance estimation to linear minimum-variance estimation). If one still wishes to approximate the optimal gain, more substantial changes to the optimization cost are required, and consequently we no longer obtain the recursive Riccati equation form. Some works attempt patchwork improvements to the equations, but these do not address the fundamental issue;

8. It should also be noted that the above discussion concerns theoretical results. Practical engineering implementation must also account for various numerical errors, device-related calibration, and modeling approximations.

### (ii) Extension of the Debate

Interestingly, in the field of state estimation/observation, there is considerable divergence between the deterministic nonlinear control community and the stochastic probabilistic framework. This divergence is particularly prominent in state estimation/observation. Historically, early applications of state estimation were in radar tracking and navigation, where random noise in the data was (what they perceived as) the primary challenge, giving rise to a series of filtering theories centered on probability and statistics. In contrast, observers in the control community served predominantly deterministic electromechanical systems—especially in motion control and motor-related fields—where the central issue was the observability of the system itself. In terms of technical paradigms, control seeks an executable control law; it is a forward problem with performance indices and other objectives. Estimation and observation, by contrast, constitute an inverse problem: inferring a plausible cause from outputs/observations, which naturally admits multiple inferential frameworks. Philosophically, observation/estimation is about understanding the world, while control is about transforming it. The former is centered on cognition, the latter on decision-making. It is evident that the estimation framework is more prone to branching into different epistemic perspectives.

On one hand, the theory of state observers within various deterministic frameworks is extremely rich. At the same time, new estimators capable of handling various complex distributions continue to develop. However, the former approaches the problem by expanding around the nonlinearity and suppressing noise or disturbances, which is comparatively elegant. The latter attempts to overcome nonlinearity through general-purpose (variational) optimization and sampling (essentially zeroth-order optimization, which is somewhat primitive and has deficiencies), while modeling noise as part of the model itself—a comparatively brute-force approach. This is primarily because, within the stochastic framework, we lack analytical design tools for nonlinearities. Representative methods of the former include various nonlinear observers and observers on manifolds; representatives of the latter include sigma-point Kalman filters, particle filters, and so on.

Overall, however, neither the deterministic nor the stochastic framework has doggedly pursued the nonlinear problem head-on. Below are three new developmental branches of filtering. First, **factor graph optimization** (the large-scale optimization direction), a new development of the probabilistic framework that considers more complex and flexible inference problem structures. Second, **invariant Kalman filtering** (the Lie group geometry direction), which has developed within both deterministic and stochastic frameworks, leveraging more manifold geometric structure. In simple terms, the additive operations in the system equations are replaced by group operations, which yields favorable results for nonlinear systems, particularly when angles or quaternions are involved, and allows Lyapunov functions to be constructed using Riemannian metrics. Third, filtering incorporating **optimal transport and variational inference** (the probabilistic-geometry direction), which essentially provides inference tools for handling distributions that are difficult to characterize (better high-dimensional integration methods), and also refines sampling strategies—for example, particle filters can be upgraded from traditional MCMC to more powerful sampling algorithms.
