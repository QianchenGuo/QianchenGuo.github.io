Although I don't know the historical motivation behind compact sets, let me write a bit according to my own thoughts.

Ever since analysis developed further out of calculus, infinity began to be studied by mathematicians. And studying infinity is by no means ethereal or pseudoscientific.

Reducing the infinite to the finite is one line of thought, and compactness is precisely that property in [set theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=620807750&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=%E9%9B%86%E5%90%88%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity) that turns the infinite into the finite. The most important finite-cover property of compact sets gives us a tool to study [infinite sets](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=620807750&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%A0%E9%99%90%E9%9B%86&zhida_source=entity): when there are infinitely many elements, we can extract finitely many of them to solve the problem.

[Compact sets](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=620807750&content_type=Answer&match_order=3&q=%E7%B4%A7%E9%9B%86&zhida_source=entity) can also be used in many other places, and in different spaces there are also many equivalent conditions characterizing them.

Moreover, for instance, compact operators also possess nice properties; by using compactness to constrain the range of an operator, they behave far better than [unbounded operators](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=620807750&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%A0%E7%95%8C%E7%AE%97%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity).

As another example, consider compact sequences: the Ascoli–Arzelà theorem is used to study compact sequences, and in essence it characterizes compact sets in complete [metric spaces](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=620807750&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=%E5%BA%A6%E9%87%8F%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity); the practical result is nothing more than "extracting a subsequence."
