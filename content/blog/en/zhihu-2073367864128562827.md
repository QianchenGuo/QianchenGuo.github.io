In fact, optimization algorithms with global search capability are collectively known as **black-box optimization**, a subfield of zero-order optimization. Another branch in optimization is **[global optimization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%A8%E5%B1%80%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity)**, which typically addresses mixed-integer nonlinear programming (MINLP) problems. Black-box optimization and global optimization are, in fact, intimately connected.

One connection is that black-box optimization methods are mostly stochastic algorithms that cannot guarantee global optimality, whereas some global optimization methods are deterministic. For basic concepts along these lines, refer to the following answer:

[https://www.zhihu.com/question/19731925/answer/369899451?share_code=1ryTOBiRpowFT&utm_psn=2073937433641620368](https://www.zhihu.com/question/19731925/answer/369899451?share_code=1ryTOBiRpowFT&utm_psn=2073937433641620368)

Another connection is that black-box optimization often focuses on specific solution algorithms while somewhat neglecting the decomposition of problem frameworks. In the earlier [Zero-Order Optimization (I): Overview and Model-Free Methods](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1903595957171513009) and [Zero-Order Optimization (II): Model-Based Methods](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1959271531701077799), we presented specific algorithms. However, from the perspective of global optimization, for nonconvex MINLP, the continuous relaxation typically forms a nonconvex global optimization problem, which is generally NP-hard [\[1\]](#ref_1). Therefore:

1.  All methods must make trade-offs, each with its own applicability conditions and theoretical guarantees. Some can only serve as relaxations or [subroutines](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AD%90%E7%A8%8B%E5%BA%8F&zhida_source=entity), some can only guarantee finding a [suboptimal solution](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AC%A1%E4%BC%98%E8%A7%A3&zhida_source=entity), and others are restricted to solving problems with specific structures. There is no "silver bullet" method in global optimization; in practice, one often needs to select or combine different techniques based on problem structure.
2.  The starting point of all algorithms is **finding feasible solutions (Primal Side) – proving optimality (Dual Side)**: constructing [relaxed problems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9D%BE%E5%BC%9B%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity) to provide a reliable lower bound (or upper bound) and thus certify optimality **– overall strategy (Strategy)**: how to efficiently combine the two. Beyond the specific algorithms for [problem solving](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98%E6%B1%82%E8%A7%A3&zhida_source=entity), one needs an overarching problem decomposition strategy. Many people are familiar with certain gradient-based nonlinear programming algorithms, as well as the zero-order optimization algorithms from the previous two articles, but these merely qualify as methods for solving subproblems within a broader strategy.

This third installment of the series provides a concise overview of problem decomposition strategies—an immense and still evolving field that also offers many insights for black-box optimization.

## 1. Introduction

In global optimization (especially MINLP), an "overall strategy" refers to the high-level algorithmic framework that determines how the original problem is decomposed into a series of manageable subproblems and how these subproblems interact. Specific methods such as gradient-based [optimization methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) and the zero-order optimization methods mentioned in the previous two articles are merely tools for solving these subproblems.

### 1.1 Overview

If we look beyond global optimization to the entire optimization field, there are nine major categories. They all share the philosophy of "problem decomposition," but in fundamentally different ways:

1.  **Spatial Branch and Bound (Spatial B&B) – Feasible region decomposition**: Branch and bound (including its variants such as LP/NLP-based B&B) is the most commonly used decomposition strategy, as it is **the only "general-purpose" strategy that guarantees convergence to a global optimum in finite time without relying on problem-specific structure**. It relies solely on the action of "partitioning the space" and is applicable to nearly any continuous or discrete problem.
2.  **Tree-Based Exploration–Exploitation Decomposition – Feasible region decomposition**: The key difference from traditional strategies is that it does not "prune" by solving relaxed problems; instead, it guides the search through "optimistic sampling," efficiently approaching the optimum under a limited evaluation budget.
3.  **[Structural decomposition](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BB%93%E6%9E%84%E5%88%86%E8%A7%A3&zhida_source=entity) (primal–[dual decomposition](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AF%B9%E5%81%B6%E5%88%86%E8%A7%A3&zhida_source=entity)) – Decomposing variables and constraints**: This is the most classic and systematic decomposition strategy in mathematical programming and operations research. It identifies problem structure, splits the problem into a "master problem" and several independent "subproblems," and exploits [duality](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AF%B9%E5%81%B6%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) to coordinate by exchanging dual information.
4.  **Neighborhood / Variable-Subset Decomposition (Large Neighborhood Search / Fix-and-Optimize) – Decomposing variables by "active sets"**: In each iteration, only a subset of variables is released for optimization while the rest are fixed. Global search is achieved by continually changing the subset of "[free variables](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%87%AA%E7%94%B1%E5%8F%98%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity)." This provides heuristic upper bounds.
5.  **Sequential Approximation (Outer Approximation / Family of Cutting-Plane Methods) – Approximating the [objective function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%AE%E6%A0%87%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity)**: Rather than partitioning the [domain](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AE%9A%E4%B9%89%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity), this strategy iteratively adds globally valid cuts (linearization constraints) to the original problem, gradually tightening the "relaxed version" of the [feasible region](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=3&q=%E5%8F%AF%E8%A1%8C%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity) until it approaches the original problem.
6.  **Local Strategies (Trust Region / Filling Function / Tunneling Function) – Escaping local optima**: These methods essentially construct local strategies to escape local optima and belong to the class of [heuristic methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%90%AF%E5%8F%91%E5%BC%8F%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity), providing upper bounds for the problem.
7.  **Multistage Decomposition (Dynamic Programming) – Decomposition along "time"**: Suitable for process [engineering problems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B7%A5%E7%A8%8B%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity) with temporal ordering, the entire time horizon is sliced into multiple stages, solved sequentially or backward through value functions.
8.  **Cooperative Co-evolution (CC) – Decomposition by population**: This is the dominant framework in **[Evolutionary Computation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%9B%E5%8C%96%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97&zhida_source=entity)** for large-scale problems. Inspired by "[divide and conquer](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%86%E8%80%8C%E6%B2%BB%E4%B9%8B&zhida_source=entity)," the high-dimensional decision variable vector is decomposed into multiple low-dimensional subpopulations (subproblems). Each subpopulation evolves independently, but when evaluating individual fitness, representatives (collaborators) are selected from other subpopulations to assemble a complete solution.
9.  **Multi-objective Decomposition – Decomposing the objective function**: These strategies are specifically designed for problems with multiple conflicting objectives. The entire [multi-objective optimization problem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%9A%E7%9B%AE%E6%A0%87%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity) is decomposed into a sequence of single-objective subproblems, each being a scalarized version of the original [multi-objective problem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%9A%E7%9B%AE%E6%A0%87%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity).

### 1.2 Solver Integration

These strategies are not mutually exclusive; the most powerful modern solvers are often their synthesis. The "overall strategy" in global optimization is a toolbox:

1.  Need guaranteed convergence and generality → choose spatial/variable branching.
2.  Need to exploit problem structure (sparsity/separability) → choose mathematical decomposition (Benders/DW) to generate [cutting planes](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E5%89%B2%E5%B9%B3%E9%9D%A2&zhida_source=entity).
3.  Need to accelerate upper-bound search → choose neighborhood decomposition (LNS/Kernel Search).
4.  Need to tighten relaxations via iterative cuts → choose sequential approximation (OA/ECP).

For concrete examples, one can consult the master practitioners of global optimization:

These techniques form the algorithmic foundation of deterministic global optimization solvers such as [BARON](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=BARON&zhida_source=entity), ANTIGONE, and Couenne [\[2\]](#ref_2). As illustrated by the SCIP [\[3\]](#ref_3) framework documentation, the most powerful solvers actually mix all these "strategies": within the overarching branch-and-bound framework, they run Benders decomposition to generate cuts, run LNS heuristics to find upper bounds, and run OA to accelerate convergence. Additionally, convex MINLP admits hybrid strategies such as [\[4\]](#ref_4) and [\[5\]](#ref_5). AlphaGo also integrates MCTS (an [optimistic optimization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B9%90%E8%A7%82%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity)) combined with a DP framework and uses deep [neural networks](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A5%9E%E7%BB%8F%E7%BD%91%E7%BB%9C&zhida_source=entity) for value estimation. Some large-scale optimization approaches combine cooperative co-evolution with CMA-ES as the subproblem solver.

### **3\. Black-Box Optimization**

Zero-order optimization is a more constrained setting. Only strategies that inherently rely on "function-value sampling + search-space partitioning" or "population/swarm-based evolution" can be transplanted directly. Although the ideas of branch-and-bound and neighborhood decomposition are general, their internal lower-bound construction or subproblem solvers must be replaced with zero-order methods to be practically applicable. The approaches that can be adapted to black-box optimization include:

- For **decomposition of the feasible region**, **branch-and-bound** requires constructing lower bounds (e.g., convex relaxations), but certain variants (e.g., DIRECT) construct lower bounds using Lipschitz constants or optimistic estimates, requiring only function values. Hence, the branch-and-bound idea can be made zero-order, provided that zero-order lower-bound construction methods are adopted. **Optimistic optimization** is based entirely on function-value evaluations, selecting nodes via tree-based space partitioning and an optimism criterion, requiring neither gradients nor analytic structure—it is a purely zero-order global optimization method.
- For **decomposition of the objective function**, **multi-objective decomposition** breaks the problem into single-objective subproblems, and **local strategies** target only a local surrogate-model subproblem; both can employ zero-order optimization for the subproblems.
- **Temporal decomposition** methods such as dynamic programming or MPC impose even fewer requirements on the lower-level solver. They can be solved entirely with sampling-based or zero-order optimization methods, and they support black-box system models.
- **Neighborhood decomposition** and **co-evolutionary decomposition** group high-dimensional variables or search particles and solve each low-dimensional subproblem with a sub-solver. As long as the sub-solver is zero-order, the entire framework is zero-order.

However, structural decomposition methods rely on the problem's algebraic structure (e.g., linear constraints, separability), dual variables, and subproblem convexity. They require exact gradients and constraint expressions; in zero-order settings, constructing dual information or generating cutting planes is infeasible. Sequential approximation's core step involves constructing an approximating expansion of nonlinear constraints or the objective at the current point; without gradient information, effective cutting planes cannot be generated.

## II. Branch and Bound (B&B)

By **recursively decomposing the feasible region** into smaller subregions (branching), and on each subregion computing an **upper bound** on the objective value (by finding feasible solutions heuristically) and a **lower bound** (by solving a relaxation problem). If the lower bound of a subregion already exceeds the current known global upper bound, that region is "pruned" because it cannot contain the optimal solution. This excludes regions that cannot contain the global optimum, gradually approaching it. It does not require the problem to be convex, and can handle both integer variables (by branching on integers) and continuous variables (spatial branching), as long as a lower (or upper) bound can be computed for any subregion. This is the most mainstream framework for global optimization. Typical application scenarios are problems with strict optimality-certificate requirements, such as chemical process design and circuit design.

Markowitz and Manne first proposed branch-and-bound for MILP solution in 1957 [\[6\]](#ref_6), and it was later extended to convex MINLP [\[7\]](#ref_7), also serving as a cornerstone for nonconvex MINLP. For mixed-integer nonlinear programming (MINLP), the **LP/NLP-based Branch-and-Bound**[\[8\]](#ref_8) is formed by adding extra constraints that force specific variables to take integer values. Compared with MILP branch-and-bound, the relaxation is no longer a simple linear relaxation; instead, each constraint and the objective function are relaxed using convex underestimators/concave overestimators. Besides narrowing the bounds of integer variables, the variables involved in constraints must also be bounded, and the corresponding approximation functions updated accordingly. Hence, for MINLP, in addition to the convex relaxation of the entire problem that can improve convergence speed, tighter relaxations of specific constraints/functions can also accelerate convergence. Its efficiency depends heavily on obtaining tight lower bounds, which leads to poor performance in high-dimensional problems.

For solvers, see GAMS' [solver list](https://www.gams.com/latest/docs/S_MAIN.html).

### 1\. Algorithm Framework

Consider the problem:

$$
\min_{x \in \Omega} f(x), \quad \Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n
$$

The algorithm maintains a set of pending subregions $\mathcal{L}$. Initially $\mathcal{L} = \{\Omega\}$. A node list $\mathcal{L}$ is maintained, where each node corresponds to a subdomain $B\subseteq X$, along with a global upper bound $U$ (current best feasible solution) and a global lower bound $L$ (minimum lower bound over all nodes). The iterative steps are:

1.  **Selection**: Remove a subregion $B$ from $\mathcal{L}$ (usually the one with the smallest lower bound).
2.  **Branching**: Partition $B$ into finitely many smaller subregions $B_1,\dots,B_k$ (typically by bisecting along the longest edge or a variable).
3.  **Bounding**: For each subregion $B_i$, compute a lower bound $\ell(B_i)$ satisfying $\ell(B_i)\le \min_{x\in B_i} f(x)$; simultaneously attempt to find a feasible point in $B_i$ to update the upper bound $U$.
4.  **Pruning**: If $\ell(B_i)\ge U$, then $B_i$ cannot contain a point better than the current best solution; delete it.
5.  **Termination**: Stop when $\mathcal{L}=\emptyset$ or $U-L\le\epsilon$.

### 2\. Branching Strategies

How the original problem is split into subproblems directly determines the size of the search tree and thus affects the efficiency of solving the problem within a reasonable time [\[9\]](#ref_9). Common branching rules include:

-   Most infeasible branching, selecting the variable whose rounding direction is the most ambiguous [\[10\]](#ref_10);
-   Pseudocost branching, using row coefficients and dual solution values as weights;
-   Strong branching, selecting the branching variable most likely to significantly improve solution quality by evaluating fractional candidate variables [\[11\]](#ref_11);
-   Branching with reduced-cost fixing [\[12\]](#ref_12).
-   In recent years, hybrid branching has become increasingly widespread. For example, strong-branching/pseudocost hybrid strategies employ strong branching in the upper levels of the search tree (until a preset depth) and switch to pseudocost branching at deeper levels. Another approach introduces strong-branching initialization into pseudocost branching, using strong branching to compute initial estimates for variables whose pseudocosts are uninitialized [\[13\]](#ref_13). Reliability branching extends this idea further, performing strong branching not only for variables with uninitialized pseudocosts but also for variables whose pseudocost values are unreliable [\[10\]](#ref_10). More refined hybrid branching rules have also been proposed, further optimizing the branching process [\[14\]](#ref_14).

Many other variants exist, and the concepts here are somewhat intertwined—for instance, spatial B&B, general B&B, geometric B&B, and how branching is performed. Not all spatial B&B methods, nor all B&B methods, relax discrete variables into continuous ones. See:

[https://www.zhihu.com/question/574272494/answer/2817170472](https://www.zhihu.com/question/574272494/answer/2817170472)

### 3\. Lower-Bound Computation

The efficiency of branch-and-bound depends entirely on the tightness of the lower bound. Common lower-bound construction methods include:

**(1) Convex Relaxation**

-   If the continuous relaxation of the MINLP is convex, branch-and-bound is well suited. Use a convex function (e.g., linear, quadratic, αBB) $g(x)\le f(x)$ that underestimates $f$ on $B$, then solve the convex problem to obtain a lower bound. For example, the classical αBB method [\[15\]](#ref_15) targets smooth functions, exploiting a lower bound on the Hessian to construct a convex quadratic term: $g(x)=f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i (x_i-\underline{x}_i)(\overline{x}_i-x_i)$, where $\alpha_i$ is large enough to make $g$ convex.
-   The McCormick relaxation, for the bilinear term $xy$, constructs the tightest convex lower and concave upper bounds on a rectangular domain:

    $$
    xy \ge \max\{ \underline{x}y+\underline{y}x-\underline{x}\underline{y},\ \overline{x}y+\overline{y}x-\overline{x}\overline{y} \}
    $$

-   Some nonconvex problems actually have equivalent convex representations, often requiring the introduction of extra lifting variables to obtain an extended formulation. **Geometric Programming (GP)** is the most classical example. The problem takes the form: $\min_{x>0} f(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} c_k \prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{a_{ki}}$, where $c_k>0$ and $a_{ki}$ are real. Through the variable substitution $y_i = \ln x_i$, the original problem becomes a convex optimization problem in $y$: $\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^n} \sum_{k=1}^{K} c_k e^{a_k^\top y}$. The convexity of the sum of exponentials makes the problem tractable. Many engineering problems (e.g., circuit design, network flow allocation) can be formulated as geometric programs. This is applicable only when the objective and constraints are posynomials. The variable substitution $y_i=\ln x_i$ converts them exactly into convex problems.
-   Another example is the transformation of quasiconvex functions. By applying a monotone transformation (e.g., logarithm, power function), certain quasiconvex problems can be converted into convex ones.
-   For polynomial optimization, the Lasserre hierarchy provides a convergent algorithm based on semidefinite relaxations. In the Lasserre hierarchy, integers are transformed into binary variables, and a binary variable $x$ can be further expressed as a polynomial equality (1-x)x=0, so polynomial MINLP can be solved by the Lasserre hierarchy.

**(2) Interval Arithmetic**

Used to **rigorously** compute the range of a function over a box. Given a box $X = [\underline{x}, \overline{x}] \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, the interval extension $F(X)$ is an interval containing $f(X) = \{f(x): x\in X\}$, i.e., $F(X) \supseteq f(X)$. Interval extensions are obtained automatically by replacing the operations in the function with interval operations. Using interval arithmetic, a lower bound for the function value over any box can be computed as $\text{lb}(X) = \underline{F(X)}$ and an upper bound as $\text{ub}(X) = \overline{F(X)}$.

Interval arithmetic can be combined with **outward rounding** to achieve rigorous inclusion at the machine level, meaning the computed interval is guaranteed to contain the true range of function values. Hence, interval methods can **prove** that no global optimum exists within a certain box, and can even prove—within finite steps—that a point is globally optimal (provided accuracy requirements are met). This is a significant advantage over other heuristic algorithms.

The main issue with interval analysis is the **dependency problem**: because correlations between variables are ignored, interval extensions often severely overestimate the function range, leading to poor pruning efficiency. As dimensionality increases, the computational cost of interval methods grows exponentially, so they are typically only suitable for low-dimensional problems.

**(3) Lipschitz Lower Bounds**

If $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L\|x-y\|$, then on a ball centered at $x_i$ with radius $r_i$, the lower bound is $f(x_i)-L r_i$. **Lipschitz optimization** is an important class of deterministic global optimization methods, often categorized under branch-and-bound or interval analysis frameworks. If the objective function $f$ satisfies the Lipschitz condition on $\Omega$: $|f(x)-f(y)| \le L \|x-y\|, \quad \forall x,y\in\Omega$ and the constant $L$ is known, then a sawtooth-shaped lower-bound function can be constructed:

$$
\underline{f}(x) = \max_{i=1,\dots,N} \{ f(x_i) - L \|x-x_i\| \}
$$

where $x_i$ are already evaluated points. This lower bound function is piecewise linear (conical in multiple dimensions) and serves as a global lower bound. The point minimizing this lower bound function is then used as the next sampling point, gradually approaching the global optimum. The well-known Piyavskii–Shubert algorithm is based on this idea in one dimension; multidimensional extensions include DIRECT (which does not require knowing $L$) and MCS, among others. Lipschitz optimization is also deterministic, as it exploits smoothness information of the function to exclude regions that cannot contain the global optimum.

**(4) Dual Relaxation**

Lagrangian duality, SDP relaxation, etc. These are standard techniques in optimization and need no further elaboration.

**(5) DC Decomposition (Difference of Convex)**

Difference-of-convex (DC) programming methods [\[16\]](#ref_16). In DC programming, a nonconvex objective function is rewritten over a compact set as the difference of two convex functions. Although DC programming itself remains nonconvex, it can be solved via **convex relaxation** or **iterative convexification** (e.g., the DCA algorithm). Several algorithms have been developed in the literature for solving DC programs, including the DC Algorithm (DCA) [\[17\]](#ref_17), the Convex-Concave Procedure (CCP) [\[18\]](#ref_18), and various proximal or penalty-enhanced variants for improved convergence (Lipp and Boyd, 2016; Wen et al., 2018).

**(6) Homotopy / Continuation Methods**

A homotopy parameter is introduced to construct a family of problems $H(x, \lambda)$ such that at $\lambda = 0$, the problem is easy to solve (e.g., convex or with a known solution), and at $\lambda = 1$, the original problem is recovered. The solutions $x(\lambda)$ form a path from the initial solution to the target solution. By numerically tracking this path, a solution to the original problem is obtained. For global optimization, one often starts from a convexified/smoothed version and gradually deforms it into the original nonconvex function. This is also a highly important idea applicable in many contexts, for example:

-   Linear homotopy: $H(x, \lambda) = (1-\lambda) G(x) + \lambda F(x)$, where $F(x)$ is the gradient or KKT system of the original problem and $G(x)$ is an easily solvable problem (e.g., $G(x) = \nabla f_\lambda$, which at $\lambda=0$ is the gradient of a convex function).
-   Convexification homotopy: $f_\lambda(x) = (1-\lambda) \frac{1}{2}\|x - x_0\|^2 + \lambda f(x)$, starting near $x_0$ and gradually transitioning to the original function as $\lambda$ increases.
-   Gaussian homotopy (already encountered in "II. Gradient Estimation Methods"): $f_\lambda(x) = \mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)} f(x + \lambda \xi)$. As $\lambda$ goes from large to small (or 0 to 1), the function transitions from a highly smoothed version to the original function.
-   Penalty homotopy: $H(x, \lambda) = f(x) + \frac{1}{\lambda} \sum_i \max(0, g_i(x))^2$, gradually restoring the constraints as $\lambda \to \infty$.

Predictor–corrector methods for path tracking

-   Predictor step: At the current point $(x_k, \lambda_k)$, solve for the tangent vector $v = (\dot{x}, \dot{\lambda})$ such that: $H_x(x_k, \lambda_k) \dot{x} + H_\lambda(x_k, \lambda_k) \dot{\lambda} = 0$, where $H_x$ and $H_\lambda$ are partial-derivative matrices/vectors. Then predict along the tangent direction: $(x_{k+1}^{(0)}, \lambda_{k+1}^{(0)}) = (x_k, \lambda_k) + \Delta s \, v$, where $\Delta s$ is the step size.
-   Corrector step: Fix a constraint (e.g., fix $\lambda$ or use an arc-length parameter) and solve with Newton's method:

$$
\begin{cases} H(x, \lambda) = 0 \\ \text{constraint equation} \end{cases}
$$

Common constraint equations:

-   Fixing $\lambda$: $\lambda = \lambda_{k+1}^{(0)}$, then use Newton's method to solve $H(x, \lambda)=0$ for $x$.
-   Pseudo-arclength method: add the condition $\dot{x}_k^T (x - x_k) + \dot{\lambda}_k (\lambda - \lambda_k) = \Delta s$, so that the correction lies on a hyperplane perpendicular to the tangent, enabling passage through turning points.
-   Arclength method: add the condition $\|x - x_k\|^2 + (\lambda - \lambda_k)^2 = \Delta s^2$ and solve the augmented system with Newton's method.

Meanwhile, adjust $\Delta s$ according to the convergence behavior of the iterations: increase the step size if the corrector converges in few iterations, otherwise decrease it. Monitor the condition number of the Jacobian matrix $H_x$, reducing the step size near singular points.

-   Homotopy paths may exhibit **turning points, bifurcations, or discontinuities**; numerical tracking may fail to reach the global optimum of the original problem, and the path may stop or jump to another branch.
-   Even if the path exists, it may terminate at a local solution rather than the global optimum, unless the original problem has special structure (e.g., convexity, monotonicity) or the homotopy construction guarantees that the path leads to the global optimum.

### 4. Convergence Theory

**Theorem**: If the branching process generates a sequence of nested subdomains whose diameters tend to zero, and the lower-bound computation is **consistent** (i.e., for any sequence $\{B_k\}$ shrinking to a point $x^*$, $\lim_{k\to\infty} \ell(B_k)=f(x^*)$), then the branch-and-bound algorithm converges to the global optimum under infinite refinement, and any accuracy $\epsilon$ can be achieved in finitely many steps. The key to this theorem is that the global lower bound $L=\min_{B\in\mathcal{L}}\ell(B)$ approaches the true minimum as subdomain diameters shrink, while the global upper bound $U$ is obtained from any feasible solution; the two bounds squeeze together so that $U-L\to 0$.

The method may not find the exact optimum in finitely many steps, but it can provide an interval of arbitrary accuracy around the global optimum.

### 5. Representative Methods

DIRECT (DIviding RECTangles) is a derivative-free global optimization algorithm proposed by Jones et al., which can be viewed as an adaptive branch-and-bound method. It requires no Lipschitz constant and searches for the global optimum by simultaneously balancing "exploration" (subdividing larger regions) and "exploitation" (subdividing smaller regions). It is particularly well-suited for low-dimensional black-box function optimization. Other representative solvers include BARON, SCIP[\[3\]](#ref_3), LINDO, and others.

## III. Optimistic Optimization: Tree-Based Exploration–Exploitation Decomposition[\[19\]](#ref_19)

The core idea of optimistic optimization is to recursively partition the search space into a tree (e.g., KD-tree, Monte Carlo tree), maintaining for each node an "optimistic value"—the best possible lower bound the node could achieve (such as the upper confidence bound, UCB). This value balances the node's current best observation against its uncertainty. At each iteration, the algorithm selects and expands the node with the highest optimistic value, thereby dynamically focusing computational resources on the most promising regions. Applicable scenarios include black-box optimization, hyperparameter tuning, reinforcement learning (e.g., AlphaGo), online planning, and other problems where evaluation is expensive.

Consider unconstrained black-box global optimization: $\min_{x\in X} f(x)$, where $X\subset\mathbb{R}^d$ is a compact set, $f$ is evaluable but has no closed form (black box), and $f$ is typically assumed to satisfy local smoothness near the global optimum (e.g., a local Lipschitz condition). Since evaluation is costly, we must approximate the global optimum using as few function evaluations as possible.

### 1. Tree-Based Space Partitioning

Hierarchical partitioning: Partition $X$ into a series of nested cells. Let $C_{h,i}$ denote the $i$-th cell at depth $h$, satisfying:

- $C_{0,1}=X$;
- Each $C_{h,i}$ is further partitioned into $K$ subcells $C_{h+1,i_1},\dots,C_{h+1,i_K}$ (typically $K=2^d$, i.e., bisection along each dimension);
- Nesting: Subcells are fully contained in their parent cell;
- Each cell $C_{h,i}$ has a representative point $x_{h,i}\in C_{h,i}$ (often the center).

Tree nodes: Each node of the tree corresponds to a cell $C_{h,i}$. The root node has depth $0$, and leaf nodes are the most finely partitioned cells. The growth of the tree is precisely the progressive refinement of the search space.

### 2. Constructing Optimistic Values

**(1) DOO: Optimistic Lower Bound with Known Lipschitz Constant**

If $f$ satisfies a global Lipschitz condition: $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L\|x-y\|$, then on cell $C_{h,i}$, a lower bound on the function value is: $b_{h,i} = f(x_{h,i}) - L \cdot \operatorname{diam}(C_{h,i})$,

where $\operatorname{diam}(C_{h,i})$ is the diameter of the cell (maximum distance between any two points). This $b_{h,i}$ serves as an **optimistic lower bound** on $f$ over $C_{h,i}$.

**(2) SOO: Optimistic Criterion with Unknown Lipschitz Constant**

When $L$ is unknown, an absolute optimistic lower bound cannot be computed. SOO's strategy is to use **only relative comparisons**. Among nodes at the same depth, a smaller $f(x_{h,i})$ indicates a more promising node. Specifically, SOO maintains function values for all evaluated nodes and at each iteration:

1. Finds the current maximum depth $h_{\max}$;
2. For each depth $h\le h_{\max}$, selects the node with the smallest $f(x_{h,i})$ among all depth-$h$ nodes that have not yet been expanded;
3. Expands the children of these selected nodes simultaneously;
4. Updates the tree and the best value found.

SOO's key advantage: **No knowledge of $L$ is required—optimistic selection is achieved through lateral (same-depth) comparisons**.

**(3) Optimistic Value in UCT**

In Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), node selection uses the UCB1 formula:

$$
\text{UCT}(v) = \bar{X}_v + C_p \sqrt{\frac{2\ln n_{\text{parent}}}{n_v}},
$$

where $\bar{X}_v$ is the average return of node $v$; $n_v$ is the number of times node $v$ has been visited; $n_{\text{parent}}$ is the visit count of the parent; and $C_p>0$ is an exploration constant. The first term leverages known information (exploitation), while the second encourages exploring less-visited nodes (exploration). This is essentially a statistical optimistic bound: the average return plus a confidence boundary inversely proportional to visit count.

### 3. Algorithm Framework (Using SOO as an Example)

Initialization: Let the root node be $C_{0,1}=X$ and evaluate $f(x_{0,1})$. Record the current best value $f^*=f(x_{0,1})$ and optimal solution $x^*=x_{0,1}$. The tree $T$ contains only the root node.

Iterative loop: while the evaluation budget is not exhausted

1. Find the current maximum depth $h_{max}$.
2. Let $v =$ root node.
3. For each depth $h = 0, 1, ..., h_{max}$: a. Among all unexpanded depth-$h$ nodes with the smallest $f(x_{h,i})$ at that depth, select the best candidate node $v$; b. If $v$ has been expanded, descend into its best child node.
4. Expand all $K$ children of node $v$: evaluate $f$ at the children's representative points; update $f^*$ and $x^*$; add the children to the tree.
5. Stop if the budget is exhausted or target accuracy is reached.

DOO follows a similar procedure but selects nodes using the absolute optimistic lower bound $b_{h,i}$ (i.e., it expands the node with the smallest $b_{h,i}$).

### 4. Convergence Theory

(1) Convergence of DOO: Assume there exists a semi-metric $\ell$ (possibly asymmetric) such that $f(x) - f^* \le \ell(x, x^*)$, and $\ell$ shrinks with cell size. If the partitioning rule guarantees $\operatorname{diam}_{\ell}(C_{h,i}) \le C \cdot \delta^h$ (with $\delta<1$), then DOO's error bound after $n$ evaluations is:

$$
f(x_n^*) - f^* \le C \cdot \delta^{\Omega(n^{1/d})},
$$

where $x_n^*$ is the best point among the first $n$ evaluations. This implies that the **error decays exponentially with the number of evaluations**, though the rate is constrained by the dimension $d$.

(2) Convergence of SOO: SOO does not require $L$ to be known, but the convergence rate is slightly slower. If $f$ satisfies a local Lipschitz condition near the global optimum (with unknown constant $L$), then SOO's error after $n$ evaluations satisfies:

$$
f(x_n^*) - f^* \le C \cdot \left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\alpha},
$$

where $\alpha$ depends on the local smoothness and dimension. Compared to DOO, SOO's exponential convergence degrades to polynomial convergence, though in practice the two exhibit similar performance.

(3) Properties of MCTS/UCT: UCT achieves a **consistent regret bound** in finite-horizon Markov decision processes (MDPs): if rewards lie in $[0,1]$, then after $n$ iterations, UCT's cumulative regret relative to the optimal policy is $O(\sqrt{n \log n})$. This implies that MCTS's selection policy converges to the optimal action under infinite iterations.

Concrete algorithm variants:

| Algorithm | Type | Lipschitz Knowledge | Objective | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DOO | Deterministic | Required | Continuous black box | Exponential convergence, limited applicability |
| SOO | Deterministic | Not required | Continuous black box | Similar to DOO but without needing $L$ |
| StoSOO | Stochastic | Not required | Continuous black box | Handles stochastic noise |
| HOO | Stochastic | Required (local) | Continuous black box | Online learning perspective, uses confidence intervals |
| UCB-AIR | Stochastic | Not required | Continuous black box | Adaptive partitioning |
| MCTS/UCT | Stochastic | Not required | Decision problems | Tree search + stochastic simulation |

Optimistic optimization essentially replaces lower-bound estimation with optimistic values, **sacrificing rigorous pruning in exchange for scalability and globality in black-box settings**. It does not guarantee finite-step termination to $\epsilon$-accuracy, but it can be proven to converge to the global optimum under infinite evaluations, with controllable error bounds for the first $n$ evaluations.

## IV. Structural Decomposition (Primal–Dual Decomposition)

These methods decompose the original problem **along structural components**, coordinating subproblems through the exchange of dual information. They achieve only finite or asymptotic convergence and are suitable for problems with block structure (amenable to decomposition) and many integer variables. Benders decomposition / column generation are far more efficient than branch-and-bound on problems with strong linear structure but an extremely large number of integer variables (e.g., large-scale network design). They are commonly applied to large-scale mixed-integer programming (MIP) and mixed-integer nonlinear programming (MINLP), such as supply chain optimization, network design, and unit commitment.

### 1. Benders Decomposition[\[20\]](#ref_20)

Partition variables into complex variables $y$ (e.g., integer variables) and simple variables $x$ (continuous variables).

$$
\begin{aligned} \min_{x,y} & \quad c^T x + d^T y \\ \text{s.t.} & \quad Ax + By \le b, \\ & \quad x \in X,\ y \in Y. \end{aligned}
$$

After fixing the complex variables $y=\bar{y}$, the subproblem becomes a convex NLP (easily solved):

$$
\begin{aligned} Q(\bar{y}) = \min_{x} & \quad c^T x \\ \text{s.t.} & \quad Ax \le b - B\bar{y},\\ & \quad x\in X. \end{aligned}
$$

If the subproblem is feasible, its dual optimal solution $\bar{u}$ yields an optimal linear cut (a Benders cut) returned to the master problem: $\theta \ge d^T y + \bar{u}^T (b - By)$. If the subproblem is infeasible, a feasibility cut (e.g., via Farkas' lemma) is used to eliminate infeasible $y$. The master problem is:

$$
\begin{aligned} \min_{y,\theta} & \quad \theta \\ \text{s.t.} & \quad \theta \ge d^T y + \bar{u}_k^T (b - By) \quad \forall k, \\ & \quad y\in Y. \end{aligned}
$$

By iteratively solving subproblems to generate cuts, the master problem gradually approximates the true objective, converging in finitely many steps (when $Y$ is finite) or asymptotically. If $Y$ is a finite set and the set of dual optimal solutions to the subproblem is finite, Benders decomposition terminates at an optimal solution in a finite number of iterations. In the general convex continuous case, the cutting-plane method converges to the optimal value.

### 2. Dantzig–Wolfe Decomposition / Column Generation

The problem is reformulated as a "set covering/partitioning" form. The master problem handles a subset of columns (variables), while the subproblem (pricing problem) dynamically generates new columns that improve the master problem's objective. This is the core strategy in GCG (a branch-and-price-and-cut framework built on SCIP). Suppose the constraint matrix has a block-angular structure:

$$
\min \sum_{s=1}^S c_s^T x_s \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{s=1}^S A_s x_s \le b,\ x_s\in X_s.
$$

Represent each subproblem $X_s$ as a convex combination of a finite point set (or extreme points): $X_s = \operatorname{conv}(\{x_s^1,x_s^2,\dots\})$. Let $x_s=\sum_j \lambda_{s,j} x_s^j$ and substitute into the original problem to obtain the **master problem**:

$$
\begin{aligned} \min_{\lambda} & \quad \sum_{s,j} (c_s^T x_s^j) \lambda_{s,j} \\ \text{s.t.} & \quad \sum_{s,j} (A_s x_s^j) \lambda_{s,j} \le b, \\ & \quad \sum_j \lambda_{s,j}=1,\ \lambda_{s,j}\ge 0. \end{aligned}
$$

The master problem contains only a subset of columns (variables). For each subproblem $s$, solve the **pricing problem**:

$$
\min_{x_s\in X_s} \left( c_s^T - \pi^T A_s \right) x_s,
$$

where $\pi$ is the dual multiplier of the master problem. If the optimal value is negative, a new column is generated and added to the master problem; otherwise, the current master problem optimum is the original problem's optimum. Since the master problem is an exact reformulation of the original problem (using convex combinations to represent all feasible points), column generation eventually attains optimality. In practice, the restricted master problem (RMP) is solved iteratively, stopping when no column with negative reduced cost can be generated.

### 3. Lagrange Relaxation

Coupling constraints that are difficult to handle are "absorbed" into the objective via multipliers, decomposing the original problem into several independent, easily solvable subproblems. Dual search is used to update the multipliers, yielding a lower bound. For problems with coupling constraints:

$$
\min f(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad g(x)\le 0,\ x\in X.
$$

Construct the Lagrangian $L(x,\lambda)=f(x)+\lambda^T g(x)$ and the dual function: $q(\lambda)=\min_{x\in X} L(x,\lambda)$. The dual problem $\max_{\lambda\ge 0} q(\lambda)$ is a concave maximization and can be solved via subgradient methods to update $\lambda$. This is a common technique in convex optimization and nonlinear programming, where—building on Lagrange relaxation—we often go further by considering only the KKT system, typically augmented with interior-point Newton methods (high convergence order but expensive Hessian inversions), augmented Lagrangian methods to enhance convexity (though this breaks separability), and ADMM for decomposition (which restores separability), among others.

Lagrange relaxation and cutting planes:

$$
q(\lambda)\le f^*
$$

provides a lower bound; solving the dual yields multipliers that can generate cutting planes for the master problem. This method is the dual perspective of Benders decomposition / column generation.

## V. Neighborhood / Variable Subset Decomposition

This is a powerful metaheuristic strategy that does not guarantee global optimality (proving optimality may incur prohibitive [computational cost](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97%E6%88%90%E6%9C%AC&zhida_source=entity), or may even be infeasible [\[21\]](#ref_21)), but aims to find high-quality feasible solutions (upper bounds) within acceptable time by pruning subtrees early and reducing the search tree size of branch-and-bound algorithms. Almost all large-scale MIP/MINLP solvers (e.g., SCIP [\[3\]](#ref_3), Gurobi) embed LNS as a core primal heuristic for finding high-quality feasible solutions.

Starting from a feasible solution, most variables are fixed, and only a small subset is released (based on certain rules, such as fractional values or random selection), forming a small subproblem (MIP/NLP) to be solved exactly. This is a "Destroy-Repair" strategy. These methods rely on exact solution of the subproblem, so even with most variables fixed, the subproblem may still be difficult, but it is typically much easier than the original problem.

They can be divided into two categories: start heuristics (also called construction heuristics), which generate an initial feasible solution, and improvement heuristics, which optimize an existing feasible solution. Representative start heuristics include diving [\[22\]](#ref_22) [\[23\]](#ref_23), neighborhood rounding [\[22\]](#ref_22) [\[23\]](#ref_23), Relaxation Enforced Neighborhood Search (RENS) [\[22\]](#ref_22), and the feasibility pump [\[24\]](#ref_24); representative improvement heuristics include local branching [\[25\]](#ref_25), proximity search [\[26\]](#ref_26), Relaxation Induced Neighborhood Search (RINS) [\[27\]](#ref_27), and optimization polishing [\[28\]](#ref_28). Start and improvement heuristics are strongly interdependent: start heuristics provide improvement heuristics with the initial solution needed for optimization, while improvement heuristics refine the low-quality solutions produced by start heuristics [\[21\]](#ref_21).

In addition, preprocessing (also called presolving) is a core component of efficient integer programming. Brearley et al. [\[29\]](#ref_29) and Savelsbergh [\[30\]](#ref_30) both emphasized its importance. The goal of preprocessing is to quickly identify and remove redundant constraints and variables, while tightening variable bounds, yielding a smaller and easier problem model. This is especially important for [branch-and-bound](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%86%E6%94%AF%E5%AE%9A%E7%95%8C%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity), since it typically requires solving a large number of linear/nonlinear programming subproblems [\[23\]](#ref_23). Preprocessing techniques can be applied at the root node (before solving the first relaxation) as well as at subsequent nodes of the search tree [\[31\]](#ref_31). Multiple studies have shown that preprocessing can significantly improve the performance of MIP solvers [\[31\]](#ref_31) [\[21\]](#ref_21) [\[32\]](#ref_32).

### 1. Large Neighborhood Search (LNS)

In SCIP [\[3\]](#ref_3), LNS manifests as heuristics such as RENS, RINS, and Crossover, and is the most powerful tool for finding high-quality feasible solutions (upper bounds). Without it, pure branch-and-bound would be virtually unusable in industrial practice. The strategy is: starting from the current solution or LP relaxation solution $x^*$, fix most variables (typically over 90%), and release only a small variable subset $\mathcal{F}$ (based on fractional values or conflict analysis), forming a small sub-MIP/NLP to be solved.

$$
\min_{x_\mathcal{F}} f(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x_i = x_i^* \ \forall i\notin\mathcal{F}.
$$

This subproblem is small and can be solved exactly using a MILP/NLP solver. The solution is then updated, a new release subset is selected, and the process repeats.

**Destroy-Repair**: LNS alternates between "destroy" (fixing a subset of variables) and "repair" (optimizing the remaining variables). The selection strategy for the release set varies, e.g., random, based on fractional values, or based on conflict analysis.

### 2. RINS (Relaxation Induced Neighborhood Search)

During the branch-and-bound process, take the LP relaxation optimum $x^{LP}$ and the current best integer solution $x^{inc}$. The release set is defined as:

$$
\mathcal{F} = \{i \mid x_i^{LP} \text{ is near-integer but } x_i^{inc} \text{ differs from that integer, or both are non-integer}\}.
$$

Fix the other variables and solve the sub-MIP. RINS effectively improves the integer solution. There are also heuristics similar to Local Branching.

### 3. RENS (Relaxation Enforced Neighborhood Search)

Simply round the fractional variables in the LP solution to nearby integers, fix all non-fractional variables, and solve the sub-MIP. Simple yet effective for finding an initial feasible solution.

### 4. Kernel Search

Partition the variables into a "kernel" (important variables) and several "buckets." First optimize the kernel (possibly adding a few other variables), then merge each bucket with the kernel sequentially, solving a series of much smaller independent subproblems. Variables outside the current bucket are fixed, keeping the problem size manageable, while adaptive heuristic strategies can also be incorporated. This is mentioned as a new feature in SCIP 10.

## VI. Sequential Approximation (Outer Approximation / Cutting-Plane Family)

The core idea is linearization, applicable to **convex MINLP** problems, since [cutting planes](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%87%E5%B9%B3%E9%9D%A2&zhida_source=entity) provide globally valid underestimators. This approach is widely used in process systems engineering. These strategies do not solve the original problem directly; instead, they iteratively construct and solve an approximate, easier problem (such as an LP or mixed-integer linear program (MIP)) to approximate the original. They solve convex problems efficiently. Historically, as early as 1957, Markowitz and Manne combined branch-and-bound with the cutting-plane method (a sequential approximation approach) to propose branch-and-cut [\[33\]](#ref_33). Two decades later, researchers successfully applied branch-and-cut to practical problems [\[34\]](#ref_34) [\[35\]](#ref_35). Subsequent research focused on improving the two core components of branch-and-cut: branching strategies and cutting planes. The branching strategy is the key factor determining the worst-case exponential complexity of LP-based branch-and-cut algorithms. This was later extended to convex MINLP [\[36\]](#ref_36).

### 1. Outer Approximation (OA) [\[37\]](#ref_37)

OA alternates between solving a MIP relaxation (providing a lower bound) and an NLP subproblem (providing an upper bound). This is a "sequential iteration" strategy with no branch-and-bound tree. For **convex MINLP**:

$$
\begin{aligned} \min_{x,y} & \quad c^T x + d^T y \\ \text{s.t.} & \quad g_j(x,y)\le 0 \quad (j=1,\dots,m),\\ & \quad x\in\mathbb{R}^n,\ y\in Y \text{ discrete}. \end{aligned}
$$

Assume $g_j$ is differentiable in the continuous variables $x$ and jointly convex. The two subproblems are solved alternately:

- **NLP subproblem** (with integer variables fixed at $y^k$): solve the continuous NLP to obtain the optimal $x^k$ and an upper bound.
- **MILP master problem**: construct linearization cuts in the $(x,y)$ space:

$$
g_j(x^k,y^k) + \nabla g_j(x^k,y^k)^T \begin{pmatrix} x-x^k \\ y-y^k \end{pmatrix} \le 0.
$$

All cuts are added to the MILP master problem, which is solved to obtain a new integer candidate $y^{k+1}$ and a lower bound. Each iteration takes a **first-order Taylor expansion** (cutting plane) at the integer solution of the NLP subproblem. Since the first-order expansion of a convex function at any point is a global underestimator, the linearization cuts never remove any feasible point. As iterations proceed, the MILP master problem becomes increasingly tight, and eventually its optimal value equals that of the original convex MINLP. If both the MILP and NLP are solved exactly, OA converges in a finite number of iterations (since the number of integer combinations is finite). For **convex MINLP**, because linearization cutting planes are highly effective, convergence is typically much faster than pure branch-and-bound (OA is the benchmark for convex MINLP).

### 2. Extended Cutting Plane (ECP)

The cutting-plane method was pioneered by Dantzig et al. [\[38\]](#ref_38) [\[39\]](#ref_39) and Gomory [\[40\]](#ref_40) in the MILP domain and later extended to MINLP. It improves the LP relaxation model by removing infeasible regions while never excluding feasible integer solutions.

One of the most classical cutting-plane algorithms is the Gomory mixed-integer cut [\[41\]](#ref_41), a generalization of the Gomory fractional cut [\[42\]](#ref_42) applicable to MILP problems without requiring any special problem structure. Subsequently, many other types of cutting planes were proposed to exploit special problem structures, including disjunctive cuts [\[43\]](#ref_43) (disjunctive programming), knapsack cover cuts [\[44\]](#ref_44) [\[45\]](#ref_45) (0-1 knapsack constraints), flow path cuts [\[46\]](#ref_46) (network flows), implied bound cuts [\[47\]](#ref_47), lifted projection cuts [\[48\]](#ref_48), GUB cover cuts [\[49\]](#ref_49), flow cover cuts [\[46\]](#ref_46) [\[50\]](#ref_50) (network flows), clique cuts [\[51\]](#ref_51) [\[52\]](#ref_52), mixed-integer rounding cuts [\[53\]](#ref_53), and multicommodity network flow cuts [\[54\]](#ref_54). Bixby and Rothberg [\[55\]](#ref_55) noted that due to the complex interactions among various cutting-plane methods, evaluating the performance of a new cut in isolation is extremely challenging. To address this, their study disabled one cutting-plane method at a time to assess its individual impact. The results showed that, despite Gomory mixed-integer cuts being the earliest proposed cutting-plane method, disabling them caused the largest performance degradation in the solver, demonstrating their effectiveness. Furthermore, while most cutting-plane methods are broadly effective across various problem instances, some are more specialized and perform better on specific model classes [\[21\]](#ref_21).

In MINLP, ECP can be viewed as a variant of OA [\[56\]](#ref_56). It is similar to outer approximation but **does not solve the NLP subproblem**. In each iteration, only the MILP master problem is solved to obtain an integer solution $\bar{y}$, and the nonlinear constraints are then linearized at that point, with cuts added to the master problem to tighten the relaxation. The process is more straightforward and brute-force, but also more "sequentialized." Convergence holds under convexity assumptions, though it may be slower than OA.

### 3. Kelley's Cutting-Plane Method

For a continuous convex problem $\min_{x\in X} f(x)$, at the iteration point $x^k$, use a subgradient $s^k\in\partial f(x^k)$ to construct a linear cut: $f(x) \ge f(x^k) + (s^k)^T (x-x^k).$

These cuts are added to an LP model, minimizing a variable 

$$
\theta

$$

 as the objective, gradually approaching the minimum of the original problem. This method is essentially a special case of Benders decomposition in pure continuous convex optimization.

## VII. Local Strategies

These methods can in theory be applied to general continuous unconstrained problems, but in practice they lack robustness and convergence guarantees, are sensitive to parameters, and often fall into new difficulties. They are not general-purpose methods; they are closer to heuristic research. This part includes model-based methods but with weak theory and many heuristic tricks. These methods are all tricks for escaping local optima after becoming trapped.

### 1. Trust-Region Methods

More of a strategy than an optimization algorithm. The core idea is to define a "trust region" around the current iteration point, approximate the objective function with a simple surrogate model within that region, and update the iteration point by solving a subproblem of the surrogate model restricted to the trust region. In zero-order optimization, the surrogate model can be constructed via polynomial interpolation, [Gaussian process](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%AB%98%E6%96%AF%E8%BF%87%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) regression, finite-difference second-order Taylor expansions, and so on.

Given an initial point, initial trust-region radius , acceptance parameter , and trust-region update thresholds . Solve the surrogate subproblem within the trust region to obtain an update step. Then compute the ratio of actual decrease to predicted decrease, which is typically no greater than 1. If the ratio exceeds the acceptance parameter, the step is accepted; otherwise, the point is not updated. Additionally, if the ratio is too small, the surrogate approximation is poor and the trust-region radius is shrunk. If the ratio exceeds a threshold, the approximation is good and the radius is expanded.

Trust-region methods are especially suitable for nonconvex problems: the trust region makes optimization steps more robust and less dependent on the starting point. They are more stable than line-search methods: line search first determines a direction and then a step size, whereas trust-region methods determine both simultaneously. However, solving the subproblem is computationally expensive (iteratively solving a constrained optimization problem).

### 2. Filled Function Method

For unconstrained continuous optimization, construct an auxiliary function $P(x)$ from the current local minimizer $x_k$ such that $x_k$ is a maximizer of $P$, while any point lower than $x_k$ is a minimizer of $P$. By locally minimizing $P(x)$, one can enter another basin lower than the current one; then re-minimize the original function $f(x)$ in the new basin to obtain a lower local minimizer. This process repeats until the filled function can no longer produce descent points. In theory, this allows jumping over low valleys, but constructing such a filled function requires tuning several parameters, and solving for the global optimum of the auxiliary function is itself a global optimization problem. Convergence proofs typically require the objective function to have only finitely many local minima and to satisfy certain smoothness conditions.

The earliest classical form (Ge, 1990) is:

$$
P(x; r, \rho) = \frac{1}{r + f(x)} \exp\left(-\frac{\|x - x_k\|^2}{\rho^2}\right)
$$

where: $r > 0$, satisfying $r + f(x_k) > 0$, usually taken as a small positive number; $\rho > 0$ controls the exponential decay range — too large makes the exponential term too flat, while too small makes $P$ change significantly only near $x_k$, making escape difficult. Typically tuned by experimentation, with $r = \max(0, -f(x_k)) + \epsilon$ or simply $r=1$.

Another common form:

$$
P(x) = -\bigl[ f(x) - f(x_k) \bigr] \exp\left(-\frac{\|x - x_k\|^2}{\rho}\right)
$$

This function attains a maximum value of $0$ at $x_k$, and is negative in regions where $f(x) < f(x_k)$; therefore, minimizing $P$ is likely to find a lower value of the original function than the current point.

Minimization of the original function $f$ typically uses quasi-Newton methods (e.g., BFGS/L-BFGS) or derivative-free methods (such as those in [Zero-Order Optimization (I): Overview and Model-Free Methods](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1903595957171513009)). Minimization of the filled function $P$ also employs similar methods, but since $P$ can be ill-conditioned, more robust line searches are sometimes used. The process terminates if multiple consecutive (e.g., $2n+1$ directions) filled-function minimizations fail to find a lower point, or if a preset limit on the number of local minima is reached.

There are also variants such as adaptive filled functions, which automatically adjust $r,\rho$ during iteration based on current information, and parameter-free filled functions that eliminate the exponential term, e.g., $P(x) = -\|x - x_k\|^2 \cdot \max(0, f(x) - f(x_k))$ or $P(x) = - \|x - x_k\|^2 \bigl[ f(x) - f(x_k) \bigr]$, avoiding the need to tune $\rho$. Filled functions can also be combined with trust regions, using trust-region constraints to control step size and improve numerical stability.

### 3. Tunneling Method

The tunneling method was proposed by Levy and Montalvo. It also starts from a local minimizer $x_k$, but aims to directly solve the nonlinear equation $T(x) = 0$, where $T$ is a "tunneling function" such that: $x_k$ is a zero of $T(x)$; near $x_k$, $T$ can tunnel through to another point satisfying $f(x) \le f(x_k)$; the new point serves as the starting point for the next phase of local minimization.

A common form is:

$$
T(x) = \frac{f(x) - f(x_k)}{\|x - x_k\|^{2\lambda}}
$$

where $\lambda > 0$, typically $\lambda = 1$ or $2$. Increasing $\lambda$ makes $T$ flatter near $x_k$ and faster-varying farther away, helping escape; however, too large a value causes numerical instability. The denominator removes the singularity at $x_k$, making $T$ well-defined near $x_k$ and its zero findable.

Sometimes $T(x) = f(x) - f(x_k)$ is used, possibly with a regularization term added. Near $x_k$, $T$ may be very flat, requiring special handling.

Newton's method or quasi-Newton methods are commonly used to solve $T(x)=0$. Alternatively, one can fix a direction $d$ and use one-dimensional root finding to determine the step size. Since $T$ may have multiple zeros and singularities, the iteration must start from a point close to but not equal to $x_k$. Since $x_k$ itself is a zero, a small offset $\Delta$ is added to the initial step, and solutions with $\|x - x_k\| < \epsilon$ are excluded. $T$ may have a repeated zero at $x_k$ with the derivative tending to zero, slowing Newton's method. [Regularization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AD%A3%E5%88%99%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) or increasing $\lambda$ can help. Multi-start strategies with random perturbations can also be incorporated to improve the algorithm, but solving for the zeros of the tunneling function is not robust, especially in high dimensions, where the computational cost is substantial.

## 8. Multi-Stage Decomposition

For **dynamic** or **sequential decision** problems, the decisions along the time horizon are decomposed into a series of stages. Furthermore, any **combinatorial optimization** problem can be reformulated as a multi-stage decision problem ([there are quite a few papers now on reinforcement learning + combinatorial optimization (dozens or more), but the methods seem to fall into just a few categories—what do you think?](https://www.zhihu.com/question/406601430/answer/1347077532)). Many process systems engineering problems, such as heat exchanger networks and batch scheduling, commonly employ multi-stage decomposition. Although general-purpose solvers do not explicitly use it, this remains an important strategy for solving large-scale dynamic optimization problems. However, since this topic is closely related to [motion planning](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%90%E5%8A%A8%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity), it will be discussed in detail in other articles; here I will keep it brief.

### 8.1 Dynamic Programming (DP)

Multi-stage decision problems are decomposed into a nested sequence of single-stage subproblems, solved backward via the Bellman equation. This approach applies to problems with **[optimal substructure](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=281685503&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E5%AD%90%E7%BB%93%E6%9E%84&zhida_source=entity)**, such as time-ordered decision making.

Let the system state be $s_t$, the decision be $a_t$, the stage reward be $r_t(s_t,a_t)$, and the state transition be $s_{t+1}=T(s_t,a_t)$. The overall objective is to maximize/minimize $\sum_{t=0}^{N} r_t$. The value function is: $V_t(s_t) = \max_{a_t} \left\{ r_t(s_t,a_t) + V_{t+1}(T(s_t,a_t)) \right\}.$ The terminal condition is $V_{N+1}(s)=0$ (or given). The Bellman equation is solved via **backward induction** to obtain the optimal policy.
