——————————————————————

First, let me say how analysis feels different from algebra, in my experience.

Along my path as a mathematics enthusiast, the analysis subjects I've encountered are mathematical analysis and complex analysis. On the geometry side, I've also touched differentiable manifolds (real) and a bit of set-theoretic topology. With manifolds I've tasted the beauty of geometry, but since I only know the surface, I'll leave that aside. Of the first two, as most people would agree, complex analysis is quite beautiful if you don't force the very heavy theorems; mathematical analysis, by contrast, has that very traditional analytic flavor.

My view aligns with that of many working mathematicians: analysis is a "hard" subject, while algebra is "soft." To put it in my own words: analysis is like a beast struggling through dense undergrowth—occasionally you stumble onto a hidden clearing like complex analysis, but you still feel every step of the toil. Algebra, on the other hand, is like an ape swinging through the high canopy—you may get tangled in a vine now and then, but your sights are set on the sky, and there's always a sense of soaring thought. In short, analysis is more about solid, down-to-earth computation, while algebra emphasizes conceptual understanding; both have their own distinctive beauty, and neither is superior.

That said, analysis isn't entirely "hard"—there are soft results in it as well. The hard/soft dichotomy in mathematics can be searched on Zhihu.

——————————————————————

Mathematical analysis, as a foundational course for mathematics majors and for other programs with serious mathematical requirements, needs no defense of its importance. In this article, I want to try to explain my understanding of mathematical analysis from a perspective more suited to mathematics proper, and then, as usual, give brief reviews of books I've read.

*Note added later: For some reason, whenever I write about mathematical analysis, I end up digging deeper and deeper, and I can never sustain a single main thread the way I could with advanced algebra. That itself says something about the difference between analysis and algebra—and also about my own limited skill. Please bear with me.*

——————————————————————

Mathematical analysis is, in content, analysis in Euclidean space, from one dimension to *n* dimensions (not infinite-dimensional). Euclidean space is the direct sum—or Cartesian product—of copies of the real field **R**. From another angle, Euclidean space is a real inner product space, a metric space with the Euclidean metric; this is our object of study.

Our first task is to define our object and study its properties. That's why real numbers come first in mathematical analysis. In primary and secondary school, we simply used the properties of real numbers as "axioms"; now we must define them. To begin with, the theory of the reals is no trivial matter—it's deeply intertwined with algebra. It includes the definition of real numbers, their completeness, and their properties. This is why I always say that beginners shouldn't get bogged down in real-number theory; absorb it slowly.

There are four standard ways to define the reals, each with its own pros and cons:

1. **Infinite decimal expansions**
2. **Dedekind cuts**
3. **Cantor's fundamental sequences**
4. **The axiomatic method**

Personally, I like the axiomatic method for its directness—it gets you to the real field quickly—while also admiring the elegance of Dedekind cuts.

The completeness of the real number system is captured by seven equivalent theorems: (1) the supremum existence theorem, (2) the nested interval theorem, (3) the monotone convergence principle, (4) the Cauchy convergence criterion, (5) the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, (6) the accumulation point theorem, and (7) the Heine–Borel (finite open cover) theorem. These seven are mutually derivable.

Next come the algebraic properties of the reals and the Archimedean property.

As is well known, the Bourbaki school views mathematics as organized around three main structures: field structure, order structure, and topological structure. The reals carry all three: we've mentioned the field structure; there's also the order structure (inequalities) and the topological structure (open sets, neighborhoods, and all that follows).

In the end, we obtain the reals: a complete, totally ordered field.

Once the reals are defined, we can use their topology to define limits for sequences and functions. Limits are the cornerstone of calculus. From Newton to Weierstrass, the notion of limit was progressively rigorized; eventually people escaped the shadow of infinitesimals and the *second crisis in mathematics*, building a firm foundation with the ε–N and ε–δ language. As we'll see later, the same idea of limit generalizes to more abstract spaces—only the details of the language change.

With limits in hand, we turn to the search for invariance.

Invariance of limits under function maps is *continuity*. We study continuous functions on closed intervals because they attain extrema, are bounded, have the intermediate value property, and are uniformly continuous—all stemming from the presence of boundary in closed sets. In Euclidean space, closed and bounded is equivalent to compact (extendable to finite-dimensional normed spaces), and compact sets are the "good" closed sets. Continuous functions preserve closed sets but not open ones; add monotonicity, though, and a monotone continuous function preserves open sets too. Here we see that monotonicity guarantees extrema are attained only at the boundary.

Uniform continuity is "nice" continuity: it means the function isn't too steep. Every continuous function on a closed interval is uniformly continuous, while a uniformly continuous function on an *open* interval is bounded—no closedness needed. So on bounded intervals, uniform continuity preserves boundedness, and if we extend to the closed interval, we get extrema. Moreover, like many other notions, uniform continuity is a condition that allows interchanging operations. We see again: what properties are preserved under operations, and when can we exchange limits—these are the perennial questions.

Now for the real calculus.

We introduce derivatives, differentials, and the various rules of differentiation. Most people, though outwardly agreeing with their teachers that *dy/dx* is just notation, secretly want to treat it as a fraction because of the invariance of the first differential form, and they find the definition of the differential somewhat murky. In fact, digging into this can lead all the way to differentiable manifolds. My own view: in a first analysis course, use the expedient exposition that spells out the map relation between *dy* and *dx* (Chen Tianquan, I believe, does something like this). The invariance of differential forms genuinely simplifies our computations, without leaving beginners bewildered or feeling the definition is ad hoc. Also worth noting: differentiation as an operator is linear and satisfies the Leibniz (derivation) law.

Finally, following the trail Fermat's theorem → Rolle's theorem → Lagrange's mean value theorem → Cauchy's mean value theorem, we arrive at the summit of differential calculus: Taylor's formula. (Calling it a summit is no exaggeration—I may return to this in a future "gleanings" post.) Derivatives and mean value theorems have a wealth of applications.

Next comes single-variable integral calculus. We first define the indefinite integral as the inverse operation of differentiation; the integral operator is therefore linear. The chain rule gives integration by substitution, and the product rule gives integration by parts. Incidentally, integration by parts is the continuous analogue of Abel's summation formula.

Then comes the definite integral. Most analysis texts cover only the Riemann integral and defer Lebesgue integration to real analysis. The point of the definition is to understand the mechanism of partitions and Riemann sums; the partition technique also shows up in many proof problems, and the Darboux sum definition helps in understanding upper and lower limits of sequences. The most important part of the definite integral is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus—that is, the study of integrability. Typical analysis books (like Chen Jixiu's, which I read) introduce Darboux sums first and then use them; engineering textbooks basically give a Riemann-sum definition without rigorizing it.

The FTC comes in several strengths, reflecting the ongoing quest for elegance while excluding counterexamples:

1. **Entry version:** F(x) is continuously differentiable, f(x) = F′(x). Neat and pretty, but not rigorous.
2. **Standard version:** F(x) is differentiable, f(x) is integrable, f(x) = F′(x).
3. **Refinements of the standard version:**
   - (1) F differentiable, f integrable, f = F′ except at finitely many points.
   - (2) F differentiable, f integrable, f = F′ except at countably many points.
   - (3) F Lipschitz continuous, f integrable, f = F′ almost everywhere. This is the strongest version achievable in Riemann integration theory, but its proof touches real analysis.
4. **Lebesgue version (strongest):** F is absolutely continuous, f is its almost-everywhere derivative. This is fully a real-analysis matter.

In the definite integral we also meet the Newton–Leibniz formula. This formula has deep content: when you later encounter Green's theorem, Gauss's theorem, and Stokes's theorem, you'll find they're all of the same family—each expresses the relation between an integral over a region and the values on its boundary.

The definite integral has, of course, extensive applications. You don't need me to say it: open any physics or engineering textbook and you'll find the familiar integral sign (though not all of those are definite integrals).

After that come improper integrals, split into infinite integrals and singular (improper at a point) integrals. These connect closely with the series theory to come, via the integral test. The convergence or divergence of an improper integral is tied to the rate at which the integrand tends to 0 or approaches the singular point; personally, I can sense this is related to the *order* (magnitude) of the function.

Series theory is a delightful topic. Beginners often feel it's somewhat disconnected from the rest of calculus—almost an island within the subject—yet it's subtly interwoven with everything else. That perception is accurate. Some texts, like Yu Pin's notes for the Qiu class, place series right after the theory of sequence limits; more conventional texts slot them in after single-variable calculus. My own inclination is to view it this way: function series are central to approximation theory (Taylor's formula is, after all, the peak of differential calculus) and lead naturally to Hilbert spaces in functional analysis, while constant-term series are the "values" of function series. The sequences we studied earlier serve as tools (partial sums) for series. And Fourier series—truly beautiful. Who would have guessed that trigonometric functions form such a wonderfully well-behaved orthogonal basis?

Then comes multivariable calculus. I prefer to see it as both a continuation and a launching pad: going from one variable to many is a natural generalization, so we should view it hand-in-hand with single-variable calculus, while remaining alert to the differences. Especially in multivariable differential calculus, the arbitrariness of approach directions produces conclusions quite unlike the one-dimensional case. Unsurprisingly, the multivariate Taylor formula is one summit of this part of the subject; the other is the implicit function theorem. Your understanding deepens considerably once you encounter the rank theorem, embedding theorems, and the like—these describe the local geometric behavior of smooth submanifolds (in Euclidean space).

Common proofs of the implicit function theorem include: elimination, extremum methods, fixed-point methods, and differential-equation methods. What they share is that they're all, at bottom, solving equations—just by different routes. The differential-equation approach requires some qualitative theory of ODEs.

Incidentally, in the multivariable setting, linear algebra starts pulling its weight; differentiation and integration become more visibly linear operators. We meet the Jacobian matrix, the Hessian, and so on. Constrained extrema also take on a new sheen on manifolds.

The multivariable viewpoint is inherently more general—one variable is just a special case—so it serves as a launchpad. Vector-valued calculus in particular is more meaningful than scalar calculus, since a vector function from ℝⁿ to ℝⁿ is itself a generalization of a scalar function ℝⁿ→ℝ. Moreover, vectors carry *orientation*, just as calculus on ℝ uses the order structure of ℝ; in higher dimensions, the analogous notion is given by orientation. These ideas extend directly to differentiable manifolds, where we discover how elegant the general conclusions are—Stokes's theorem, for instance. And here we also meet what is arguably the hardest theorem in mathematical analysis to prove: the change-of-variables formula for multiple integrals.

Most analysis texts finesse the proof with non-rigorous shortcuts. The subtlety lies in controlling the size of local pieces in a fine partition—sizes that determine whether errors can be controlled. Even when control is possible, there are several ways to handle the local problem. In full rigor, the known proofs include the "simplest diffeomorphism" method, Schwartz's method, and Lax's method, all of which draw on more advanced knowledge.

- The **simplest diffeomorphism method** reduces the diffeomorphism to its simplest form, then uses the one-dimensional change of variables and induction; the key is to make the reduction legitimate and ensure the resulting object exists.
- **Schwartz's method** is more modern, but often invokes the sup norm and the finite-increment theorem.
- **Lax's method** is more recent: using line integrals and Green's theorem to connect multiple integrals with boundary integrals, then inducting up to dimension *n*.

One remark: continuity can be generalized to topological spaces. Differentiability, along with its associated theorems—mean value theorem degenerating to the finite-increment theorem, Taylor's formula—extends trivially to finite-dimensional normed linear spaces. Fixed-point theorems, meanwhile, need only a complete metric space. So even within mathematical analysis one could broaden the setting; it all depends on the goals and the level of the reader.

Finally, there's the introductory harmonic-analysis material in analysis courses. Since I haven't yet studied harmonic analysis myself, I'll refrain from comment, beyond the superficial impression that the "kernel" appearing in transforms is a very worthy object of study.

*Addendum: Now that I've seen the Fourier transform, let me offer a few shallow thoughts. The journey from Fourier series to Fourier series on ℂ to the Fourier transform is thoroughly splendid. I marvel both at the discovery of this great formula and at the vistas it opens. First, the beauty of trigonometric series on ℂ—really the beauty of the basis of a function space: the tangled interplay of sin and cos becomes the harmonious family of exponentials with base e. It's as if I can see, by analogy, the role of power functions on ℝ. Meanwhile, on the transformed frequency side, one can develop a whole analysis of its own (I wonder if harmonic analysis works in this territory). This beautiful basis of an infinite-dimensional space ties us to functional analysis: the whole apparatus of that subject can be brought to bear. It's a wide-open field.*

There's also the topic of integrals depending on a parameter. I'm not very familiar with it, so I won't risk exposing my ignorance; I've seen it used in some theorem proofs recently. I've heard some math departments skip it entirely, and while parameter-dependent integrals can be converted to ordinary ones, the process gets ugly.

As you can see, in laying out this big framework I keep hinting at higher-level material. My attitude toward injecting advanced content into mathematical analysis matches some others': I don't object to introducing, especially to math majors, some ideas from later courses. But we must be careful about *motivation* and *depth*.

The motivation can't be "advanced for the sake of advanced," stuffing things into analysis arbitrarily—that just discourages students or traps them in prerequisites for too long, wasting their efficiency; in that case, better to have them meet the advanced course directly. The right motivation is either: the advanced notion genuinely clarifies something in analysis, making proofs cleaner and more transparent; or it gives students a glimpse through a small hole of how analysis sits within mathematics—since much of analysis is a special case, in Euclidean space, of things in topology and functional analysis. For math majors, it also provides an early acquaintance with later courses.

Depth matters too. My advice: don't be redundant; just enough. Also mind the *seam* between advanced and elementary material—e.g., after introducing an advanced concept, you may express it in a more elementary or even non-rigorous way (the non-rigor should be flagged; it's for readability).

As for the multiplicity of methods, each has its strengths and weaknesses. The job of a teacher—or a learner—is to sidestep those weaknesses and to absorb the techniques and ideas across methods. There's also no need to cover every method exhaustively; that could diminish efficiency.

——————————————————————

I've recently come to feel that I didn't build my foundation in analysis solidly enough the first time, and I'm planning to go through it again over the break. From what I've said, I think you can appreciate that a foundational subject like analysis is never truly "finished" in one pass. After encountering more advanced topics, we inevitably return, and then the text reads fresh each time.

For a beginner of average aptitude, there's no need to make life hard by picking Rudin or Zorich. Instead, choose one main text per stage, with several auxiliary references. For newcomers, books like Chen Jixiu's, Shi Jihuai's, or Zhang Zhusheng's are quite friendly. Among foreign options, Courant's *Introduction to Calculus and Analysis* is well regarded (someone I know read it and praised it highly). Tao's *Analysis* is also, I hear, a hands-on, from-the-natural-numbers approach worth reading. Mei Jiaqiang's book, closely tied to geometry, is worth consulting as well. If any of these come with video lectures, even better for those who like a teacher to walk them through. For a second or third pass, choose according to your own situation: Chen Tianquan, Zorich, Rudin, Yu Pin, Xie Xilin, and other advanced materials are all options.

Here I've also hinted at a broader philosophy: learning must be tailored to your own circumstances. How strong is your desire? How high are your aims? Where do you position yourself?—all of these shape your plan.
