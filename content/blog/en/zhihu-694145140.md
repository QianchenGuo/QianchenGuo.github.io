Before discussing observers on Lie groups, this article introduces the multiplicative extended Kalman filter (MEKF), which is used for nonlinear observation problems. Professor Jin's earlier article is a useful reference:

[Invictus: A Very Simple Introduction to the Principles of Kalman Filters (Observers)](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/42390886)

For a nonlinear system on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$:

$$\dot{x}=f(x,u,t),\qquad y=h(x,u,t)$$

The standard procedure for designing a state observer is to copy the dynamics and add a feedback correction term, where $K(t)$ is to be designed. In practice there are uncertain disturbance signals, but they are omitted here for clarity.

$$\dot{\hat{x}}=f(\hat{x},u,t)+K(t)\left[h(\hat{x},u,t)-h(x,u,t)\right]$$

For the error we obtain an error dynamics, and we linearize it locally by computing its Jacobian:

$$\dot{\tilde{x}}=(A(t)+K(t)C(t))\tilde{x}+O(\|\tilde{x}\|),\qquad A(t)=D_x f(\hat{x}(t),u(t),t),\ C(t)=D_x h(\hat{x}(t),u(t),t)$$

What remains is to design $K$ so that the error dynamics is stabilized at the origin. We apply Kalman-style observation to this linearized error system. There will, of course, be a linearized disturbance term, but it can be omitted: stabilization already provides bounded-disturbance rejection, as explained in the reference above.

The error dynamics evolves in a relatively small region, which makes local linearization plausible. This is the basic idea behind the multiplicative EKF for nonlinear systems.

For notational simplicity, we rewrite the linear error dynamics as the linear system to be observed:

$$\dot{\hat{x}}=A(t)\hat{x}+B(t)u+\delta(t),\qquad \hat{y}=C(t)\hat{x}$$

We then perform optimal observation. In the observer dynamics, with disturbances omitted, $\delta(t)$ is the relaxation or feedback correction term $v(t)$.

The optimal solution minimizes the following performance index:

$$J\big(\hat{x}(0),\delta(\cdot)\big)=\big(\hat{x}(0)-x_0\big)^{\top}P_0\big(\hat{x}(0)-x_0\big)+\int_0^{T}\left[\delta^{\top}Q\delta+u^{\top}Uu\right]dt$$

There are two classical ways to solve this optimal control problem. The calculus-of-variations approach is more involved and less common; the dynamic-programming approach is simpler and more frequently used. Either way, one obtains the following Riccati equation:

$$\dot{P}=A(t)P+PA^{\top}(t)-PC^{\top}(t)Q(t)C(t)P+U(t)$$

Here $Q$ and $U$ are the weighting matrices in the performance index; $Q$ is positive semidefinite, and $U(t)\ge v_{m}I$ is positive definite. In addition, a uniform observability condition must hold. Let $\Phi(t,s)$ denote the state-transition matrix of the linear error dynamics, i.e., the linear system to be observed. The following inequalities must be positive definite over a window:

$$\frac{1}{T}\int_{t-T}^{t}\Phi^{\top}(s,t)U(s)\Phi(s,t)\,ds>p_u I,\qquad \frac{1}{T}\int_{t-T}^{t}\Phi^{\top}(s,t)C^{\top}(s)Q(s)C(s)\Phi(s,t)\,ds>p_q I$$

Roughly speaking, the first inequality ensures that the Riccati equation receives sufficient persistent excitation; the second ensures uniform observability. When $U$ and $Q$ are positive definite, these conditions are easy to satisfy.

Using standard optimal-control techniques, one can show that the Riccati equation admits a solution $P(t)$ whose eigenvalues are bounded, with $p_{m}$ and $p_{M}$ positive constants:

$$p_{m}I\le P(t)\le p_{M}I$$

This guarantees convergence of the observer error dynamics. We can define the Lyapunov function

$$V(x)=x^{\top}P^{-1}(t)x$$

Using the $P^{-1}$ relation, the derivative of $V$ is first dominated by a linear term involving $U(t)$. If $U$ is only positive semidefinite, we obtain at best asymptotic stability. If the uniform observability condition also holds, the same optimal-control techniques show that $\dot{V}$ is dominated by a constant related to the bounds above, yielding exponential stability. If $U$ is positive definite, the uniform observability condition is automatically satisfied, and exponential stability follows.

The gain $K(t)$ is then determined by:

$$K(t)=k(t)P(t)C^{\top}(t)Q(t),\qquad k(t)\ge 0.5$$

The standard Kalman filter corresponds to $k=1$.

It is worth emphasizing that most online material presents the Kalman filter in a probabilistic framework, yet often avoids convergence analysis and only vaguely assumes sufficient observability. This is not to say the probabilistic framework is wrong. It is more involved; one can show that bounded disturbances lead to bounded estimation errors, with a final bound determined by the disturbance bound and a constant controlled by the Lyapunov function. In other words, the essential behavior depends only on disturbance bounds, not on the statistical properties of the noise.

The probabilistic presentation can also mislead beginners and even practitioners into focusing on optimality while neglecting the convergence of the observer. In some places one only mentions convergence of the estimated distribution, which does not imply convergence of the estimate itself. In the probabilistic framework, $U$ and $Q$ are related to noise covariance matrices. For stability, however, one should not set the nominally positive definite $U$ to zero regardless of the statistical properties of the noise; otherwise the linear term in the Lyapunov derivative vanishes, and the sign of the derivative is dominated by nonlinear terms, making negative definiteness impossible to guarantee.

On large-scale maps, the EKF fails because landmarks are observed only intermittently, so the persistent-excitation condition is absent. A memoryless observer such as the Kalman filter requires this condition for input-to-state stability under disturbances; otherwise, even small disturbances can cause large errors. When the error becomes large, the linearization assumption behind the EKF breaks down, which leads to divergence. Graph-based and submap methods are far more effective in this setting.

This also explains why the eigenvalues of $P$ should remain positive and bounded when the EKF converges. If the eigenvalues of $P$ become unbounded or negative, something is wrong; this is usually caused by failure of the two uniform observability conditions and has nothing to do with whether the noise is Gaussian, a random process, or even present at all.
