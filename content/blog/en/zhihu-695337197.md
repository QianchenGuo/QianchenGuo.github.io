The main result is an observer on the Lie group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ with almost-global asymptotic convergence. The previous article serves as background:

[Extended Kalman Filtering Without Probabilistic Assumptions](/en/blog/zhihu-694145140)

The standard EKF relies on local linearization and therefore works only locally; it may fail over a large range. The result in this article observes the nonlinear system directly and does not rely on linearization.

## Problem Formulation

This is one of the basic problems in visual localization and navigation. It addresses pose estimation in SLAM, VIO, and VIM. The EKF and its variants have become common front-end pose estimators in SLAM.

We have a robot, which could be a UAV, a ground vehicle, or another type, equipped with a monocular camera and an IMU. The IMU provides angular velocity and acceleration in the body frame.

> **I. Robot Kinematics**

![Kinematics diagram](https://pica.zhimg.com/v2-64251b7cc12757954f0b5a4438ff8248_r.jpg)

Here $R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is the rotation matrix, $p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ the position, $\omega$ the angular velocity, $v$ the velocity, $e_{3}=(0,0,1)^{\top}$, and $g\in\mathbb{R}$ the gravitational acceleration in the reference frame, whose right-handed $z$-axis points downward. The cross in the lower right denotes the skew-symmetric cross-product matrix.

Note that $R^{\top}e_{3}g$ depends on the rigid-body attitude, and $R$ must be estimated. We only know the IMU measurement $a(t)$, not the actual acceleration. In many cases, however:

1. When the UAV pitch angle is small, $R^{\top}e_{3}g\approx e_{3}g$, and horizontal acceleration can be used to estimate the pitch angle, so this term is known.
2. In 3D vision, homography can approximate the gravity term: it estimates the normal of the ground or sea plane, i.e., the gravity direction, from which the coordinates of $g$ in the body frame are obtained.

To simplify the problem, we assume the acceleration is fully known.

> **II. The Monocular Camera**

Using visual feature extraction, a monocular camera obtains static feature points, from tens to hundreds per frame, but without depth. Each feature point is called a landmark. As the robot moves and observes a landmark from different angles, the relative depth can be estimated from its own trajectory; together with the landmark direction, the landmark coordinates can be recovered.

At least three non-collinear static landmarks $x_i$ are needed. We can assume their absolute coordinates are known (this assumption is not necessary, as will be seen later).

The monocular camera also provides the direction of each landmark in the body frame, expressed as a unit vector $d_i$:

$$d_i=R^{\top}\frac{x_i-p}{\|x_i-p\|}$$

![Direction geometry](/blog-assets/zhihu/695337197/image-04.jpg)

We also define the projection operator.

$\Pi_{d_i}=I-d_i d_i^{\top}$

Applying it to a vector $x$ gives the projection of $x$ onto the plane normal to $d_i$. Since $x_i-p\parallel d_i$, we clearly have:

$$\Pi_{d_i}(x_i-p)=0$$

It is worth noting that this monocular setting differs from early robot SLAM. The latter does not use an IMU and involves no velocity; it uses multiview geometry to compute the geometric similarity of landmarks and pose. Because the result is only a similarity shape, there is a scale ambiguity and the depth cannot be fully recovered. In contrast, this article uses the IMU to obtain velocity or acceleration, which contains scale information, and avoids the complicated algebraic manipulations of multiview geometry.

> **III. Observer Dynamics**

1. Assume the body-frame velocity can be measured. The observer dynamics are:

$$\dot{R}=R(\omega+\omega_u)^{\times}$$

If the body-frame velocity cannot be measured but acceleration can, we have a third-order system:

$$\dot{R}=R(\omega+\omega_u)^{\times},\qquad \dot{p}=-\omega^{\times}p+\omega_u,\qquad \dot{v}=-\omega^{\times}v+a(t)$$

where

$$p_b=R^{\top}p$$

is the position coordinate in the body frame. We do not consider the reference-frame position $p$, which yields linear error dynamics. The update terms:

$$\omega_u,\quad v_u,\quad a_u$$

are used to stabilize the estimation error and are computed from the difference between the measured output and the estimated output.

For the gravity term, instead of the approximations mentioned earlier, we estimate it directly as a vector on $S^{2}$:

$$\dot{R}=R\omega_u(t)^{\times},\qquad \dot{p}=Rv_u,\qquad \dot{v}=-\omega^{\times}v+a(t)+Ra_u$$

We use $\hat{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ to estimate the gravity term. Under sufficient feature points and variation of the acceleration signal, relaxing the range from $S^{2}$ to $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ does not cause observability problems. The terms with subscript $u$ are update terms used to stabilize the estimation error:

$$\dot{R}=R(\omega+\omega_u)^{\times},\quad \dot{p}=-\omega^{\times}p+v_u,\quad \dot{v}=-\omega^{\times}v+a(t)+a_u,\quad \dot{q}=-\omega^{\times}q+q_u$$

> **IV. Errors**

1. For $R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$, one cannot subtract as in a linear space. The error is usually defined in one of the following two ways, analogous to a group difference:

$$\tilde{R}=R^{\top}\hat{R}\quad \text{or}\quad \tilde{R}=\hat{R}R^{\top}$$

When the error is zero, $R=\hat{R}$ and the output is $I$, the identity matrix. In the ordinary EKF below, we use the first definition.

2. For $p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$, we subtract directly:

$$\tilde{p}=\hat{p}-p$$

## MEKF

This is a result published in the IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control in 2017; see the second reference at the end.

Following the principles of geometric control, we first derive the error dynamics, then parametrize with quaternions, locally linearize at the origin (assuming the estimation error is small), and finally design an EKF to stabilize the error dynamics.

> **I. Error Dynamics**

The attitude error dynamics are derived as follows:

$$\dot{\tilde{R}}=-\omega^{\times}\tilde{R}+\tilde{R}(\omega+\omega_u)^{\times}$$

where we define:

$$\omega_u^{\times}=\omega^{\times}-R^{\top}\hat{\omega}_u^{\times}$$

Using quaternions, the dynamics on $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ are mapped to dynamics on $\mathbb{R}^{4}$, where the rotation angle $\lambda_0\in\mathbb{R}$ and the axis of rotation $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$. Since the quaternion is unit, $\lambda_0=1-\|\lambda\|^{2}$. Substituting to eliminate $\lambda_0$ and linearizing at the origin gives:

$$\tilde{q}=(\lambda_0,\lambda^{\top})^{\top},\qquad \tilde{R}=I+2\lambda_0\lambda^{\times}+2(\lambda^{\times})^{2}$$

$$\dot{\lambda}=\omega_u^{\times}\lambda-2\omega^{\times}\lambda+O(\|\lambda\|\|\omega_u\|)+O(\|\omega\|\|\lambda\|^2)$$

Because we use the body-frame position coordinate, the position error dynamics are already linear time-varying and require no linearization:

$$\dot{\tilde{p}}=-\omega^{\times}\tilde{p}+v_u$$

> **II. Output Equation**

Define the output as a quantity identically equal to zero:

$$y_i=\Pi_{d_i}\left(R^{\top}(x_i-p)\right)$$

The observer output is defined accordingly:

$$\hat{y}_i=\Pi_{d_i}\left(R^{\top}(\hat{x}_i-\hat{p})\right)$$

The output tracking error is obtained by subtraction:

$$\tilde{y}_i=y_i-\hat{y}_i$$

After a lengthy derivation and linearization, we obtain a linearized error dynamics and output equation, which we treat as the observed linear system and use to design a Kalman observer. This is the multiplicative extended Kalman filter. See the related article:

$$\tilde{y}_i=\Pi_{d_i}\left((R^{\top}x_i)^{\times}\tilde{\lambda}-\tilde{p}\right)+O(\|\tilde{\lambda}\|^{2})$$

## Nonlinear Observer Design

The nonlinear observer below achieves almost-global convergence when the system is sufficiently observable; the non-convergent points have measure zero in $\mathrm{SO}(3)$. Global convergence is impossible because $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is a compact Lie group on which a continuous vector field cannot be globally convergent. This result follows from the Poincare-Hopf theorem: the sum of equilibrium indices on a compact manifold equals its Euler characteristic.

The observer has the following features:

1. It is geometrically intuitive, avoiding a pile of matrix algebra.
2. Under sufficient observability and persistent excitation, it is almost-globally asymptotically stable.
3. It is a cascade observer divided into two stages.

![Cascade observer structure](/blog-assets/zhihu/695337197/image-28.jpg)

We may drop the assumption that the three landmark absolute coordinates are known. For example, if the observer is used as a SLAM back end, the standard SLAM setting has neither a reference frame nor known coordinates; the $x_i$ are not even estimable a priori.

The only requirement is that the absolute geometric relations are invariant under rotation and translation. Thus, once enough observability has been established over a past window, a sliding-window observation of past estimates can recover the coordinates of the three landmarks in the current body frame. We then treat the current time as the initial time, the current position as the origin, and the current attitude as the identity; the body-frame coordinates of $x_i$ become absolute coordinates. This is called key-frame selection in SLAM, and the current frame is used as the key frame. The key frame is then treated as the reference frame.

We therefore assume $x_i$ is known, leaving the pose estimation problem and the problem of determining the coordinates of newly observed landmarks.

> **I. Estimating Landmark Coordinates in the Body Frame**

**1. Landmark Dynamics**

Let the estimated landmark distribution match the actual one. Define the relative displacement of the landmark in the body frame:

$$z_i=R^{\top}(x_i-p)$$

Its dynamics can be derived as follows, again split into the cases where velocity is measurable and where only acceleration is measurable. It is clearly linear, so the observer dynamics are also linear:

$$\dot{z}_i=-\omega^{\times}(t)z_i-v(t)$$

$$\dot{z}_i=-\omega^{\times}(t)z_i-v(t),\qquad \dot{v}=-\omega^{\times}(t)v+a(t)$$

At the same time define the output (identically zero):

$$y_i=(I-d_i d_i^{\top})z_i=\Pi_{d_i}z_i$$

Correspondingly, the observer output is:

$$\hat{y}_i=\Pi_{d_i}\hat{z}_i$$

Note that we do not put a hat on $d_i$, because it is directly available from the monocular camera. This is crucial: it makes the output error linear time-varying:

$$\tilde{y}_i=y_i-\hat{y}_i=\Pi_{d_i}\tilde{z}_i$$

Since the error dynamics are also linear, many methods apply, such as KF and linear least squares, both of which give globally convergent estimates.

**2. A Gradient Method Based on MHE when Velocity is Measurable**

Linear least squares is actually sliding-window least squares, also called moving horizon estimation (MHE), and is very common in robot SLAM and VIM.

We introduce a special improvement over MHE, called a gradient method based on MHE. In least squares, computing the sliding-window position involves the state-transition equation, its matrix, and its integral; this method avoids that computation.

Introduce a virtual system $(R_v,p_v)$ evolving open-loop along the kinematics equation. It may diverge because of open-loop integration, but we do not care.

$$\dot{R}_v=R_v\omega(t)^{\times},\qquad \dot{p}_v=R_v v(t)$$

![Virtual landmark diagram (handwritten note)](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-c0acb78842fcfe793b13d7988aa4147d_r.jpg)

Let $x_{v,i}$ be the $i$-th virtual landmark. We require the virtual landmark coordinates in the body frame and the direction angle to be parallel, i.e., $R^{\top}(x_{v,i}-p_v)\parallel d_i(t)$ for all $t$. Thus $x_{v,i}$ must satisfy infinitely many (for all $t$) linear equations:

$$\Pi_{d_i(t)}\left(x_{v,i}-p_v(t)\right)=0,\qquad \forall t$$

We cannot solve for $t$ directly because $\Pi$ is positive semidefinite and not invertible. Standard MHE squares the expression, integrates over the window, and minimizes to obtain a linear system. Here we integrate directly: for the current time $t$, integrate the above over the window $(t-T,t)$, and a simple derivation gives:

$$\left(\int_{t-T}^{t}w(s)\Pi_{d_i(s)}\,ds\right)x_{v,i}=\int_{t-T}^{t}w(s)\Pi_{d_i(s)}p_v(s)\,ds,\qquad w(s)>0$$

Here $w$ is an adjustable weight and can be set to 1. Although $\Pi$ is positive semidefinite, under uniform observability the coefficient on the left, i.e., the integral, is positive definite for a sufficiently large window, so $x_{v,i}$ can be solved. There are two methods.

(1) Define

$$G(t)=\int_{t-T}^{t}\Pi_{d_i(s)}\,ds,\qquad G(t)x_{v,i}=\int_{t-T}^{t}\Pi_{d_i(s)}p_v(s)\,ds$$

Under uniform observability and for sufficiently large $T$, this matrix is positive definite, so $x_{v,i}$ can be solved algebraically.

(2) Alternatively, solve it iteratively by designing the following gradient-based observer:

$$\dot{x}_{v,i}=-kG(t)x_{v,i}+k\int_{t-T}^{t}\Pi_{d_i(s)}p_v(s)\,ds$$

Since $-kG(t)$ is negative definite, the system converges to the equilibrium $x_{v,i}$:

$$x_{v,i}(t)\to x_{v,i}\qquad \text{as }t\to +\infty$$

This method is equivalent to ordinary least squares but avoids some computation. There are many acceleration tricks, such as preconditioning $G(t)$. Once $x_{v,i}$ is obtained, we can compute $\hat{z}$ and obtain the error dynamics below.

**3. Landmark Error Dynamics and Stabilization**

The estimation error:

$$\tilde{z}_i=R^{\top}(x_{v,i}-p_v)-R^{\top}(x_i-p)$$

and the derived error dynamics:

$$\dot{\tilde{z}}_i=-\omega^{\times}\tilde{z}_i-kG(t)\tilde{z}_i$$

Here $G(t)$ is the observability Gramian of the system; uniform observability corresponds to it being positive definite.

Since $\omega^{\times}$ is a rotation matrix, a Lyapunov function is easy to choose:

$$V(\tilde{z}_i)=\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{z}_i\|^2$$

and:

$$\dot{V}(\tilde{z}_i)\le -2k V(\tilde{z}_i)$$

This shows exponential stability. If velocity is measurable, the observability condition holds as long as the robot does not move along a straight line directly toward $x_{v,i}$, which is easy to guarantee.

**4. When Acceleration is Measurable**

For the case where velocity is not measurable but acceleration is, we similarly introduce a virtual system:

$$\dot{R}_v=R_v\omega(t)^{\times},\qquad \dot{p}_v=R_v v(t),\qquad \dot{v}_v=-\omega^{\times}v_v+a(t)$$

Because $v$ is unknown, there is an initial velocity error

$$v_e=R_v(0)^{\top}(\hat{v}(0)-v(0))$$

which grows linearly in time, i.e., the term $v_e t_v(t)$. As in the velocity-measurable case, the virtual landmark in the body frame must be parallel to the direction measured by the monocular camera. Writing the equation at the end of the velocity error, it is linear in both $x_{v,i}$ and $v_e$:

$$\Pi_{d_i(t)}\left(x_{v,i}-p_v(t)-v_e t_v(t)\right)=0,\qquad \forall t$$

Using standard MHE, square and integrate over the window:

$$\min_{x_{v,i},\,v_e}\int_{t-T}^{t}\left\|\Pi_{d_i(\tau)}\left(x_{v,i}-p_v(\tau)-v_e t_v(\tau)\right)\right\|^{2}d\tau$$

Minimization gives the optimal estimate at time $t$:

$$\left(x_{v,i}^{*}(t),\ v_e^{*}(t)\right)$$

The velocity estimate is then obtained, and it converges to the true body-frame velocity as $t\to\infty$:

$$\hat{v}(t)=v_v(t)+R_v(t)v_e^{*}(t)$$

We must reset $p_v$ and $t_v$ to zero after a step length $T$ to keep the variables bounded.

> **II. Attitude Estimation from Known Landmarks**

![Attitude estimation diagram](/blog-assets/zhihu/695337197/image-60.jpg)

**1. Reference Vectors**

After estimating $x_i$, construct reference vectors that characterize the relative positions between landmarks. Define the reference vector in the reference frame:

$$r_i=x_{i+1}-x_i$$

and the reference vector in the body frame:

$$r_i'=R^{\top}r_i$$

Its estimate can be written as:

$$\hat{r}_i'=\hat{R}^{\top}r_i$$

and is convergent. Thus $r_i'$ can be treated as known, and the attitude $R$ is to be estimated.

**2. Observer Dynamics for $R$**

Note that even though we have:

$$r_i'=R^{\top}r_i$$

we cannot solve for $R$ algebraically because the system has noise; the result of such a solution may not lie in $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and would require additional algebraic decomposition and projection. Using an observer is therefore the simpler and better approach.

There is strong geometric meaning here: the cross product of the estimated reference vector in the absolute frame and the actual $r_i$ points along the negative rotation-error axis, which reduces the rotation error angle and drives $\hat{R}\to R$. We therefore set the update term as:

$$\omega_u=\sum_i k_i\,\hat{r}_i'\times r_i'$$

The observer dynamics are:

$$\dot{\hat{R}}=\hat{R}(\omega+\omega_u)^{\times},\qquad \hat{r}_i'=\hat{R}^{\top}r_i$$

with constants:

$$k_i,\ \ell_i>0$$

Define the attitude estimation error as follows, using a different definition from MEKF because proving convergence with the earlier definition would be difficult:

$$\tilde{R}=\hat{R}R^{\top}$$

The error dynamics are:

$$\dot{\tilde{R}}=(\omega+\omega_u)^{\times}\tilde{R}-\tilde{R}\omega^{\times}$$

Under the uniform observability condition, all landmark coordinates in the body frame converge globally, so, loosely speaking, it suffices to analyze the system after convergence (this can be made rigorous).

For the Lyapunov function, since trace and $\cos\theta$ are related, and geometrically the rotation error angle decreases, we can use trace:

$$V(\tilde{R})=\frac{1}{2}\left(3-\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{R})\right)$$

Similarly, moving $r_i$ to the origin makes the error norm decrease, so we can construct:

$$V(\tilde{R})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_i\left\|\bar{r}_i-\tilde{R}r_i\right\|^2$$

Compared with the previous one, this is only a weighted version, so we have:

$$\dot{V}(\tilde{R})\le -\sum_i \ell_i\left\|\bar{r}_i-\tilde{R}r_i\right\|^{2}<0$$

It can also be shown that the update term follows the negative gradient direction of this third Lyapunov function, and:

$$\dot{V}(\tilde{R})<0,\qquad \forall \tilde{R}\notin\{I,R_1,R_2,R_3\}$$

Here $I$ is the equilibrium of the error system; the other three $R_i$, $i=1,2,3$, are also equilibria with zero derivative. Since almost-global stability can be proved, the $R_i$ are unstable. The probability of reaching them in practice is zero, so the observer can be regarded as globally asymptotically stable in practice.

Summary of the geometric meaning: the update term has a negative projection on the rotation-error axis, and it follows the negative gradient direction of the Lyapunov function.

## Quasi-Kalman Filtering on Lie Groups

Here $r_i$ is the reference vector in the reference frame and $r_i'$ is its counterpart in the body frame. The attitude estimation in the nonlinear observer above can be solved by a Kalman observer on Lie groups.

In the references, Robert Mahony's 2012 result on $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ was generalized to general Lie groups in 2013. Here we only introduce the 2012 result.

First we need to define a Riemannian metric on $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ so that performance indices and Lyapunov functions can be defined:

$$\langle A,B\rangle_{\Omega}=\operatorname{tr}\left[A\Omega B^{\top}\right],\qquad A,B\in \mathfrak{so}(3)$$

Also define:

$$2P_s(M)=M-M^{\top},\qquad 2P_a(M)=M+M^{\top}$$

The core idea is to minimize a least-squares performance index defined on the Lie group using the metric above:

$$J(R,\delta(\cdot))=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(\|\delta\|^2+\ell\|y-\hat{y}\|^2\right)d\tau+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left[(R_0-I)K_0^{-1}(R_0-I)\right]$$

Here $\delta$ is a virtual relaxation input, a skew-symmetric angular-velocity matrix in $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, and $\operatorname{vec}\delta$ vectorizes it. Minimizing this performance index yields an HJB equation on $\mathrm{SO}(3)$:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}(R,t)+H^{\ast}(R,\operatorname{d}V(R,t);t)=0$$

The Hessian term is the inverse of the Hessian of the Lyapunov function $V$ at $\hat{R}$. Solving the HJB equation gives $V$, but it cannot be solved directly. We replace the Hessian term with an approximation and obtain a Riccati equation that can be iterated; this Riccati equation gives $V$.

Experiments in the paper show that this result outperforms the standard multiplicative EKF (MEKF): under similar noise suppression, it converges faster, because the Riccati equation expresses the weights more accurately.

Substituting and simplifying gives the observer dynamics and the iterative equation for the Hessian matrix:

$$\dot{\hat{R}}=\hat{R}\omega^{\times}+\hat{R}\left(\operatorname{vec}\left(\sum_i P_s(\hat{R}^{\top}r_i r_i^{\top})\right)\right)^{\times},\qquad \dot{Q}=A(t)Q+QA(t)^{\top}-Q\left(\sum_i P_s(\hat{R}^{\top}r_i r_i^{\top})\right)Q$$

Here $Q$ is the Hessian of $V$ and satisfies an extended Riccati equation. Compared with the previous observer, the positive scalar weights $k_i$ are replaced by the positive definite matrix $Q^{-1}$ derived from the Riccati equation, i.e., the weights are adjusted adaptively.

Although $V$ is only approximate, almost-global convergence can still be guaranteed. The vector field of the error dynamics is the negative gradient of the cost function:

$$J(R)=\sum_i\left\|r_i-\tilde{R}\bar{r}_i\right\|^2$$

Since the gradient on the Lie group depends on the inner product and hence on the metric, this agrees with the Lyapunov function obtained in the nonlinear observer section. Positive definiteness of $Q$ means the cost decreases almost globally, which proves almost-global asymptotic stability.
