## 1. Introduction to Trajectory Planning

First, we need smooth trajectories. Compared with front-end path planning, at the level of robot [motion planning](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%90%E5%8A%A8%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity), smooth trajectories imply smoother motion — the robot should not come to a full stop at corners when turning. Moreover, non-smooth paths introduce abrupt changes in high-order dynamics terms, sometimes producing singularities, which collectively leads to higher energy consumption.

Even if our front-end is a kinodynamic path planner that already satisfies dynamic constraints, we can still improve the quality of the solution through [trajectory optimization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BD%A8%E8%BF%B9%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity). Since kinodynamic path planning is a relatively global method, it is difficult to simultaneously handle time allocation once the trajectory is found, and sometimes it does not satisfy [time constraints](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E9%99%90%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity). Local methods, on the other hand, must consider not only [dynamic constraints](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6%E7%BA%A6%E6%9D%9F&zhida_source=entity) but also time and energy savings, [actuator](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%89%A7%E8%A1%8C%E5%99%A8&zhida_source=entity) limits, and task-level planning arising from mission requirements. Therefore, we should not fixate on the front-end/back-end split or cling to a particular formulation; rather, the trajectory as a whole should be refined from coarse to fine.

If a front-end path planner already exists, the back-end can also be referred to as trajectory generation. Given [boundary conditions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BE%B9%E7%95%8C%E6%9D%A1%E4%BB%B6&zhida_source=entity) for the initial and goal poses, plus intermediate conditions at waypoints (either manually specified or obtained from path planning), the trajectory must satisfy certain smoothness requirements. In the simplest case, this amounts to minimizing the rate of change of the control input.

Because a robot's online perception range is limited — especially the range over which reliable obstacle information (e.g., distance, depth) can be obtained — we adopt receding-horizon replanning: first, search for a global plan at the start of navigation, or initialize with straight-line segments; then use front-end planning to find a [local path](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B1%80%E9%83%A8%E8%B7%AF%E5%BE%84&zhida_source=entity), which can also be directly taken from the [global path](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%A8%E5%B1%80%E8%B7%AF%E5%BE%84&zhida_source=entity) if it meets the requirements; use back-end planning to generate the local trajectory; finally, use [receding-horizon](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E6%BB%9A%E5%8A%A8%E6%97%B6%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity) control to track the trajectory and replan once the horizon is executed. Note that excessively frequent replanning can degrade consistency between successive trajectories, causing left-right oscillation. We may also adopt an exploration strategy: sometimes the absence of a feasible solution is due to [vision sensor](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%A7%86%E8%A7%89%E4%BC%A0%E6%84%9F%E5%99%A8&zhida_source=entity) noise causing mapping errors; in such cases, a more aggressive approach is to generate safe, short trajectories in the surrounding area to observe the environment and cover it.

**This article focuses on the application of [polynomial trajectories](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%9A%E9%A1%B9%E5%BC%8F%E8%BD%A8%E8%BF%B9&zhida_source=entity) in specific scenarios; subsequent articles will further address other topics in trajectory planning.**

## 2. Differential Flatness (Quadrotor Example)[\[1\]](#ref_1)

Quadrotors and several other classic UAVs, including some simplified car models, possess the property of differential flatness. Differential flatness means that the system states and control inputs can be expressed as functions of the flat output variables and their derivatives in the flat output space. This allows us to plan directly in the flat output space, greatly reducing the dimensionality of the planning space.

It has been proven that for an underactuated system such as a UAV, one can design a geometric tracking controller on SE(3). Any smooth trajectory generated in the flat output space can be tracked, provided that the states and their derivatives are bounded. This also implies that the control of quadrotors and similar UAVs can be addressed satisfactorily.

Below, we show that for a quadrotor, a flat output state set can be chosen as xyz plus the [yaw angle](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=yaw%E8%A7%92&zhida_source=entity).

Note: the total thrust of a quadrotor is always perpendicular to the propeller plane, meaning the UAV can only generate force along the z-axis of its [body frame](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%93%E5%9D%90%E6%A0%87%E7%B3%BB&zhida_source=entity). Clearly, a quadrotor is an underactuated system, which implies that its attitude and acceleration are tightly coupled (there is a one-to-one mapping between directions). During planning, one may choose either acceleration or attitude, but since attitude involves the [rotation matrix](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%8B%E8%BD%AC%E7%9F%A9%E9%98%B5&zhida_source=entity) — a strongly [nonlinear](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) factor — acceleration is generally chosen for planning. Furthermore, the quadrotor is highly symmetric: the body is invariant under horizontal rotation, so the yaw angle can be treated as independent of the forces and torques.

## 3. Polynomial Trajectory Generation

For UAVs, polynomial trajectories have the following advantages: polynomials are composed of [power functions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B9%82%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) of various orders, making it convenient to specify smoothness constraints and [order relationships](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B9%82%E6%AC%A1%E5%85%B3%E7%B3%BB&zhida_source=entity); derivatives are easy to compute, with [closed-form solutions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%97%AD%E5%BC%8F%E8%A7%A3&zhida_source=entity); trajectories in different dimensions can be planned in a decoupled manner, since high-dimensional polynomials can be parameterized by one-dimensional polynomials. Polynomials are not universal, however — they are optimal only for simple integrator-chain models and the single-square control input problem (i.e., where the [cost function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E4%BB%B7%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) is the [integral of the squared](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B9%B3%E6%96%B9%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86&zhida_source=entity) control input). When the problem formulation changes, polynomials become merely a degenerate, suboptimal case. Nevertheless, in many situations, polynomials offer sufficient convenience across various aspects that suboptimality is acceptable.

### (2) Minimum Snap[\[2\]](#ref_2)

Below, we use minimum snap as an example to formulate polynomial trajectory planning as a [QP problem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=QP%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity). This was first published in 2011 by the GRASP Lab at the University of Pennsylvania, led by Vijay Kumar.

![Handwritten note](/blog-assets/zhihu/15266756391/image-08.jpg)

For a QP problem, we can derive a closed-form solution, but some details require attention. When optimizing the [polynomial coefficients](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%9A%E9%A1%B9%E5%BC%8F%E7%B3%BB%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) of each segment, since the polynomial coefficients have no clear physical meaning, high-order terms in t become very large when t is large, which tends to drive the corresponding high-order coefficients to very small values — a [singular value](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A5%87%E5%BC%82%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity) — introducing numerical instability. When optimizing derivative constraints instead, since these have [physical meaning](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%89%A9%E7%90%86%E6%84%8F%E4%B9%89&zhida_source=entity), velocity and acceleration do not exhibit singular values provided the time intervals are chosen appropriately.

Adopting the common "scaling" technique in optimization, we transform the optimization variables from polynomial coefficients to derivative constraints at each point, and use a selection matrix C to separate fixed variables from free variables. The closed-form solution then becomes straightforward to express:

![](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-7287e5e306982469f68a7a366de1ecb9_r.jpg)

In the original minimum snap paper, the authors used [RRT*](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=RRT%2A&zhida_source=entity) for the front-end and trajectory generation for the back-end. Experiments showed that this approach is faster and produces higher-quality results than kinodynamic RRT*. The significance of this fast trajectory generation method lies in its ability to guarantee arrival at waypoints at specified times and closed-form derivatives at any time instant in simple environments, with the assistance of front-end planning. Furthermore, through convex optimization solvers or closed-form solutions, trajectory generation can be accomplished with modest computational cost.

### (III) Optimization Details

The closed-form solution looks elegant in theory, but it is not always the better choice. The closed-form approach involves matrix inversion, which becomes time-consuming for large matrices, and commercial [embedded](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B5%8C%E5%85%A5%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity) devices have limited computational power. In comparison, numerical solving is often more robust, especially today with the significant development of mature and stable solvers (e.g., Mosek). In most cases, using QP combined with the numerical-stability-related regularization operations just mentioned is sufficient. Furthermore, solving three small-scale problems is generally more stable and faster than solving a single large-scale problem. However, coupled solving allows different weights to be applied to the trajectories on the three axes, which is equivalent to having a higher degree of freedom. In minimum snap, the three axes are completely independent. But later we will introduce [joint optimization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%81%94%E5%90%88%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) methods.

## IV. Iterative Methods

Paths generated by front-end methods such as RRT\* and A\* being collision-free does not guarantee that the generated trajectory is also collision-free, because trajectory optimization itself adjusts within a neighborhood of the path and can exhibit [overshoot](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B6%85%E8%B0%83&zhida_source=entity). In Section III, only trajectory generation was considered, without the ability to control the trajectory shape between waypoints, and safety collision avoidance was not addressed. A naive approach is real-time [collision detection](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A2%B0%E6%92%9E%E6%A3%80%E6%B5%8B&zhida_source=entity) and replanning, which requires that the computational cost of both collision detection and planning algorithms be small enough to meet real-time requirements. The idea behind iterative methods is that since the initial path is collision-free, we only need to iteratively make the trajectory approach the original path to ensure the trajectory is collision-free.

### (I) Adding Waypoints

![Handwritten note](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-b5768e23d64a5d7ab433e7acc7c29e01_r.jpg)

One method is that when a segment collides, a new waypoint is added at the midpoint of that segment. As more waypoints are added, the trajectory becomes closer to the original path. However, too many segments also increase computational cost and make the trajectory less smooth. This is also why we do not start with many segments in the first place.

This method works well in environments where obstacles are not too dense. When obstacles are dense, too many new waypoints are added and the trajectory conforms too closely to the path. This results in kinks and sharp turns along the trajectory, where the vehicle moves very slowly due to dynamic constraints. Theoretically, adding waypoints does cause the local quality of the trajectory to degrade rapidly, because if the optimization only considers global information, the iterative local adjustments become abrupt. Moreover, there is generally no justification for adding a waypoint at the midpoint of a segment.

This trajectory generation approach relies on the "quasi-static assumption," which assumes the motion is slow enough to be considered static, or equivalently, that dynamics are ignored (only kinematics are considered), allowing the path to be tracked with arbitrarily high precision. This assumption requires that the velocity, acceleration, and all higher-order derivatives at the start and end points be zero (rest-to-rest trajectory). If this requirement is not met, the algorithm cannot guarantee that the trajectory converges to the path, and therefore cannot guarantee the safety and feasibility of the trajectory. Since adding waypoints merely brings the trajectory closer to the path, many iterations may be required, and there is no way to prove that a finite number of waypoint additions will make the trajectory collision-free.

These two major drawbacks motivate us to further explore optimization-based methods that explicitly optimize waypoints or obstacle constraints.

### (II) Iteratively Checking Extrema and Adding Constraints [\[3\]](#ref_3)

Online iterative solving (repeated QP or convex optimization) is time-consuming, and even when no feasible solution exists around the path, we may need many iterations to discover this issue. We can instead check only the extrema of the position, velocity, and acceleration polynomial curves of the trajectory, and impose new, stricter constraints at the extrema that violate the constraints.

![Handwritten note](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-d6bd9845dec2caf2580585de32ae188d_r.jpg)

Finding the extrema of a polynomial trajectory is a root-finding problem. For high-order polynomials, only numerical methods (such as [Newton's method](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Newton%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity)) are feasible. In practice, however, this is converted into an eigenvalue problem of the polynomial companion matrix, a more mature approach that is used in MATLAB's `roots` function.

## V. Explicit Methods

A more natural way to consider obstacles is to transform them into a [feasible region](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%AF%E8%A1%8C%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity) problem for the optimization variables, and then into optimization constraints.

### (I) Hard Constraints

**1. Corridor-based methods** [\[4\]](#ref_4)

In complex environments, safe regions are found and represented as a union of convex sets. Within the convex corridor, hard constraints are imposed on the optimization to keep the trajectory inside the convex coverage.

Specifically, an [octree map](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%AB%E5%8F%89%E6%A0%91%E5%9C%B0%E5%9B%BE&zhida_source=entity) can first be used to detect obstacles; then a graph [search algorithm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%90%9C%E7%B4%A2%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) is used to find a feasible path in the octree map, which naturally forms a corridor. Since octree partitioning may result in corridors that are not large enough, each voxel in the corridor must be inflated, and duplicate voxels removed. This ultimately yields a convex corridor with a reduced number of nodes, each having maximum volume. After inflation, each node in the corridor becomes a cube. To maintain the convexity of the optimization problem, the cubes should be made as convex as possible (of course, using an octree map with inflation along the x, y, and z axes is the simplest approach).

![Handwritten note](/blog-assets/zhihu/15266756391/image-10.jpg)

Subsequently, the trajectory optimization problem is solved within the corridor (no waypoints are needed at this point), by simply converting the feasible region of the corridor into safety constraints (inequality constraints) in the optimization. Specifically, this includes the start and end state constraints $Ap=b$, intermediate waypoint state constraints $Ap=b, Ap\leq b$, and continuity constraints $Ap_{i}=Ap_{i+1}$ — three point constraints — as well as boundary constraints $A(t)p\leq b, \forall t\in\left[ t_{l},t_{r} \right]$ and dynamic constraints (velocity and acceleration bounds) $A(t)p\leq b, \forall t\in\left[ t_{l},t_{r} \right]$ — two interval constraints (handling what the point constraints cannot). Interval constraints cannot be directly imposed on the optimization problem. One approach is to [discretize](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A6%BB%E6%95%A3%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) the parameterized constraints in time, but that leads to excessively high constraint dimensions. Alternatively, the waypoints between segments can be fixed within the overlapping region of adjacent boxes, though this method still cannot guarantee that the entire trajectory lies within the bounding boxes. A simpler approach is the one in Section IV(II), which ignores interval constraints initially and performs a posteriori checks. In short, there is no particularly satisfactory solution.

The advantages of the above scheme are: front-end path search is more efficient because it is performed on a simplified graph constructed from the octree; the back-end solves a convex optimization problem in a convex corridor quickly; and the convex corridor provides some degrees of freedom, such as implicit time allocation (see Section VII(II)), leading to better solutions. The drawback is that interval constraints cannot be handled well, which can be addressed by iteratively checking extrema and adding extra constraints as described in Section IV(II).

![Handwritten note](/blog-assets/zhihu/15266756391/image-12.jpg)

Implicit time allocation

**2. Dense constraints** [\[5\]](#ref_5)

The cube size is first determined, and obstacles are inflated according to the cube size before path planning. The path is discretized according to certain rules to obtain discrete [timestamps](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E6%88%B3&zhida_source=entity), and a large number of bounding boxes are added at different timestamps, ensuring significant overlap between adjacent boxes. This divides the path into many dense short segments. Simultaneously, the robot's velocity and acceleration are controlled to ensure it does not move too far in a short time, and it can be proven that a bounded piecewise-constant acceleration between timestamps will not exceed the safe range of the bounding boxes. Ultimately, solving a single QP yields the solution.

![Handwritten note](/blog-assets/zhihu/15266756391/image-14.jpg)

The discretization step size and cube size are parameters that need to be set, and their values are related to the velocity and acceleration limits. The detailed derivation can be found in the paper.

The drawback of this method is that the generated trajectory is always overly conservative, and the robot can only move at very low speeds. The advantage, however, is that although the dense constraints may appear to increase computational cost, in reality each segment only requires solving a single-variable optimization, and the sparse problem structure guarantees real-time performance with theoretical guarantees that the trajectory remains within the corridor.

**3. Barrier function methods (interior point methods)**

**4. Mixed-integer optimization**

This is generally used in formation obstacle avoidance; for a single robot it is rarely necessary. This approach reveals the NP-hard complexity arising from the non-convexity and [discreteness](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A6%BB%E6%95%A3%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) (discontinuity) of obstacles.

$$
\forall t_{k}, \\x_1(t_k)-x_2(t_k)\leq d_x, or\ x_2(t_k)-x_1(t_k)\leq d_x,or\  y_1(t_k)-y_2(t_k)\leq d_y,or\  y_2(t_k)-y_1(t_k)\leq d_y,or\  z_1(t_k)-z_2(t_k)\leq d_z,or\  z_2(t_k)-z_1(t_k)\leq d_z
$$

To maintain a safe distance d from the "obstacle" (coordinate subscript 2, which in the original paper is another robot), the safe region outside is non-convex. The logical selection of collision avoidance can be translated into mathematical language using the Big-M method, effectively rewriting "or" into "and," thereby turning it into a mixed-integer optimization. The complexity grows exponentially with the number of integer variables. When an integer variable is zero, the corresponding constraint is activated, and the subsequent trajectory optimization becomes a mixed-integer QP problem.

$$
\forall t_{k}, \\x_1(t_k)-x_2(t_k)-c_0M\leq d_x,x_2(t_k)-x_1(t_k)-c_1M\leq d_x, y_1(t_k)-y_2(t_k)-c_2M\leq d_y,y_2(t_k)-y_1(t_k)-c_3M\leq d_y, z_1(t_k)-z_2(t_k)-c_4M\leq d_z,z_2(t_k)-z_1(t_k)-c_5M\leq d_z,\\\sum_{i=0}^{5}{c_i}=5,M=100000,c_i\in\{0,1\}
$$

Solving this is very slow and cannot achieve online real-time performance.

### (II) Soft Constraints

Hard constraints partition the environment into safe and obstacle regions. Treating safe regions as equivalent can lead to situations where the robot is in a safe zone, but due to being too close to an obstacle, there is a collision risk in actual operation caused by [control precision](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%B2%BE%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity) and environmental uncertainty. In other words, hard-constrained optimization is less robust to disturbances, noise, and degradation of corridor quality. Soft constraints, on the other hand, incorporate the distance to obstacles more naturally into the optimization. Each obstacle in the environment provides distance gradient information, similar to an [artificial potential field](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BA%BA%E5%B7%A5%E5%8A%BF%E5%9C%BA&zhida_source=entity). Additionally, to ensure the smoothness of the generated trajectory, cost terms such as dynamic feasibility are added to the [objective function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%AE%E6%A0%87%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity). The final result is a weighted sum of a cost function and a [penalty function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BD%9A%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity). Of course, soft-constrained optimization cannot guarantee that constraints are always satisfied, especially when the cost function is poorly designed.

Common penalty functions, depending on the norm, include the L2 penalty, the L1 penalty, and the Huber penalty (commonly used in [SLAM](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=SLAM&zhida_source=entity); it is a piecewise combination of L1 and L2 penalties, using L2 near the feasible region and L1 far away to prevent excessive growth).

Soft-constrained minimum snap is typically used for local online trajectory planning or replanning. Moreover, a two-step optimization strategy can be employed during optimization. First, only the collision cost is optimized to keep the trajectory as far from obstacles as possible; then time is reallocated and the trajectory is reparametrized; finally, the smoothness cost and dynamic penalty terms are jointly optimized. Experiments show that starting the optimization from a position relatively far from obstacles yields better practical results and faster convergence.

## VI. Spline Curve Optimization

Spline curves are a broad class of special polynomial curves with a series of favorable properties, making them highly suitable for considering obstacles in polynomial trajectory optimization. Both hard-constraint and soft-constraint optimization can be used. [\[6\]](#ref_6)

### (I) Bezier Curves

**1. Definition**

The basis of a polynomial is the power function $p_{\mu}(t)=p_{\mu}^{0}+p_{\mu}^{1}t+p_{\mu}^{2}t^{2}+\dots+p_{\mu}^{n}t^{n}$, whereas the basis of a Bezier curve is the Bernstein polynomial, obtained recursively via the de Casteljau algorithm (1959): $B_{\mu}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{c_{\mu}^{i}b_{n}^{i}(t)},\ b_{n}^{i}(t)=C_{n}^{i}t^{i}(1-t)^{n-i}$, where $\mu\in\left\{ x,y,z \right\}$ is the dimension component index. The coefficients of a [Bezier curve](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B4%9D%E5%A1%9E%E5%B0%94%E6%9B%B2%E7%BA%BF&zhida_source=entity) polynomial can be obtained from general polynomial coefficients via a [linear mapping](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%98%A0%E5%B0%84&zhida_source=entity). This means that all previous polynomial trajectory generation and [optimization methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) can be applied to Bezier curves. For example, for a [sixth-order polynomial](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%AD%E9%98%B6%E5%A4%9A%E9%A1%B9%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity), letting $p_{\mu}=(p_{\mu}^{0},\dots,p_{\mu}^{6})^{T},\ c_{\mu}=(c_{\mu}^{0},\dots,c_{\mu}^{6})^{T}$, we have $p=Mc$, where

$$
M=\left(\begin{array}{}\ 1&\ 0&\ 0&\ 0&\ 0&\ 0& 0\\  -6&\ 6&\ 0&\ 0&\ 0&\ 0 &0\\  \ 15&-30&\ 15&\ 0&\ 0&\ 0&0\\  -20&\ 60&-60&\ 20&\ 0&\ 0& 0\\  \ 15&-60&\ 90&-60&15&\ 0& 0\\ -6&\ 30&-60&\ 60&-30&\ 6& 0\\ \ 1&-6&\  15&-20&15&-6&1\\ \end{array}\right)
$$

The vector of basis coefficients $c^{i}=(c_{x}^{i},c_{y}^{i},c_{z}^{i})^{T},\ i=1,\cdots,n=degB$ is called the control points, whose number is $degB+1$. Unlike the coefficients $p^{i}=(p_{x}^{i},p_{y}^{i},p_{z}^{i})^{T},\ i=0,\cdots,n=degp$ of a general polynomial, these control points have physical meaning.

**2. Properties**

(1) Endpoint interpolation property: A Bezier curve always starts at the first control point and ends at the last control point, and does not pass through the other control points.

(2) Convex hull property: A Bezier curve $B(t)$ composed of a set of control points $\{c^{i}\}$ is entirely contained within the convex hull of the control points. This property is key to obstacle avoidance using Bezier curves.

(3) Derivative closure property: The derivative curve $B'(t)$ of a Bezier curve $B(t)$, called the hodograph, is also a Bezier curve. Its control points are $n(c^{i+1}-c^i), n=degB$. Similarly, the control points of $B''(t)$ are $n(n-1)(c^{i+2}-2c^{i+1}+c^{i})$. The control points of the derivative curve are [linear combinations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%BB%84%E5%90%88&zhida_source=entity) of the control points of the original curve. This property allows us to constrain any [first derivative](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%80%E9%98%B6%E5%AF%BC%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) by simply constraining the control points of the position trajectory $B(t)$.

(4) Fixed time interval: Bezier curves are always defined on [0, 1], which is somewhat undesirable and requires a [time scale](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E5%B0%BA%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity) transformation.

**3. Problem Transformation**

A convex corridor is a series of [convex polygons](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%87%B8%E5%A4%9A%E8%BE%B9%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity). Each cube corresponds to a segment of the Bezier curve. If the control points of the curve are forced to lie within the polygon, then the trajectory is completely contained within the convex hull of the control points. This guarantees that the trajectory remains entirely within the convex corridor (note that this cannot be guaranteed if the corridor contains non-convex cubes). In this case, the number of constraints equals the number of control points, i.e., the curve order plus one. Meanwhile, since the derivative control points are linear combinations of the original curve's control points, the derivative curves can also be constrained within a convex hull.

The recursive formula for the control points of different-order derivatives of an $n$-degree Bezier curve is

$$
a_{\mu j}^{0,i}=c_{\mu j}^{i},a_{\mu j}^{l,i}=\frac{n!}{(n-l)!}(a_{\mu j}^{l-1,i+1}-a_{\mu j}^{l-1,i}),l\geq1
$$

Endpoint constraints: $a_{\mu j}^{l,0}\cdot s_{j}^{(1-l)}=d_{\mu j}^{( l）}$, continuity constraints: $a_{\mu j}^{\phi,n}\cdot s_{j}^{(1-\phi)}=a_{\mu.j+1}^{\phi,0}\cdot s_{j+1}^{(1-\phi)}$, safety constraints: $\beta_{\mu j}^{-}<c_{\mu j}^{i}<\beta_{\mu j}^{+}$, and dynamic feasibility constraints:

$$
v_{m}^{-}<n(c_{\mu j}^{i+1}-c_{\mu j}^i)<v_{m}^{+},a_{m}^{-}<n(n-1)(c_{\mu j}^{i+2}-2c_{\mu j}^{i+1}+c_{\mu j}^{i})<a_{m}^{+}
$$

where $j=1,\cdots,m$ denotes the trajectory segment index, $\mu\in\{x,y,z\}$ denotes the position component, $i=0,1,\cdots,n$ denotes the control point index, $l\in\mathbb N^+,\phi\in\mathbb N$ denote the derivative order, $\beta_{\mu j}^{-}, \beta_{\mu j}^{+}$ are the bounds of the $j$-th convex polyhedron in the convex corridor, and $v_{m}^{-},v_{m}^{+},a_{m}^{-},a_{m}^{+}$ are the velocity and acceleration bounds.

### (II) [B-Spline Curves](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=252076091&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=B%E6%A0%B7%E6%9D%A1%E6%9B%B2%E7%BA%BF&zhida_source=entity)

For Bézier curves, as the number of control points increases, the trajectory order also increases, and changing a single control point affects the entire curve. This can lead to instability at higher orders. In trajectory optimization, we do not want a trajectory with too high an order (which makes it overly flexible), nor do we want a single control point's movement to alter the entire trajectory. However, if we use piecewise segments, we must introduce many equality constraints to ensure they connect end-to-end.

This is why B-spline curves, which are truly well-suited for trajectories, were found. Through the introduction of knots, the basis functions become compactly supported, which greatly benefits numerical stability and automatically satisfies the end-to-end continuity constraints.

**1. Three Key Elements and B-Spline Basis**

A B-spline is a piecewise Bézier curve that naturally ensures smoothness at the segment junctions, and does not restrict the independent variable $t$ to the interval $\left[ 0,1 \right]$. Three key elements influence its coefficients: the degree $p_{b}$, the $N+1$ control points $\left\{ \bold Q_{0},\bold Q_{1}, \cdots,\bold Q_{N}\right\},\bold Q_{i}\in \mathbb R^{3}$, and the knot vector $\left( t_{0},t_{1}, \cdots,t_{M} \right)^{T}\in \mathbb R^{M+1}，t_{i}\leq t_{i+1}$, which satisfy $M=N+p_{b}+1$. The parametric range of a B-spline is $t \in \left[ t_{p_{b}}, t_{M-p_{b}}\right]$. Why is this so? We need to examine its basis functions—the B-spline basis $N_{i,p}\left( t \right)$.

Let $\left[ t_{m}, t_{m+1}\right)$ be the $m$-th knot interval, and $N_{i,p}\left( t \right)$ be the $i$-th basis function of degree $p$, satisfying the Cox-de Boor recurrence relation (1972):

$$
N_{i,0}\left( t \right)=  \begin{cases} 1 \ \ t_{i}\leq t \leq t_{i+1} \\ 0  \ \ \text{otherwise}  \end{cases}， N_{i,p}\left( t \right)= \frac{t-t_{i}}{t_{i+p}-t_{i}}N_{i,p-1}\left( t \right)+ \frac{t_{i+p+1}-t}{t_{i+p+1}-t_{i+1}}N_{i+1,p-1}\left( t \right)
$$

**2. Properties**

(1) Control Point Influence Range & Knot Interval Correspondence

Given control points $\left\{ \bold Q_{0},\bold Q_{1}, \cdots,\bold Q_{n}\right\},\bold Q_{i}\in \mathbb R^{3}$, we can obtain a B-spline of degree $p$: $\bold C\left( t \right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{N_{i,p}\left( t \right)\bold Q_{i}}$. If a control point $\bold Q_{k}$ is changed to $\bold Q_{k}+v$, the B-spline curve becomes

$$
\tilde{\bold C}\left( t \right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{N_{i,p}\left( t \right)\bold Q_{i}+N_{k,p}\left( t \right)v} =\bold C\left( t \right)+N_{k,p}\left( t \right)v

From the "pyramid," we know that changing one control point affects only a finite, known interval—namely, the support of the basis function corresponding to that control point. Conversely, we also know that a knot interval is influenced by only a finite number of control points: $\left[ t_{m}, t_{m+1}\right)\subseteq\left[ t_{p_{b}}, t_{M-p_{b}}\right]$ is affected only by $\left\{ \bold Q_{m-p_{b}} \cdots \bold Q_{m} \right\}$.

(4) Derivative Closure Property

The first derivative of $\bold C\left( t \right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{N_{i,p}\left( t \right)\bold Q_{i}}$ is $\bold C’\left( t \right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{N_{i+1,p-1}\left( t \right)\bold V_{i}}$, and the second derivative is $\bold C''\left( t \right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}{N_{i+2,p-2}\left( t \right)\bold A_{i}}$, where

$$
\bold V_{i}=\frac{p\left( \bold Q_{i+1}-\bold Q_{i} \right)}{u_{i+p+1}-u_{i+1}},\bold A_{i}=\frac{\left( p-1 \right)\left( \bold V_{i+1}-\bold V_{i} \right)}{u_{i+p+1}-u_{i+2}}
$$

.

This requires proof by mathematical induction of:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}N_{i,p}\left( t \right)= \frac{p}{t_{i+p}-t_{i}}N_{i,p-1}\left( t \right)+ \frac{-p}{t_{i+p+1}-t_{i+1}}N_{i+1,p-1}\left( t \right)
$$

.

Based on previous calculations, for $p=1$ the above formula becomes

$$
\frac{d}{dt}N_{i,1}\left( t \right)= \frac{1}{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}N_{i,0}\left( t \right)+ \frac{-1}{t_{i+2}-t_{i+1}}N_{i+1,0}\left( t \right)
$$

which is clearly true. Assume it holds for $p-1$ where $p>1$, i.e.,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}N_{i,p-1}\left( t \right)= \frac{p-1}{t_{i+p-1}-t_{i}}N_{i,p-2}\left( t \right)+ \frac{-\left( p-1\right)}{t_{i+p}-t_{i+1}}N_{i+1,p-2}\left( t \right)
$$

For $p$, we have the recurrence relation

$$
N_{i,p}\left( t \right)= \frac{t-t_{i}}{t_{i+p}-t_{i}}N_{i,p-1}\left( t \right)+ \frac{t_{i+p+1}-t}{t_{i+p+1}-t_{i+1}}N_{i+1,p-1}\left( t \right)
$$

Then

$$
\frac{d}{dt}N_{i,p}\left( t \right)= \frac{1}{t_{i+p}-t_{i}}N_{i,p-1}\left( t \right)+ \frac{-1}{t_{i+p+1}-t_{i+1}}N_{i+1,p-1}\left( t \right)+\frac{t-t_{i}}{t_{i+p}-t_{i}}\frac{d}{dt}N_{i,p-1}\left( t \right)+\frac{t_{i+p+1}-t}{t_{i+p+1}-t_{i+1}}\frac{d}{dt}N_{i+1,p-1}\left( t \right)
$$

$$
= \frac{1}{t_{i+p}-t_{i}}N_{i,p-1}\left( t \right)+ \frac{-1}{t_{i+p+1}-t_{i+1}}N_{i+1,p-1}\left( t \right)+\frac{t-t_{i}}{t_{i+p}-t_{i}}\left(\frac{p-1}{t_{i+p-1}-t_{i}}N_{i,p-2}\left( t \right)+ \frac{-\left( p-1\right)}{t_{i+p}-t_{i+1}}N_{i+1,p-2}\left( t \right) \right)+\frac{t_{i+p+1}-t}{t_{i+p+1}-t_{i+1}}\left(  \frac{p-1}{t_{i+p}-t_{i+1}}N_{i+1,p-2}\left( t \right)+ \frac{-\left( p-1\right)}{t_{i+p+1}-t_{i+2}}N_{i+2,p-2}\left( t \right) \right)

## VII. Implementation Issues

**Initial Value Selection**

The minimum snap trajectory can be used as an initial value, which offers good smoothness but may be unsafe due to the lack of obstacle avoidance. Alternatively, the polyline obtained from path planning can serve as the initial value. Although it has poorer smoothness, it guarantees collision-freeness. For collision-free initial values, the interior point method (barrier function method) can be applied iteratively to ensure that constraints are always satisfied.

### (1) Numerical Stability

Convex optimization is, in most cases, not scale-invariant, meaning that the magnitude of the optimization variables greatly affects the optimization performance. In motion planning, this manifests in the time dimension.

The time axis can be divided into two types: the absolute time axis and the relative time axis. In the absolute time axis, time accumulates continuously across trajectory segments, which is mathematically clear, but the time values for later segments become large and significantly impact higher-order terms. In contrast, the relative time axis starts from zero for each segment, resolving the issue of large time scales on the global timeline, but it cannot handle segments that are excessively long or short.

![](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-718289eb2b3f49dbebd933b850113c19_r.jpg)

Top: relative time axis; bottom: absolute time axis.

For segments that are too long or too short, normalization can be applied. Time normalization can be performed for short segments or for every segment individually. Alternatively, a scaling factor can be added to each trajectory segment. Another approach is to normalize the problem itself: for large-scale scenarios, one can first solve a small-scale problem and then rescale it back. Both types of normalization significantly improve numerical stability.

### (2) Time Allocation

For the aforementioned time-space decoupled optimization methods, we need to allocate time to each trajectory segment. For piecewise trajectories, the time allocation per segment greatly influences the final trajectory. Allocating too much time to a segment causes trajectory distortion; appropriate allocation yields smoother trajectories; and too little time may render the trajectory infeasible.

![](https://pica.zhimg.com/v2-0e3d6d3ab61a48d9819834521e4e6530_r.jpg)

Blue trajectory: excessive time allocation; red trajectory: appropriate time allocation.

**1. Desired Trapezoidal Velocity Profile** [\[8\]](#ref_8) [\[9\]](#ref_9)

A simple approach is to prescribe a desired trapezoidal velocity profile, i.e., an "accelerate–cruise–decelerate" curve under bounded acceleration. For each segment, given the distance (or an estimate thereof), a time value can be computed using the desired average velocity as the time allocation.

This method is simple but conservative and far from optimal. It does not make the robot move quickly, and each segment appears rather rigid. Moreover, it is only applicable to proportional allocation across segments and cannot allocate the total time as a whole.

Compared to waypoint-based methods, this approach is better suited to corridor-based methods. Corridors provide redundant degrees of freedom in the solution space compared to waypoints, allowing waypoints to move within overlapping box regions. This somewhat alleviates time mismatch issues, although the tolerable mismatch has an upper bound.

**2. Iterative Numerical Solution** [\[10\]](#ref_10)

The reason minimum snap cannot solve for the optimal time in the same way as a two-point boundary value optimal control problem is that the cost matrix in the QP formulation contains T₁ through T_M. This is not a standard QP problem and is more complex to solve. In the same paper, a penalty function over the time intervals is subsequently added to the cost function.

![](https://pica.zhimg.com/v2-87961583b18b655dd9c90e9eb6cb3d46_r.jpg)

In this cost function, both the leading matrix and the penalty function contain the time intervals as optimization variables, making it a non-convex optimization problem. Furthermore, differentiating with respect to T_i yields a third-order tensor, which necessitates numerical differentiation. Gradient descent can be performed by perturbing T_i and p_j, but convergence to a good solution is not guaranteed, and the computational cost of numerical differentiation makes online dynamic adjustment impractical. In essence, the core issue is computational complexity.

Additionally, different coefficients in the penalty function lead to different results. One trick is to vary both the time proportions and the total time: first find the optimal time proportions (ratios) without considering dynamic bounds, then fix the inter-segment time ratios and stretch the total time by elongating the segments that violate dynamic constraints, preserving the ratios. Another trick is to fix a feasible total time and only adjust the time allocation ratios.
