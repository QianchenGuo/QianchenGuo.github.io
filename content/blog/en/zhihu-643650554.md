Between 1880 and 1920, many masterpieces of modern analysis textbooks were published in English, French, and German: in Britain, Whittaker's *A Course of Modern Analysis* and Hardy's *A Course of Pure Mathematics*; in France, Jordan, Picard, Hermite, and Goursat each authored their own *Cours d'analyse*; and in Germany, Carathéodory's celebrated [*Theory of Functions of a Real Variable*](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231164027&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E3%80%8A%E5%AE%9E%E5%8F%98%E6%95%B0%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0%E3%80%8B&zhida_source=entity) (then still a calculus course, modeled on Euler's texts and excluding the foundational rigor that emerged in the mid-to-late nineteenth century).

Before 1930, analysis courses were divided only into real analysis, complex analysis, [Fourier analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231164027&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%82%85%E9%87%8C%E5%8F%B6%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90&zhida_source=entity), and functional analysis.

In 1951, Rudin and his contemporaries, guided by curricular design, extracted mathematical analysis from real analysis to create a standalone introductory course in analysis.

In 1960, a classic in analysis appeared: *Foundations of Modern Analysis* (French: *Analyse*) by Dieudonné, [a leading figure of the Bourbaki school](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231164027&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B8%83%E5%B0%94%E5%B7%B4%E5%9F%BA%E5%AD%A6%E6%B4%BE&zhida_source=entity); the English edition was retitled [*Foundations of Modern Analysis*](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231164027&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E3%80%8A%E7%8E%B0%E4%BB%A3%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90%E5%9F%BA%E7%A1%80%E3%80%8B&zhida_source=entity). Dieudonné regarded his work as "modern analysis" in the postwar sense, relegating earlier textbooks to the domain of "classical analysis." His intent is made explicit in the preface to the first volume: "...I have attempted to write, for the mathematicians of the 1970s, a treatise on analysis comparable to those that Jordan, Picard, and Goursat wrote for the students of mathematics between 1880 and 1920." He aimed to produce a comprehensive treatise that would equip students to begin research immediately upon completion. (These predecessors were all nineteenth-century mathematicians; there was a French tradition, at least until the early twentieth century, of writing all-encompassing tomes that took readers from the basics to the research frontier.) However, the mathematics of the twentieth century had advanced far beyond that of the nineteenth, so early twentieth-century analysis texts—Goursat's, for example—ran to only three volumes and roughly a thousand pages. Dieudonné's treatise, by contrast, immediately expanded to nine volumes, each between 300 and 500 pages (though some appear closer to 200). Even so, it realized only a fraction of his original plan, which had been to cover every branch of analysis then in existence, following the precedent of his predecessors. Upon publication, the work's conceptual novelty—replacing countability with filters, treating Riemann integration as a useful computational exercise rather than a foundational topic, and viewing maps as variables—sparked an immediate global trend of rewriting analysis textbooks.

By 1969, Dieudonné decided to expand and revise the work. With only minor revisions, *Foundations of Modern Analysis* was reissued, nearly unchanged, as the first volume of a new work, *Treatise on Analysis*, with the subtitle *Foundations of Modern Analysis, Expanded and Revised*. The expanded second volume appeared as Volume 2 of the *Treatise*, subtitled *Expanded and Revised*. These two volumes may be treated as an expanded edition of *Foundations of Modern Analysis* and studied independently. The remaining six volumes cover large-scale topics in analysis, including real analysis, complex analysis, [calculus on manifolds](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231164027&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B5%81%E5%BD%A2%E5%BE%AE%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86&zhida_source=entity), topology, functional analysis, and Lie groups. The English edition no longer carries the subtitle *Foundations of Modern Analysis* (the French original, as far as I know, does not either). According to the original title-page announcement, ten volumes were planned; only eight circulate online, suggesting the set may never have been completed.

In the 1980s, China translated the first two volumes of the *Treatise on Analysis*, using the English subtitle *Foundations of Modern Analysis*. No electronic version of the 1960 edition exists online, though used copies and PDF scans of the Chinese translation circulate. Without exaggeration, analysis teaching has not yet moved beyond the Bourbaki era represented by Dieudonné. The influence of Dieudonné's modern analysis treatise on global analysis instruction is evident from the boastful blurb on the back cover of the English edition.
