## State Space

I'd say the first major leap in my understanding of control came from a deeper appreciation of state space.

With this foundation, I firmly recognized that differential equations are the true object of study in control theory. It then naturally occurred to me that my ODE courses always emphasized "local" solutions, and I realized this was really referring to regions (connected open sets) in Rⁿ, while anything on a larger scale lives on manifolds. Before long, I came to see that "state space = phase space = the tangent bundle of the underlying manifold."

## Abstract Contemplations

Following this, my study of control theory branched into two tracks: one rooted in the more abstract theory of dynamical systems, and the other built on the concrete study of nonlinear control theory (Khalil). I believe the two will converge in geometric control theory (Isidori). The former is more abstract and yields fewer concrete results (though I think bifurcation theory offers a possible window into control), while the latter enjoys a richer body of theoretical results.

The analysis side of control theory concerns itself with the trajectory properties of a known system—this is closely related to the geometric theory of differential equations. The design or compensation side, however, requires working backward from a desired trajectory to find the control input *u*. The two are inverse problems of each other.

From the perspective of bifurcation theory, a control system can be viewed as a differential equation with parameters—the parameters being the control inputs *u*. We are essentially studying the bifurcation theory of this differential equation, where variations in *u* correspond to changes in the equilibrium points and limit cycles of the system's trajectories.

The distinction lies in the fact that control theory differs considerably from bifurcation theory in both methodology and focus. Traditional control is more concerned with equilibrium points and their local stabilization (nearly all design problems can be formulated as such), as well as estimates of the domain of attraction. In other words, control ultimately has to land on design—given a trajectory, find *u*. Bifurcation theory, by contrast, cares more about how changes in *u* affect the topological properties of trajectories.

As my studies progressed, I got a glimpse of integrable systems theory, and started learning analytical mechanics (intermittently, and not yet finished). I also encountered some Zhihu contributors working on motion planning—especially Mr. Jin, whose insights were highly inspiring. A picture slowly formed in my mind: a bottom-up vision of a more generalized notion of control. ## Pragmatic Progress

Meanwhile, in contrast to the free and unconstrained thinking of the abstract track, the pragmatic track proceeded much more steadily—and thus more slowly. Over the course of the year, I self-studied nonlinear systems in my spare time, going through Khalil's chapters piecemeal, albeit skipping singular perturbation theory. While I could follow the recipes, I hadn't yet connected them well with the more abstract perspective, and my understanding remained shallow (to this day, I can't claim to fully grasp the essence of backstepping, so widely used in nonlinear control—it's said to be a cascade idea, but I feel there's more for me to chew on).

Looking ahead, I plan to continue with Isidori's framework and nonlinear system design methods.

## Motion Planning

My motivation for getting into motion planning came roughly from looking at the career prospects of control theory and reading various opinions on Zhihu. To be honest, I hold great respect for some of the motion planning experts active on Zhihu. Other reasons for choosing planning as my direction can be found in the later section "Tasks, Planning, and Intelligence."

During the summer of 2024, I stayed on campus for a while and self-studied some foundational theory of motion planning. I took the motion planning course for mobile robots from Shenlan Academy, and read some of LaValle's planning algorithms (intermittently, without finishing). These, together with articles by many motion planning experts on Zhihu, gradually got me started in this field.

This is my research direction. I find it quite enjoyable and liberating to work at the intersection of control, optimization, and their many offshoots, and I believe control and planning are inherently inseparable. If we take planning in the broadest sense, it can encompass both optimization theory and control theory—including optimal control, MPC, and so on (see "Tasks, Planning, and Intelligence" below).

The core thesis is that the realization of intelligence lies in task-level planning, and the feasibility of such planning hinges on our understanding of the state space of task problems. I argue that the omission of state is the central obstacle preventing us from achieving task-level planning. At best, current frameworks incorporate planning with full consideration of uncertainty and interactive game-theoretic elements—which is also the main theoretical foundation of autonomous driving stacks. ## Some Neglected Corners

Over the course of the year, I also considered a few smaller questions, though limited energy prevented me from digging deeper.

### (I) Observers, Filtering, and State Estimation

In April 2024, I watched a lecture by Mr. Jin on Bilibili and learned about some theory related to state observers—particularly on Lie groups, which further confirmed my earlier thinking about how state space affects control performance. It was there that I encountered the Kalman filter from an optimal control perspective, which cleared up my confusion about it in one go.

[/en/blog/zhihu-694145140](/en/blog/zhihu-694145140)

That article was about exactly globally asymptotically stable cascade observers on Lie groups.

[/en/blog/zhihu-695337197](/en/blog/zhihu-695337197)

Around that time, I learned from online sources and from senior labmates that there has long been a schism between two camps in the observer/filtering problem. One camp approaches it from control theory, the other from probabilistic estimation. The probabilistic approach seems more prevalent these days—probably because it's easier to grasp and requires less prior knowledge. In linear systems and EKF, the two perspectives are clearly connected; in nonlinear systems, however, the control side offers various nonlinear observer designs, while the estimation side has methods like UKF and PF that don't seem to pursue stability—the most basic requirement—as rigorously, which draws criticism. Upon learning this, I felt a sense of regret: it's a pity that both academia and industry in China have let such a large gap persist unchecked. Then again, it's understandable—work that bridges two such systems is thankless in the current academic climate. We can only wait for established senior researchers to take it on.

Then in August–September of this year, our research group provided some early training for graduate school, and I encountered the Kalman filter from the other angle—estimation.

[/en/blog/zhihu-721626051](/en/blog/zhihu-721626051)

Having experienced both theoretical frameworks firsthand, I began to sense a connection between them—it seems to be a relationship between two forms of integration (which is quite unexpected at first glance). Intuitively, some link can indeed be established (apologies—this is a weed that sprouted in my mind recently and needs tending before it becomes a garden; my phrasing is a bit messy). For instance, in optimal control, we integrate a certain term as the cost functional, and this integration is explicit. Although state estimation from the Bayesian perspective also involves integrals, those integrals are intractable. What we typically use is an approximation, and this approximation manifests through the exponential map. So the relationship between the two is that between integration and the exponential map.

To give a simple example: starting from a simple linear system, the quadratic cost functional and the Gaussian noise assumption are essentially a relationship between integrating a quadratic form and exp(quadratic form)—taking a logarithm, they're the same thing.

So in very rough terms, nonlinear observers and filters should also be related to each other as the integral of some differential form versus the exponential map on a manifold. Taking a logarithmic map gives roughly equivalent results, and in the linear case this is global. It's precisely because the exponential map has no closed-form expression in the nonlinear setting that Bayesian estimation finds itself at a loss when facing nonlinearity.

### (II) Higher-Order Fully Actuated Systems and First-Order Dynamics

Having studied a fair amount of mathematics, I'd always taken it for granted that reducing arbitrary differential equations to first-order systems was a natural move. This approach also has a nice geometric correspondence on manifolds.

It wasn't until I encountered Academician Duan's theory of higher-order fully actuated systems several times this year (multiple times on Zhihu and once at an academic conference) that I began to question: is first-order dynamics really the most natural choice? So I posed the following question on Zhihu:

[https://www.zhihu.com/question/815769804?utm_psn=1858266443285671936](https://www.zhihu.com/question/815769804?utm_psn=1858266443285671936)

My personal guess is that this reduction-to-first-order approach originates from analytical mechanics, where it facilitates determining whether a system is holonomic and integrable. My original question noted: "I can sense that this approach is more amenable to geometric description—that is, in the language of vector fields on manifolds—which conveniently connects differential equations with geometry."

This language has strong advantages for determining integrability. However, as I analyzed in my earlier article "Random Thoughts on Mathematics and Control (Continued): Setting the Record Straight," integrability is actually a concept at a higher level within control. In other words, there should exist a language of differential equations that is better suited for control system design. I believe this was also the original motivation behind Academician Duan's theory—the hope that control theory can go further in such a language.

Turning back, then: why is it that on manifolds we can only define first-order derivatives via vector fields, but cannot naturally define higher-order derivatives? I later discovered that higher-order derivatives on manifolds are related to jet bundle and jet space theory, but I haven't been able to find accessible references on this.

The underlying optimal transport and information geometry are not just hot research areas—their applications to variational inference and variational gradient descent should also be essential foundations for stochastic optimization and even motion planning from a probabilistic perspective.
