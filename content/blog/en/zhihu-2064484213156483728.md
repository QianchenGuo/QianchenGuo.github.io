In the previous installment of the zero-order optimization series, we systematically introduced model-based methods and demonstrated their vibrant vitality in contemporary [applied mathematics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BA%94%E7%94%A8%E6%95%B0%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity). Among these, methods based on [distribution optimization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) hold a central position, particularly because such methods exhibit a certain global nature in zero-order optimization, hence also referred to as global optimization.

However, we did not convey the motivation behind this approach. This article builds the global optimization framework from a high-level perspective. In a sense, the high-level viewpoint seeks a more natural optimization paradigm—one that does not rely on local information like gradients as in traditional optimization, which leads to a despairing gap when moving from convex to nonconvex problems. This, of course, also owes much to the development of convex analysis in the last century, which provided many efficient methods for convex problems, but indeed hits a wall on [nonconvex problems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E5%87%B8%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity).

From this high-level viewpoint, we discover the intertwined nature of [optimization and control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E4%B8%8E%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity). Optimization can solve control problems (whether open-loop optimal control or closed-loop MPC), and control can also handle optimization problems—this resembles a [recursive structure](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%80%92%E5%BD%92%E7%BB%93%E6%9E%84&zhida_source=entity): an optimization problem can be viewed as a control problem, and vice versa.

* * *

First, a conclusion: at the theoretical level of [global optimization methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%A8%E5%B1%80%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity), in the strict sense of "constructing a deterministic [potential function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%BF%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) free of local traps, whose [gradient flow](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A2%AF%E5%BA%A6%E6%B5%81&zhida_source=entity) converges directly to the global minimum," there are currently only two major paradigms:

1.  Exponential construction / large deviations / Wasserstein gradient flow
2.  Square-root construction / weak KAM / critical Hamilton–Jacobi equations

Other methods either merely smooth the landscape without directly eliminating local minima (e.g., Moreau envelopes, elliptic smoothing), or must rely on homotopy continuation or stochastic transitions, or are merely probabilistic restatements of these two paradigms (e.g., Schrödinger bridges). This article focuses on the theory and numerical methods of square-root-construction-type global optimization; the next installment will cover the exponential construction type (though the update schedule remains uncertain, haha).

## 1. Eikonal Equations and Global Optimization[\[1\]](#ref_1)

For [convex functions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%87%B8%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity), we typically seek minima via the gradient flow, i.e., [gradient descent](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A2%AF%E5%BA%A6%E4%B8%8B%E9%99%8D%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity). For nonconvex functions, gradient-flow trajectories can only converge to local minima or saddle points. However, if we consider not the gradient of the function itself but rather the gradient of the (viscosity) solution to an eikonal equation—a special type of [HJB equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=HJB%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity)—it becomes possible to locate the global minimum. This essentially transforms the problem of finding minima into solving a PDE (a control problem).

### 1.1 The Eikonal Equation

The eikonal equation is the stationary HJB equation for a special class of optimal control problems, particularly minimal-time problems. It is also the [nonlinear PDE](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E5%81%8F%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) encountered in wave propagation when using WKB theory to approximate the [wave equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B3%A2%E5%8A%A8%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity).

**(1) Minimal-time problem**

Consider a controlled [dynamical system](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) $\dot{x} = f(x, u), \quad u \in U$ that must reach a target set $\mathcal{C}$ from the current state $x$ in minimal time. Define the value function $T(x)$ as the minimal time to first reach the target starting from $x$. The cost is $J(x,u) = \int dt$ with terminal value 0. By the [dynamic programming](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E6%80%81%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity) principle, the [value function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E5%80%BC%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) satisfies the stationary HJB equation

$$
H(x, \nabla T) = \min_{u \in U} \{ \nabla T(x) \cdot f(x,u) + 1 \} = 0, \quad x \notin \mathcal{C} \ \  T|_{\mathcal{C}} = 0.
$$

If the control is isotropic and the [maximum speed](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E5%A4%A7%E9%80%9F%E7%8E%87&zhida_source=entity) depends only on position, $v(x) > 0$, then substituting the control set reduces the dynamics to $\dot{x} = u(x) = v(x)\, a, \ |a| \le 1$. Then $\min_{|a|\le 1} \{ v(x)\,\nabla T \cdot a \} + 1 = 0.$ To minimize $\nabla T \cdot a$, choose $a = -\frac{\nabla T}{|\nabla T|}$, so the minimum is $-v(x)\|\nabla T\|$.

Thus $-v(x)\|\nabla T(x)\| + 1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \|\nabla T(x)\| = \frac{1}{v(x)}.$ Setting the [refractive index](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8A%98%E5%B0%84%E7%8E%87&zhida_source=entity) $\ell(x) = 1/v(x)$ yields the standard eikonal equation $\|\nabla T(x)\| = \ell(x)$. Therefore, the eikonal equation is the HJB equation of the minimal-time control problem, with [Hamiltonian](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Hamilton%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity) $H(x, p) = v(x)|p| - 1 = 0$.

More generally, if the velocity set $f(x,U)$ is a convex compact set containing the origin, the [support function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%94%AF%E6%92%91%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) $\sigma(x,p) = \max_{u\in U} p \cdot f(x,u)$ determines the Hamiltonian $H(x,p) = -\sigma(x,-p) + 1 = 0$. In the [anisotropic](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%90%84%E5%90%91%E5%BC%82%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) case (Finsler metric), the eikonal equation becomes $\mathcal F(x, \nabla T) = 1$, where $\mathcal F$ is a [norm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%8C%83%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity).

**(2) Minimal-energy problem**

Now consider the system $\dot{x} = u, \ \ u \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with the goal of reaching a closed target set $\mathcal{C}$ from an arbitrary starting point $x$, regardless of time, and only minimizing the following "energy" cost:

$J(x, u(\cdot)) = \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{2}\big( \|u(t)\|^2 + \ell^2(x(t)) \big) \, dt,$

stopping immediately upon reaching $\mathcal{C}$. Define the value function $S(x) = \inf_{u(\cdot)} J(x, u(\cdot)).$ This yields the stationary HJB equation

$$
\min_{u} \Big\{ \nabla S(x) \cdot u + \frac{1}{2}\|u\|^2 + \frac{1}{2}\ell^2(x) \Big\} = 0, \ \ S|_{\mathcal{C}} = 0.
$$

The optimal control is $u^* = -\nabla S(x)$, so $-\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla S\|^2 + \frac{1}{2}\ell^2 = 0.$ Hence $\|\nabla S(x)\|^2 = \ell^2(x)$, giving the same eikonal equation as before. Since $u = -\nabla S$ and $\|\nabla S\| = \ell$, the actual speed magnitude is also $\ell$, and the cost rate becomes $\ell^2$. Thus, here $S$ is not time but rather more like an [action](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%9C%E7%94%A8%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity).

In fact, if we define the [Riemannian metric](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Riemann%E5%BA%A6%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity) $ds = \ell(x)|dx|$, then the minimal time corresponds to the shortest arc length $\int ds$; via a parameter transformation, $S(x) = \int \ell \, ds$ while the time $T$ satisfies $\int 1 \, dt = \int ds/\ell$.

- The minimal-time cost is homogeneous of degree 0 in "speed" (as long as the path shape is correct, speed magnitude only affects arrival time, not the accumulation rate of total cost per unit time); minimizing a [linear function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) on the unit ball necessarily produces a norm homogeneous of degree 1.
- The energy cost is strictly quadratic in $u$; minimizing a [quadratic function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BA%8C%E6%AC%A1%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) and substituting yields a squared term. Although equivalent, the squared form is smooth and differentiable, and the characteristic (light-ray) equations are given by the Hamiltonian system, avoiding singularities.

### 1.2 Global Optimization

Given a [nonconvex function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E5%87%B8%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) $f(x)$, seek the [global minimum](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%A8%E5%B1%80%E6%9C%80%E5%B0%8F%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity) $m = \min f$ and the global minimizer set $A = \{x \mid f(x) = m\}$. The key construction is $\ell(x) = \sqrt{2(f(x) - m)}$ (hence the name "square-root type"). Then solve the eikonal equation: 

$$
\|\nabla T(x)\| = \ell(x), \quad x \notin A, \quad T(x) = 0, \quad x \in A.
$$

Here $T(x)$ is a [viscosity solution](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%B2%98%E6%80%A7%E8%A7%A3&zhida_source=entity). The physical picture of the eikonal equation is: a wave emanates from $A$; the propagation speed of the wave at point $x$ is $1/\ell(x)$, and $T(x)$ is the time at which the wave first reaches $x$.

The characteristic curves are given by the Hamiltonian system:

$$
\dot{x} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}, \quad \dot{p} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}, \quad H(x,p) = \frac{1}{2}(\|p\|^2 - \ell^2(x)).
$$

On the zero-energy surface $H = 0$, the characteristics satisfy $\|\dot{x}\| = \|p\| = \ell(x).$ These are precisely the characteristics of the eikonal equation; their projections onto $x$-space are the wave-propagation rays and also the optimal trajectories (minimal-time paths). These characteristics emanate from $A$ and radiate outward, covering the entire space. Now, the [negative gradient](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B4%9F%E6%A2%AF%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity) of $T$ is exactly the tangent direction of these characteristics—that is, following them upstream leads back to the wave source. The value function $T$ satisfies

$$
\mathcal L_x T = \nabla T \cdot \dot{x} = -\|\nabla T\|^2 = -\ell^2 = -2(f(x)-m).
$$

- As long as $f(x) > m$, the value function decreases strictly monotonically along the characteristics, so the system does not stop at local minima. The gradient flow defined by $f$ itself stops at the [local minima](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=3&q=%E5%B1%80%E9%83%A8%E6%9E%81%E5%B0%8F%E7%82%B9&zhida_source=entity) of $f$, which are equilibria. The flow $\dot{x} = -\nabla T$ induced by the eikonal equation is defined by the wave-front arrival time $T$. The only equilibria are the wave source $A$. Using a global PDE, we construct a more appropriate value function $T$ whose only equilibria are precisely the global minimizer set of the [original function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8E%9F%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity). Then performing gradient descent on this new function naturally guides the trajectory to the global minimum.
- $\ell(x) = \sqrt{2(f(x) - m)}$ satisfies: at a [global minimum](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=6&q=%E5%85%A8%E5%B1%80%E6%9E%81%E5%B0%8F%E7%82%B9&zhida_source=entity) $x^* \in A$, $f(x^*) = m$, so $\ell(x^*) = 0$ and the wave speed is $\infty$. At any local (non-global) minimum, $f(x) > m$, so the wave speed is finite. The larger the [function value](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity), the slower the wave. The wave emanates from $A$; it propagates extremely fast in the neighborhood of $A$ and slowly elsewhere. Since the wave is "first-come, first-served," when it encounters a local minimum, the value of $\ell$ at the bottom of the basin is smaller than its surroundings but still strictly positive (because $f > m$). The wave either bypasses the basin or fills it, but never treats the basin as a source. The value of the time function $T$ at the basin floor is determined by the arrival time of the wave front that traveled around from $A$, not by the basin itself.

### 3. Theoretical Explanation

The eikonal equation $\|\nabla T\|=\ell(x),\ x\in\mathbb R^n\backslash A$, with boundary condition $T(x)=0,\ x\in A.$ The solution to this [boundary value problem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BE%B9%E5%80%BC%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity) is given by $T(x)=\inf_{u}\int_{0}^{t(u)}\ell(x(s))ds,\quad \dot x(s)=u(s),\ x(0)=x_0.$

Here $t(u)$ denotes the first arrival time of the trajectory to $A$, and $u(s)$ is the control function. One can show that the minimizing trajectory follows, almost everywhere, the gradient of the viscosity solution $T(x)$, and reaches a global minimizer in finite time (bounded by $dist(x_0,A)$).

In weak KAM theory, when the Hamiltonian is symmetric in momentum (a special case being $H = \frac{1}{2}\|p\|^2 - (f-m)$), the corresponding Mather critical value $c(H)$ is precisely the global maximum of $H(x,0)$. Considering the function $f(x)=-H(x,0)$, and apart from assuming the minimizer set $A$ is nonempty, no additional conditions are needed: one can search for minimizers through the viscosity solution (which is guaranteed to exist) of the associated stationary equation $H(x,Du)=c(H)$. In this setting, the gradient flow is taken with respect to the viscosity solution, and the minimizer set is exactly the Aubry set $A$. Essentially, this method exploits the result that the limit set of the gradient flow always lies within the Aubry set.

### 1. Essence: Monotonicity, Causality, and Graph Search

Traditional front-tracking methods—for example, explicitly marking points on the interface and computing their normal motion at each time step—are like using countless tiny needles to trace a moving surface. When the surface splits, merges, or develops sharp corners, these "needles" become entangled and the algorithm collapses. All mainstream eikonal solvers succeed because of the causal structure connecting the local solution operator and global characteristics, realized through Godunov upwind schemes. Rather than tracking the interface itself, they track the time at which the entire space is "swept" by the interface (the value function of the HJB equation). For the eikonal equation

$$
|\nabla T(x)| = 1/v(x)
$$

the first-order discretization can be written as:

$$
\left[ \sum_{j=1}^d \left( \frac{\max(T_i - T_{i,j}^\text{min}, 0)}{h} \right)^2 \right]^{1/2} = \frac{1}{v_i}
$$

where $T_{i,j}^\text{min}   = \min(T_{i+1}, T_{i-1})$ is the smaller $T$ value among the two neighbors along the $j$-th dimension. This is a remarkably precise formulation: it captures the core physical intuition of upwind schemes—information always flows from regions the wave has already reached (small $T$) toward regions it has not yet reached (large $T$).

In each dimension, the minimum of the left and right neighbors is explicitly taken, and the $\max(\cdot, 0)$ ensures the difference is nonnegative (i.e., no information flows from points with larger $T$ values than the current point). This formula is monotone: if a neighbor's value decreases, your solution never increases. This guarantees the one-way flow of information, which is precisely the origin of the term "upwind." It enables the algorithm to automatically capture complex wavefront shapes as waves pass through different media and around obstacles, without producing nonphysical oscillations.

The characteristic curves of the eikonal equation are rays (shortest paths satisfying Fermat's principle), whose direction is always $\nabla T$. This determines that information always flows from the "source" of small $T$ values to the "wavefront" of large $T$ values. Mathematically, this implies the solution operator possesses a strict partial-order dependency. The essential difference among algorithms lies in how they exploit this partial-order relation.

From a computer science perspective, these numerical methods are nothing more than applications of shortest-path algorithms in graph theory to specialized grid graphs. All methods solve the same problem: each grid point is a node. Each edge (connecting adjacent nodes) has a weight (travel time) defined by the reciprocal of the velocity field. The solution to the eikonal equation is the travel-time field from the source. All solvers, in essence, run shortest-path algorithms on this implicitly defined graph. Choosing among methods amounts to trading off between three fundamental computational paradigms: sorting, sweeping, and scheduling.

### 2. Fast Marching Method (FMM)

FMM is an extremely efficient narrow-band method for solving the eikonal equation. It actively maintains a time-sorted wavefront and explicitly resolves causal dependencies one by one. It is highly efficient and naturally handles wavefront splitting and merging. However, when the velocity field depends not only on position but also on wavefront curvature or direction, simple upwind schemes no longer satisfy strict causality, and standard FMM fails.

Mathematical essence of FMM: Dijkstra-style topological sorting. Under a monotone upwind scheme and $v > 0$, the dependency graph of the eikonal equation is a directed acyclic graph (DAG). FMM performs a single Dijkstra-style topological sort on this implicit DAG:

1.  Starting from the initial grid points, maintain the "wavefront" or "narrow band" of candidate points using a priority queue;
2.  At each step, extract the point with the smallest value—its value is mathematically guaranteed to have converged, as its causal dependencies are satisfied;
3.  "Freeze" it, and update its successors in the dependency graph.

This is a typical label-setting algorithm: once a node is solved, it never changes. The sole pillar of its correctness is the strict causality of the physical wavefront (which holds when the velocity field $v$ is strictly positive). It is purely a sorting problem. The algorithm proceeds by establishing a partial order among nodes in order of travel time. Algorithmic complexity: $O(N \log N)$, where $N$ is the number of grid points. The $\log N$ factor comes from maintaining the priority queue (heap structure), which is also the bottleneck.

The most expensive operation in Dijkstra's algorithm is Decrease-Key (updating the priority of an existing element in the priority queue). Large-scale performance optimization of FMM is almost equivalent to optimizing the priority queue of Dijkstra's algorithm, such as:

-   Using Fibonacci heaps (where Decrease-Key is theoretically $O(1)$);
-   Using radix heaps or bucket sorting (exploiting properties of edge weights);
-   Combining multi-level queues for approximation.

### 3. Fast Sweeping Method (FSM)

This is an iterative, upwind-difference-based solver that requires no auxiliary data structures. It does not simulate wavefront propagation, maintain a narrow band, or use a priority queue; instead, it propagates information through alternating-direction sweeps. Implementation is simple and requires no complex data structures. It is highly efficient for simple isotropic eikonal equations. However, for complex velocity fields or anisotropic equations, the number of iterations may increase significantly, and the convergence rate is less stable than FMM. For large-scale or GPU environments, FMM parallelization is extremely challenging, because Dijkstra's algorithm is globally sequential; forced parallelization requires careful domain decomposition and communication to avoid violating causality. FSM, in contrast, can easily update grid strips in parallel during each sweep. Its parallel strategy is lock-free, making it well suited for GPU implementation.

Mathematical essence of FSM: Gauss-Seidel-type nonlinear iteration. It abandons explicit dependency graph analysis and returns to the roots of iterative methods, turning the PDE solution into a fixed-point problem of the discretized PDE system. The key observation is that through carefully designed Gauss-Seidel sweep orders (e.g., in two dimensions, alternating four sweeps: (x+, y+), (x-, y+), (x-, y-), (x+, y-)) that traverse all grid points, where each traversal attempts to update the current point using neighbor values, boundary information can be propagated efficiently. The causal chain is implicitly and repeatedly combed through.

Alternating-direction sweeps exhaust all possible quadrant directions of characteristics. For any grid point that depends on upstream nodes, regardless of the direction of its upstream, there is always a sweep that processes it from that direction. FSM is, in essence, a highly efficient label-correcting algorithm: each node's value is repeatedly corrected until global causality is satisfied through iteration.

Algorithmic complexity is $O(N)$. Due to the nature of upwind differences, for simple cases, $2^d$ sweeps (where $d$ is the spatial dimension) are typically sufficient for convergence. Data structure optimization for FSM lies in memory access patterns. It has no complex data structures; the computational core is a highly regular, predictable loop. The performance bottleneck is memory bandwidth and cache hit rate. A well-optimized FSM implementation achieves peak floating-point performance through careful array layout, cache blocking, and vectorized instructions (SIMD) on every full-space sweep.

### 4. Fast Iterative Method (FIM)

This is a modern method that combines the strengths of the previous two and is specifically designed for complex equations. It focuses only on the "hot spots" where causality may break, applying dynamic intervention. It inherits FSM's generality (handling anisotropic and nonconvex Hamiltonians) while concentrating computational effort through an "active list," avoiding FSM's global blind sweeping.

FIM also maintains an "active list," but unlike FMM's narrow band, this list does not require global sorting or selecting a minimum. It more closely resembles a "pool of nodes needing updates." It performs local updates iteratively. At each step, a point is extracted from the "active list," its value is updated, and if the value genuinely changes, all affected neighbors are added to the "active list."

Mathematical essence of FIM: localized nonlinear Gauss-Seidel. It is an improvement over FSM. It is also a label-correcting algorithm, but the core insight is that the propagation of solution-operator errors is local under causal constraints. Unlike FSM, which blindly sweeps the entire space, it maintains only an active list that tracks nodes potentially invalidated by upstream updates. This is essentially concentrating iterations in causally nonstationary regions. It is a "compute-on-demand" implementation of the Gauss-Seidel idea on PDE grids. Label-correcting is essentially a variant of the Bellman-Ford algorithm or dynamic programming.

Algorithmic complexity is typically close to

$$
O(N)
$$

and it is often faster than FSM on many complex problems, while also handling noncausal problems that FMM cannot. The core bottleneck is dynamic task scheduling. It requires a concurrent, lock-free "active list" data structure (such as linked-list arrays or concurrent queues) to track unconverged nodes. FIM's algorithmic complexity depends on the balance between active-list management overhead and wasted updates. It is essentially dynamic programming under coarse-grained parallelism.

### 5. High-Order ENO/WENO Schemes

Beyond the classical methods mentioned above, some approaches aim for higher numerical accuracy. Classical FMM/FSM use first-order upwind differencing, which has limited precision. In essence, higher-order ENO (Essentially Non-Oscillatory) and WENO (Weighted Essentially Non-Oscillatory) schemes can be [seamlessly integrated](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%A0%E7%BC%9D%E9%9B%86%E6%88%90&zhida_source=entity) into the FMM or FSM framework. They construct high-order polynomial reconstructions by adaptively selecting the smoothest stencil within the neighborhood of the point being solved, thereby significantly improving the approximation accuracy of the solution gradient while maintaining upwind stability.

### 6. Ray Theory

These methods do not solve the entire field; instead, they transform the PDE into a system of ODEs. · Ray method: In situations where global topology cannot be handled, it abandons the global causality graph and traces only a single causal chain (characteristic curve). Using the [method of characteristics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%89%B9%E5%BE%81%E7%BA%BF%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity), the partial differential equation (eikonal equation) is converted into a system of [ordinary differential equations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B8%B8%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) (ray equations). The eikonal equation corresponds to the Hamiltonian $H(x, p) = v(x)|p| - 1 = 0$, and the characteristic curves (rays) satisfy the ODE system:

$$
\frac{dx}{ds} = \nabla_{{p}} H = v \frac{{p}}{|{p}|}
$$

where ${p} = \nabla T$ is the slowness vector, whose direction is normal to the wavefront. Along a ray, the slowness vector satisfies: $\frac{d{p}}{ds} = -\nabla_{{x}} H = -|{p}| \nabla v$

The computational efficiency is extremely high because only points along the ray path are computed, not the entire space. However, it cannot handle wavefront topological changes (such as bifurcation or merging), fails in caustic regions (where rays converge), and the uniformity of ray distribution is difficult to guarantee, potentially leading to insufficient spatial coverage. Hence, there are also wavefront construction methods that combine fields and characteristics (adaptively sampling rays along the wavefront). The global $T$ field is computed, and rays are then traced backward along its negative gradient $-\nabla T$; the field can handle bifurcations and merging, while the ray method can quickly trace the extension path of a specific tip.

## III. Measure Relaxation

So far, we have focused on ordinary gradient flows converging to local minima or saddle points of functions. One reason for this is that the way we construct the gradient flow is not entirely correct. In this section, we adopt a different perspective. In gradient-based optimization algorithms, we have fallen into pointwise convergence, constructing convergence only through local gradient information—this is also a root cause of the problem. In fact, to find the set $A = \argmin f(x)$, a more natural approach is to construct convergence of the set (in terms of measures). The general formulation involves defining a measure on the set and integrating. For numerical computation, this measure is usually chosen as a [probability measure](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A6%82%E7%8E%87%E6%B5%8B%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity), and then computed via Monte Carlo methods. At this point, you will find that gradient information itself seems unnecessary. Furthermore, one can discover that the two are dual to each other.

### 1. Definitions and Properties

Now consider a nonconvex problem:

$$
\min_{x \in X} f(x) \quad (P)
$$

where $f(x)$ is a nonconvex function and $X$ is the feasible set. We can introduce a Dirac measure $\mu$ that concentrates all probability mass at the point $x$. Then $f(x) = \int f(z) d\mu(z)$, and the original problem $(P)$ can be equivalently formulated as:

$$
\min_{\mu \in \mathcal{P}(X)} \int f(z) d\mu(z)=<f(z),\mu(z)>    \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mu \text{ is a Dirac measure.}
$$

Note that at this step, we have introduced duality through integration, embedding the original optimization function into the dual measure space. However, the [feasible region](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%AF%E8%A1%8C%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity) of this problem is constrained to Dirac measures, which is a nonconvex subset of $\mathcal P(X)$ isomorphic to $X$. Subsequently, we convexify this feasible region by expanding it to the probability [measure space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B5%8B%E5%BA%A6%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) over $X$, which is a convex space. The relaxed problem is an infinite-dimensional [linear program](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity) (IDLP), which is convex. This new problem was already encountered in our previous article [Zero-Order Optimization (II): Model-Based Methods](/en/blog/zhihu-1959271531701077799):

$$
\inf_{\mu \in \mathcal{P}(X)} \mathbb E_{\mu(z) }[f(z)]=<f(z),\mu(z)>\quad (R)
$$

A nonconvex function is like a valley. If we could fill the entire valley with sand and minimize the average height of this sand, then the lowest valley of the entire mountain range would inevitably accumulate the most sand. If one could describe the distribution of this sand, that distribution would necessarily be optimal. Thus, we have transformed the problem of finding an optimal point into finding an optimal distribution.

The measure relaxation $(R)$ and the original problem $(P)$ enjoy favorable properties that provide methods and pathways for recovering the solution to the original problem. Let $p^* = \min_{x \in X} f(x)$ and $r^* = \min_{\mu \in \mathcal{P}(X)} \int f(z) d\mu(z)$. Then:

1.  The relaxed problem $(R)$ provides a lower bound. For any feasible solution to $(R)$, i.e., any Dirac measure $\delta_x, x\in X$, by definition $r^* \le \int f d\delta_x = f(x), \ \ \forall x \in X$. In particular, for the minimizer $x^*$, we have $r^* \le f(x^*) = p^*$.
2.  There exists $\mu^* \in \mathcal{P}(X)$ that is an optimal measure for problem $(R)$. By Prokhorov's theorem, since $X$ is compact, $\mathcal{P}(X)$ is compact in the weak topology (i.e., the topology of [convergence in distribution](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BE%9D%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83%E6%94%B6%E6%95%9B&zhida_source=entity)). The objective functional $J(\mu) = \int f d\mu$ is continuous in the weak topology (since $f$ is continuous). By the [Extreme Value Theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9E%81%E5%80%BC%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity), a [continuous function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%9E%E7%BB%AD%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) on a compact set attains its minimum.
3.  The relaxation is tight, i.e., $r^* = p^*$. It suffices to show $r^* \ge p^*$. For any probability measure $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(X)$, since $f(z) \ge p^*$ for all $z \in X$, $\int f(z) d\mu(z) \ge \int p^* d\mu(z) = p^* \int d\mu(z) = p^*.$ Since this holds for all $\mu$, we have $r^* \ge p^*$.

### 2. Occupation Measures[\[2\]](#ref_2) [\[3\]](#ref_3) [\[4\]](#ref_4)

Lumped-parameter [control systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) are essentially nonlinear ODEs. A differential equation with a given [initial condition](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%9D%E5%80%BC%E6%9D%A1%E4%BB%B6&zhida_source=entity) (Cauchy problem) has a unique solution. Instead of describing a single path, we consider the set of all paths from a measure-theoretic perspective. We abandon tracking "one" specific trajectory and instead track the "flow distribution" of "a swarm" of trajectories in space-time. In this way, even if the original system is nonlinear, the equation describing its overall evolution can become linear.

Consider the following control system:

$$
\dot{x}(t)=f(t,x(t),u(t))\quad t\in[0,T]
$$

. Define measures of the system at different times:

1.  Initial measure $\mu_0$: can be understood as the probability distribution of the state at time t = 0;
2.  Final measure $\mu_T$: can be understood as the probability distribution of the state at terminal time T;
3.  Occupation measure $\mu$: can be understood as the time spent by the system during the process.

Let's examine the definition of the occupation measure in detail.

$$
\mu(A\times B\times C|x_0)=\int_0^TI_{A\times B\times C}(t,x(t|x_0),u(t|x_0))dt, \ \ A\times B\times C\in[0,T]\times X\times U
$$

Given a specific trajectory starting from $x_0$, over $[0,T]$, how much time does it spend simultaneously satisfying: "time belongs to $A$, position falls in spatial region $B$, and control input falls in region $C$". The measure $\mu$ is used to record this cumulative sojourn time. If the trajectory is deterministic, this measure is a line integral measure along the trajectory, similar to a Dirac measure piled up on a curve. Here, $I$ is the indicator function. Based on the above definition, we have the following important property:

$\int_0^Tg(t,x(t|x_0),u(t|x_0))dt=\int_{[0,T]\times X\times U}g(t,x,u)d\mu(t,x,u|x_0)$

Furthermore, define a linear Lie derivative operator $\mathcal{L}$ :

$$
v\mapsto\mathcal{L}v:=\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_i}f_i(t,x,u)=\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+\nabla v\cdot f
$$

Based on the above groundwork, the initial measure, final measure, and occupation measure can be linked together to form a weak formulation:

$$
\int_Xv(T,x)d\mu_T(x)-\int_Xv(0,x)d\mu_0(x)=\int_{[0,T]\times X\times U}\mathcal{L}v(t,x,u)d\mu(x,t,u)
$$

Or more concisely:

$$
\langle\mu_T,v(T,\cdot)\rangle=\langle\mu_0,v(0,\cdot)\rangle+\langle\mu,\mathcal{L}v\rangle\quad\forall v\in C^1([0,T]\times X)
$$

Since the above equation holds for any function $v$, through [integration by parts](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%86%E9%83%A8%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86&zhida_source=entity), we obtain the linear PDE for the measure—the Liouville equation:

$$
\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial t}+\nabla \cdot (f\mu)=\mu_0-\mu_T
$$

### 3. Duality Between Measure Relaxation and the Eikonal Equation

In the eikonal equation, we defined an optimal control problem (minimum-time problem):

$$
\begin {aligned} &\underset{u}{min} \int dt  \\ s.t.\ \  &\dot x=u(x)=v(x)a=\frac{a}{\sqrt{2(f(x)-m)}}\\ &x(0)=x_0\\ &a\in [0,1] \end {aligned}
$$

We seek the optimal control strategy $u^*$, which yields an optimal trajectory $x^*(t)$. This trajectory can be viewed as a deterministic process along the time axis, i.e., a trajectory of an optimization dynamical system. Introducing the occupation measure $\mu$ (since the system is autonomous and the [objective function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%AE%E6%A0%87%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) depends only on total time, the [time dimension](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E7%BB%B4%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity) is integrated out), we rewrite the original problem using this measure: the system dynamics become a Liouville equation constraint on the measure, along with [boundary conditions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BE%B9%E7%95%8C%E6%9D%A1%E4%BB%B6&zhida_source=entity) for the initial and terminal measures. Thus, the original optimal control problem becomes an infinite-dimensional linear program on the measure space:

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned} \min_{\mu} &\quad \int d\mu \qquad (R) \\  s.t. \ \ ∇⋅(μ(x)u(x))&=δ_{x_0(x)}−δ_{x^∗(x)}, \ \ x∈Ω \\ μ(x)u(x)⋅n&(x)=0, \ \ \ x∈∂Ω \\ μ(x)≥0 \\ ∣μ(x)u(x)∣&≤v(x)μ(x).          \end{aligned} }
$$

where $n(x)$ denotes the unit outward normal vector on the boundary of the domain. The Liouville equation for the measure can also be written in weak form with a test function: $∫ _Ω  ∇ϕ(x)⋅(μ(x)u(x))dx=ϕ(x ^ ∗  )−ϕ(x  _0   ),∀ϕ∈C  _c^1   (Ω)$. This is the result of "convexifying the feasible region" in measure relaxation—relaxing the search for a single trajectory (a nonconvex path space) to the search for a probability measure (occupation measure) satisfying linear constraints. The feasible region is a convex cone, and the objective function is linear. This is a convex optimization problem.

According to the duality theory of infinite-dimensional linear programming, the [dual variable](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AF%B9%E5%81%B6%E5%8F%98%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity) for the convex linear program (R) is a continuous function $T(x)$ on the [state space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) (which is essentially the test function $\phi$ in the weak form), satisfying:

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned} \underset{T}{sup}\quad T(x_0) - T(x^*)  \\ \text{s.t.} \quad \nabla T(x) \cdot u \le 1,  \\ \|u\| \le v(x). \end{aligned} }
$$

This constraint can be equivalently written as: $\max_{\|u\|\le v(x)} \nabla T(x) \cdot u \le 1$, and the maximum on the left-hand side is $v(x) \|\nabla T(x)\|$. Therefore, the constraint becomes: $v(x) \|\nabla T(x)\| \le 1 \quad \iff \quad \|\nabla T(x)\| \le \frac{1}{v(x)}.$

Meanwhile, to maximize $T(x_0) - T(x^*)$, and typically setting $T(x^*) = 0$, the dual problem seeks the largest $T(x_0)$ under the inequality constraint. Under optimality conditions, this [inequality constraint](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%8D%E7%AD%89%E5%BC%8F%E7%BA%A6%E6%9D%9F&zhida_source=entity) is tight (equality holds) almost everywhere, i.e.: $\|\nabla T(x)\| = \frac{1}{v(x)}.$ This is precisely the eikonal equation, which is the dual problem of the original measure relaxation problem. The value function $T$ is the dual solution of the linear program $(R)$.

### 4\. Derivation

Let $\rho=\mu u$ and introduce a [continuously differentiable](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%9E%E7%BB%AD%E5%8F%AF%E5%BE%AE&zhida_source=entity) Lagrange multiplier $T(x)$ (corresponding to the Liouville equation constraint). Construct the Lagrange functional:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mu,\rho,T) = \int 1 \, d\mu + \big\langle T,\; (\delta_{x_0} - \delta_{x^*}) - \nabla\cdot\rho \big\rangle .
$$

Here $\langle T, \delta_{x_0} - \delta_{x^*} \rangle = T(x_0) - T(x^*)$ and $\langle T, \nabla\cdot\rho \rangle = \int T \, d(\nabla\cdot\rho) =-\int \nabla T \cdot d\rho .$ (The boundary conditions ensure that $\rho$ has compact support.) Therefore,

$$
\mathcal{L} = \int 1 \, d\mu + T(x_0) - T(x^*) + \int \nabla T \cdot d\rho .
$$

The dual function is $g(T) = \inf_{\mu \ge 0,\; |\rho|\le v\mu} \mathcal{L}(\mu,\rho,T).$ We first minimize over $\rho$. Since $|\rho| \le v\mu$, there exists a [vector-valued function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%90%91%E9%87%8F%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) $u$ such that $d\rho = u \, d\mu$ and $\|u\|\le v$. Hence $\int \nabla T \cdot d\rho = \int (\nabla T \cdot u) \, d\mu .$ For fixed $\mu$, to minimize this integral we should choose $u$ to oppose $\nabla T$ with $\|u\|=v$, yielding

$$
\inf_{|\rho|\le v\mu} \int \nabla T \cdot d\rho = -\int v(x) \|\nabla T(x)\| \, d\mu .
$$

Substituting back into $\mathcal{L}$, we obtain

$$
g(T) = \inf_{\mu \ge 0} \left[ \int 1 \, d\mu - \int v \|\nabla T\| \, d\mu \right] + T(x_0) - T(x^*) .
$$

If there exists a point $x$ such that $1 - v(x)\|\nabla T(x)\| < 0$, we may concentrate $\mu$ at that point and let $\int \mu \to \infty$, driving the objective to $-\infty$. Therefore, for the [infimum](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%8B%E7%A1%AE%E7%95%8C&zhida_source=entity) to be finite, we must have

$$
1 - v(x)\|\nabla T(x)\| \ge 0 \quad \mu\text{-a.e.}, \quad \text{i.e.} \quad v(x)\|\nabla T(x)\| \le 1 .
$$

 Under this condition, $\mu = 0$ attains the infimum $0$. Hence the dual problem is:

$$
\boxed{ \max_{T \in C^1} \; \big[ T(x_0) - T(x^*) \big] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad v(x) \|\nabla T(x)\| \le 1 . }
$$

Of course, since the variables are infinite-dimensional, topological and continuity issues must be addressed. [Strong duality](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BC%BA%E5%AF%B9%E5%81%B6%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) in infinite dimensions requires additional compactness and convexity conditions (e.g., separable costs, convex control sets), but it typically holds, so we will not delve deeper here.

## IV. Numerical Methods for Measure Relaxation [\[5\]](#ref_5)

SOS converts the "qualitative" question of nonnegative polynomials into the "quantitative" problem of SDP, while the Lasserre hierarchy leverages the SDP representation of SOS to transform the infinite-dimensional, nonconvex problem of polynomial global optimization into a sequence of finite-dimensional, convex SDPs, progressively approximating the true solution by increasing the order. Its theoretical foundation lies in a deep combination of measure theory and [algebraic geometry](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity) (Putinar's Positivstellensatz), and its computational essence is solving a special convex program composed of linear constraints on polynomial coefficients plus a positive semidefinite cone. This is precisely the concrete realization of what we described earlier—"approximating infinite-dimensional LPs by an increasing sequence of finite-dimensional SDPs"—and the most successful instantiation of measure relaxation ideas in the polynomial world.

### 1\. Sum of Squares (SOS) [\[6\]](#ref_6) [\[7\]](#ref_7)

The fundamental difficulty of polynomial global optimization is that deciding whether a polynomial $f(x)$ is nonnegative on $\mathbb{R}^n$ or on some region is an [NP-hard](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=NP-hard&zhida_source=entity) problem. SOS provides a computable sufficient condition.

Definition: A real polynomial $f(x) \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ is a sum of squares (SOS) if there exist polynomials $g_1, \dots, g_k$ such that $f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^k g_i(x)^2.$

Clearly, SOS $\implies$ nonnegative. When $x$ is a single variable, or when the highest-degree term of the polynomial is at most quadratic, this condition is not only sufficient but also necessary. In general, although the condition is not necessary, the vast majority of nonnegative polynomials can be represented in sum-of-squares form. See Hilbert's 17th problem. The power of SOS lies in its equivalent characterization via positive semidefiniteness.

Key theorem: Let $z(x)$ be the vector of monomial [basis functions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9F%BA%E5%90%91%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity) of degree at most $d$ (e.g., $[1, x_1, x_2, x_1^2, x_1x_2, x_2^2, \dots]^T$). Then $f(x)$ is an SOS polynomial of degree $2d$ if and only if there exists a positive semidefinite matrix $Q \succeq 0$ such that $f(x) = z(x)^T Q z(x).$ Expanding $z(x)^T Q z(x)$ and matching coefficients with those of $f$ yields linear equality constraints on $Q$, together with $Q \succeq 0$. This is a semidefinite program (SDP).

This is the essence of the [computability](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%AF%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) of SOS: converting the infinite-dimensional condition "there exists a sum of squares of polynomials" into a finite-dimensional semidefinite constraint. Without this bridge, global optimization cannot be solved systematically.

### 2\. Semidefinite Programming (SDP)

SDP is the broadest tractable class in convex optimization; its essence is linear programming over the positive semidefinite cone. The standard primal problem is:

$$
\begin{aligned} \min_X \quad & \langle C, X \rangle \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & \langle A_i, X \rangle = b_i, \quad i=1,\dots,m, \\ & X \succeq 0, \end{aligned}
$$

where $X$ is a [symmetric matrix](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AF%B9%E7%A7%B0%E7%9F%A9%E9%98%B5&zhida_source=entity) variable and $\langle A, B \rangle = \text{tr}(A^T B)$ is the matrix inner product. The constraint $X \succeq 0$ means $X$ belongs to the positive semidefinite cone (all eigenvalues ≥ 0). SDP generalizes linear conic programming: the nonnegative orthant constraint $x \ge 0$ in LP is replaced by the cone constraint $X \succeq 0$. The dual problem is:

$$
\max_{y} \quad b^T y \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_i y_i A_i \preceq C.
$$

Strong duality holds under Slater's condition.

Modern SDP solvers (e.g., MOSEK, SeDuMi, SDPT3) commonly use primal-dual interior-point methods, which can solve the problem to arbitrary accuracy in polynomial time in theory. The idea is to perform Newton iterations on the complementary slackness condition $X S = \mu I$ (where $S$ is the dual slack variable) while decreasing the barrier parameter $\mu$ along the central path. Each iteration solves a large-scale linear system (the Schur complement equation), with complexity $O(n^3)$ ($n$ being the matrix dimension) or higher, and significant memory usage. For large-scale SDPs, first-order methods (e.g., ADMM) or specialized structure-exploiting solvers are required.

### 3\. The Lasserre Hierarchy: Embedding Nonconvex Problems into a Convergent Sequence of SDPs

**(1) Construction and Convergence**

We can now assemble SOS and SDP into the Lasserre hierarchy. Measure relaxation transforms the nonconvex optimization problem equivalently into an infinite-dimensional linear program; but infinite-dimensional problems cannot be computed directly, so we must "[discretize](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A6%BB%E6%95%A3%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity)". Consider the general polynomial optimization problem:

$$
p^* = \min_{x \in K} f(x), \quad K = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : g_j(x) \ge 0, j=1,\dots,m\},
$$

where $f, g_j$ are polynomials. The Lasserre hierarchy can be understood from two dual perspectives.

The primal problem (moment problem): Find a probability measure $\mu$ supported on $K$ that minimizes $\int f d\mu$. This is precisely the measure relaxation discussed earlier. Upon discretization, the infinite-dimensional $\mu$ is reduced to a finite set of moment variables $y_\alpha = \int x^\alpha d\mu$. Then $\int f(x)d\mu = \sum_{\alpha} f_\alpha \int x^\alpha d\mu = \sum_{\alpha} f_\alpha y_\alpha$.  
For example, choose a relaxation order $r \ge \max(\lceil \deg f/2 \rceil, \lceil \deg g_j/2 \rceil)$. Define the moment sequence $y = (y_\alpha)_{|\alpha| \le 2r}$. For any polynomial $p(x)$, if $\mu \ge 0$, then $\int p(x)^2 d\mu \ge 0$. Consider only polynomials $p(x) = \sum_{\beta} p_\beta x^\beta$ of degree $\le r$ (with $|\beta| \le r$). Expanding the squared integral:

$$
\int p(x)^2 d\mu = \sum_{\beta, \gamma} p_\beta p_\gamma \int x^{\beta+\gamma} d\mu = \sum_{\beta, \gamma} p_\beta p_\gamma \cdot y_{\beta+\gamma}
$$

Writing this quadratic form in matrix form yields the **Hankel matrix** $M_r(y)$, with $[M_r(y)]_{\beta,\gamma} = y_{\beta+\gamma}$ and dimension $\binom{n+r}{r}$. Since the expression above is $\ge 0$ for any vector $p$, the discretization condition is $M_r(y) \succeq 0$. Additionally, taking $p(x)$ of degree $\le r - d_j$ (where $d_j = \lceil \deg(g_j)/2 \rceil$, ensuring the total degree does not exceed $2r$), and substituting the discrete variables:

$$
\int g_j(x) p(x)^2 d\mu = \sum_{\beta, \gamma} p_\beta p_\gamma \left( \sum_{\delta} (g_j)_\delta \cdot y_{\beta+\gamma+\delta} \right)
$$

The matrix corresponding to this quadratic form is the **localizing matrix** $M_{r-d_j}(g_j y)$. Its entries are [linear combinations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%BB%84%E5%90%88&zhida_source=entity) of the coefficients of $g_j$ and the moments $y$. The constraint $g_j(x) \ge 0$ requires the localizing matrix $M_{r-d_j}(g_j \, y)$ to be positive semidefinite. The discretized problem is thus $M_{r-d_j}(g_j y) \succeq 0 \quad (j = 1, \dots, m)$:

$$
\begin{aligned} \inf_{y} \quad & \sum_{\alpha} f_\alpha y_\alpha \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & M_r(y) \succeq 0, \\ & M_{r-d_j}(g_j y) \succeq 0, \quad j=1,\dots,m, \\ & y_0 = 1 \quad (\int  d\mu = 1). \end{aligned}
$$

- The dual problem (SOS perspective): Use sum-of-squares representations to certify nonnegativity of $f(x) - \lambda$ on $K$. Find the [greatest lower bound](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E5%A4%A7%E4%B8%8B%E7%95%8C&zhida_source=entity) $\lambda$ such that $f(x) - \lambda = \sigma_0(x) + \sum_{j=1}^m \sigma_j(x) g_j(x)$, where $\sigma_j$ are SOS polynomials. Restricting the degrees of the SOS polynomials yields a family of SDPs called SOS relaxations. The positive semidefinite constraints $Q \succeq 0$ produced by SOS become the SDP variables. The objective and constraints of the original polynomial optimization problem are transformed into linear equalities and inequalities in terms of this SDP variable $Q$.

Strong duality guarantees that these two relaxations are dual to each other and typically have zero duality gap.

- Convergence: Let $\lambda_r$ be the [optimal value](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity) of the $r$-th order relaxation. Then $\lambda_r \le \lambda_{r+1} \le \cdots \le p^*$, and when $K$ satisfies the Archimedean condition (e.g., it is compact and contains an additional ball constraint), we have $\lim_{r\to\infty} \lambda_r = p^*$.
- Finite convergence and extraction of global solutions: In practice, for many problems, there exists a finite $r$ such that $\lambda_r = p^*$. In that case, one can check the [rank of the moment matrix](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9F%A9%E9%98%B5%E7%9A%84%E7%A7%A9&zhida_source=entity): if the rank of $M_r(y^*)$ equals a small constant (and satisfies a flatness condition), then the global minimizer(s) can be recovered from the moment vector by solving an eigenvalue problem. For example, if the rank is 1, the global minimum point is that single atom. More generally, one can extract several points and their weights, which form the support of the optimal measure.

Example: Consider the problem $\min_{x \in \{-1,1\}^n} x^T Q x$, which is nonconvex and NP-hard. We can rewrite this problem by dropping the Dirac constraint to obtain $\min_{\mu \in \mathcal{P}(\{-1,1\}^n)} \int z^T Q z \, d\mu(z)$, where now $\mu$ can be any [probability distribution](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A6%82%E7%8E%87%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83&zhida_source=entity) on the vertices of the hypercube. Then, via the moment representation and a semidefinite relaxation, we obtain an approximation to the original problem:

$$
\min \langle Q, Y \rangle, \quad \text{s.t.} \quad Y \succeq 0, \quad Y_{ii} = 1.
$$

With certain rounding techniques, one can also recover a high-quality approximate solution from $Y$.

**(2) Numerical Implementation Libraries**

Numerical implementation of the Lasserre hierarchy relies on modeling the problem as an SDP and solving it. This is typically done through specialized modeling tools:

- Modeling layer: The user inputs polynomial objectives and constraints, and the program automatically constructs the moment or SOS SDP. Common tools include:

  - GloptiPoly 3: A MATLAB toolbox that directly implements the Lasserre hierarchy, constructing moment relaxations and calling SDP solvers.
  - YALMIP: A MATLAB optimization [modeling language](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=279820842&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BB%BA%E6%A8%A1%E8%AF%AD%E8%A8%80&zhida_source=entity) with an SOS module that allows users to freely build SOS constraints.
  - SOSTOOLS: A dedicated SOS toolbox built on YALMIP.
  - The `PolyJuMP` and `SumOfSquares` packages in JuMP (Julia): Provide modern SOS/SDP modeling.

- Solver layer: The generated SDP data is fed into general-purpose SDP solvers such as MOSEK (commercial, efficient), SeDuMi, or SDPT3 (open source). For large-scale problems, specialized techniques may be required, such as exploiting problem structure (sparsity, symmetry) for dimension reduction, or using first-order methods like `CDCS` or `SDPNAL+`.

## V. A Unifying Language: Wasserstein Geometry

Optimal control, optimal transport, and stochastic analysis—these three streams each nurture their own system of partial differential equations. Yet ultimately, they all converge into the infinite-dimensional geometry on the space of measures. This connection has already been explored in areas of mathematics related to Hamiltonian dynamical systems or weak KAM theory.

### 1\. Optimal Transport and Optimal Control

Let $X, Y \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$, $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(X)$, $\nu \in \mathcal{P}(Y)$, with cost $c: X\times Y \to \mathbb{R}$. Strong duality holds between the Kantorovich primal problem (KP) and its dual (DP) (see Villani's book):

$$
\begin{aligned} \text{(KP)} \quad & \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{X\times Y} c(x,y) \, d\pi(x,y)=\inf_{T: T_\#\mu = \nu} \int c(x, T(x)) d\mu(x)  \\ \text{(DP)} \quad & \sup_{(\phi,\psi) \in L^1(\mu)\times L^1(\nu)} \left\{ \int_X \phi \, d\mu + \int_Y \psi \, d\nu \;:\; \phi(x) + \psi(y) \le c(x,y) \right\}. \end{aligned}
$$

The optimal $\phi$ is called the Kantorovich potential, and on the support of the optimal transport plan, $\phi(x) + \phi^c(y) = c(x,y)$, where $\phi^c(y) = \inf_{x\in X} [c(x,y)-\phi(x)]$.

Consider a control system $\dot{x}(t) = f(x(t), u(t)), \quad u(t) \in U, \; x(0)=x_0,$ with running cost $L(x,u)$ and terminal cost $\Phi(x(T))$. The value function is $V(t,x) = \inf_{u(\cdot)} \left\{ \int_t^T L(x(s),u(s)) ds + \Phi(x(T)) \right\}.$ The dynamic programming principle leads to the HJB equation: $-\partial_t V(t,x) + H(x, \nabla_x V(t,x)) = 0, \quad V(T,x) = \Phi(x)$, with the Hamiltonian $H(x,p) = \sup_{u\in U} \left( \langle p, f(x,u) \rangle - L(x,u) \right)$.

Rewriting optimal transport as an optimal control problem: $\dot{x} = u(t)$, with cost $\int_0^1 L(u(t)) dt$, subject to $x(0) \sim \mu_0$ and $x(1) \sim \mu_1$. Its occupation measure formulation is:

$$
\inf_{(\mu_t, v_t)} \left\{ \int_0^1 \int L(v_t) d\mu_t dt : \partial_t \mu_t + \nabla \cdot (\mu_t v_t) = 0, \mu_0, \mu_1 \text{ fixed} \right\}.
$$

### 2\. The Eikonal Equation Revisited

For costs of the $L^1$ type, we obtain the eikonal equation, and the dynamic first-hitting time function $T$ is entirely equivalent to the static Kantorovich potential $\phi$.

-   Distance cost $c(x,y) = \|x-y\|$: the Kantorovich dual constraint is $\phi(x) + \psi(y) \le \|x-y\|$. Setting $\psi = -\phi$, we get that $\phi$ is 1-Lipschitz: $\phi(x) - \phi(y) \le \|x-y\|$. When the target measure is concentrated at a single point $\nu = \delta_{x_0}$, the dual problem directly becomes:

    $$
    W_1(\mu, \delta_{x_0}) = \sup_{\phi} \left\{ \int \phi d\mu - \phi(x_0) \; \Bigg| \; |\nabla \phi| \le 1 \right\}.
    $$

    Clearly, to maximize the integral, $\phi$ must attain the maximum allowable slope, namely $\phi(x) = \text{dist}(x, x_0)$ (the distance function). Therefore, the optimal potential $\phi$ satisfies the eikonal equation: $\|\nabla \phi(x)\| = 1 \ \ \mu\text{-a.e.}$ This is the static eikonal equation, and $\phi$ is precisely the distance function.

-   Minimum-time problem. Suppose the control constraint is $|u| \le v(x)$, the running cost is $L \equiv 0$, and the terminal time $T$ is free until reaching the target set $\mathcal C$ (note this problem has a different running cost structure, but it is equivalent to the earlier minimum-time problem). The first-hitting time function $T(x) = \inf\{t \ge 0: x(t) \in \mathcal C\}$ satisfies the stationary HJB equation: $v(x) |\nabla T(x)| = 1 \iff |\nabla T(x)| = \frac{1}{v(x)}.$ This is the general eikonal equation. Its viscosity solution gives the global first-hitting time, which acts as the dynamic Kantorovich potential—the dynamic counterpart of the Kantorovich potential in optimal transport.

-   Alternatively, consider $L(u) = |u|/c$ with the control constraint $|u| \le c(x)$. In the dynamic programming occupation measure formulation, the Lagrangian is $L(v)$ subject to $|v| \le c$. Introducing the dual potential $\phi$ and performing Lagrange duality, we minimize pointwise over the velocity $v$: for fixed $\nabla \phi$, the inner minimization is

$$
\inf_{|v| \le c} \left( L(v) - \nabla \phi \cdot v \right) = \inf_{|v| \le c} \left( |v| /c - \nabla \phi \cdot v \right).
$$

For this value to be bounded below, we must have $|\nabla \phi| \le \frac{1}{c}$. In that case, the minimum inside the brackets is 0 (attained when $v$ is aligned with $\nabla \phi$ and $|v|=c$). The dual constraint thus becomes: $|\nabla \phi(t, x)| \le \frac{1}{c}, \quad \partial_t \phi \le 0.$ The Kantorovich potential $\phi$ (which is 1-Lipschitz) is exactly the first-hitting time function $T$.
