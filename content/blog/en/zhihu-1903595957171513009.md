## I. Overview

**Zero-order optimization** (ZOO), i.e., **black-box optimization**, encompasses many methods. But I think they can be broadly divided into two categories: model-based and model-free. I particularly want to focus on three parts of the model-based category: stochastic gradient estimation, [Bayesian optimization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Bayes%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity), and [distribution-based optimization methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity). I believe these directions have clear theory and great promise.

Zero-order optimization can be viewed as a branch of nonconvex, [nonsmooth optimization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%89%E6%BB%91%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity), because for convex problems and problems with sufficient [regularity](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AD%A3%E5%88%99%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) and smoothness, gradient-based algorithms can be used directly. As is well known, general optimization problems are intractable, so if one doesn't exploit the problem's structure, things are bound to fail. In practice, when the problem structure is known and good enough to permit non-zero-order algorithms, the average performance of non-zero-order algorithms is strictly better than that of [zero-order algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=3&q=%E9%9B%B6%E9%98%B6%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity).

At the fundamental level, if a problem provides only a zero-order [oracle](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Oracle&zhida_source=entity) and lacks information, then to guarantee finding a [global minimum](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%A8%E5%B1%80%E6%9E%81%E5%B0%8F%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity), one can only resort to exhaustive search. This is consistent with the No Free Lunch theorem. In other words, no optimization algorithm has an advantage on average. But the cost of exhaustive search is prohibitive. That's why we use [surrogate models](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E7%90%86%E6%A8%A1%E5%9E%8B&zhida_source=entity), heuristic methods, sample memory, and so on to emulate the black box. This is the exploration-exploitation trade-off that any information-limited (incomplete-information) problem faces.

As for what "complete information" means, I think my article explains it quite clearly; it's analogous to the completeness of a [state space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity).

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/708362997?share_code=1pYgzvsgwJysX&utm_psn=1906320232366211556](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/708362997?share_code=1pYgzvsgwJysX&utm_psn=1906320232366211556)

In short, the biggest difference between model-based and model-free methods is whether a surrogate model exists. A surrogate model is a way of modeling the [objective function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%AE%E6%A0%87%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) \( f \) when it is a black box, or transforming it into a new objective. In [model-free methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=3&q=%E6%97%A0%E6%A8%A1%E5%9E%8B%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity), since there is no surrogate model, one essentially constructs a (single-point or multi-point) [search strategy](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%90%9C%E7%B4%A2%E7%AD%96%E7%95%A5&zhida_source=entity); in model-based methods, one constructs a surrogate model through some technical tricks, and the problem reduces to optimizing the surrogate.

Model-free zero-order optimization primarily consists of **search-based methods** and **heuristic methods**, especially population-based [evolutionary algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%9B%E5%8C%96%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity). The difference is that the former relies on a single point to find a descent direction (though multi-point parallel strategies can be added), while the latter, population-based evolutionary algorithms, not only use multiple points simultaneously but also incorporate [evolutionary strategies](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%9B%E5%8C%96%E7%AD%96%E7%95%A5&zhida_source=entity) and various heuristic tricks.

## II. Pattern Search Methods

The basic idea of pattern search methods [\[1\]](#ref_1) is to approach the global minimum by repeatedly locating local minima (or "patterns") of the objective function. This simplifies computation and provides good [robustness](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%B2%81%E6%A3%92%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) and adaptability. As early as the 1960s, Friedlander and Mead proposed the first pattern search method. Since then, many variants and improvements have emerged, such as cyclic or unidirectional search patterns, the introduction of global and local distance concepts, and the incorporation of [trust-region](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BF%A1%E8%B5%96%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity) techniques.

As I mentioned in [Tasks, Planning, and Intelligence](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/708362997), the core of such simple search methods lies in **[coarse-graining](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%B2%97%E7%B2%92%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity)** or **heuristics**. Below I briefly introduce some typical pattern search methods, which can be classified by the type of "pattern" used: simplex-based, random sampling, search directions, golden-section, or grid-based. These are all coarse-graining or heuristic approaches. Among them, any method that uses search directions relies on [line search algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%90%9C%E7%B4%A2%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity).

### 1. Simplex Method (Nelder-Mead Algorithm) (1965) [\[2\]](#ref_2)

This is a very classic and effective algorithm. The basic idea is to use a simplex in the high-dimensional [search space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%90%9C%E7%B4%A2%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) to locate the optimal region, thereby approximating the optimal solution.

The advantage of a simplex is that its vertex representation is computationally convenient. By evaluating and sorting [vertex values](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%A1%B6%E7%82%B9%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity), and repeatedly applying reflection, expansion, contraction, and shrinkage operations to eliminate the worst point, the vertex values are progressively improved, reshaping the simplex and gradually approaching the function's minimum. The algorithm has good global search performance and robustness. However, it converges slowly and is sensitive to the initial solution. It is the default zero-order optimization algorithm in many optimization libraries.

### 2. Global Random Search

This is a rather trivial idea: sample randomly over the entire solution space and keep only the best point seen so far. The main difficulty with such algorithms is that there is no prior information, making the exploration-exploitation process inefficient and severely affected by problem dimensionality.

### 3. Greedy Local Search

Compared with global random sampling, this approach actually exploits problem-relevant information by searching for a descent direction near a [prior estimate](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%88%E9%AA%8C%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1&zhida_source=entity) and iterating. This effectively plays a role analogous to a gradient, and the introduction of this gradient-like mechanism provides better [convergence rates](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%94%B6%E6%95%9B%E9%80%9F%E7%8E%87&zhida_source=entity) and convergence guarantees than global search [\[3\]](#ref_3).

**(1) Hooke-Jeeves Algorithm (1961)** [\[4\]](#ref_4) **, Rosenbrock Algorithm (?)**

Both are unidirectional search algorithms. The Hooke-Jeeves algorithm is a relatively simple [greedy algorithm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B4%AA%E5%BF%83%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) that is effective and reliable. Starting from an initial point, it alternates between axial searches (exploratory moves) and pattern moves to progressively approach the optimum. It searches along specific directions, computing a step size for each direction to determine the next point. If a better function value is found, it continues; otherwise, it switches direction or step size. Termination is based on a step-size threshold, a maximum number of iterations, or the improvement in function value. Because the directions are fairly arbitrary, it is prone to getting stuck in local optima.

The Rosenbrock algorithm is similar to Hooke-Jeeves. However, Rosenbrock's method generates an orthogonal set of directions based on step sizes accumulated during the iteration. By dynamically adjusting this orthogonal set, it is more adaptive in high-dimensional problems, performing particularly well on functions like the [Rosenbrock function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Rosenbrock%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) that have narrow, fast-moving directions.

**(2) Powell's Algorithm (1964)**

Powell's algorithm can be viewed as a [conjugate direction method](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%B1%E8%BD%AD%E6%96%B9%E5%90%91%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity); it stems from a very simple observation.

It is an optimization method based on unidirectional search patterns and [orthogonal transformations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AD%A3%E4%BA%A4%E5%8F%98%E6%8D%A2&zhida_source=entity). The algorithm rotates the [coordinate system](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9D%90%E6%A0%87%E7%B3%BB&zhida_source=entity) to eliminate the influence of irrelevant directions and performs unidirectional searches along each direction to determine step sizes. This greatly reduces the number of searches and the computational load, thereby improving [convergence speed](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%94%B6%E6%95%9B%E9%80%9F%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity) and accuracy.

If the solution obtained by iterative analysis along certain directions no longer improves, the algorithm performs unidirectional searches and orthogonal transformations to eliminate the contour of the coordinate system. A single orthogonal transformation may involve transforming many coordinates, but it can quickly lead to better results.

**(3) Gaussian Local Search**

The search direction is multivariate Gaussian [random noise](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9A%8F%E6%9C%BA%E5%99%AA%E5%A3%B0&zhida_source=entity); points that improve the objective function value after adding this perturbation are retained.

**(4) Coordinate Descent Search**

Searches along coordinate axes, typically in the form of cyclic [coordinate descent](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9D%90%E6%A0%87%E4%B8%8B%E9%99%8D%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) or randomized coordinate descent. Its [global convergence](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=257456207&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%A8%E5%B1%80%E6%94%B6%E6%95%9B%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) relies on the assumption that the objective function converges to a unique minimum along any coordinate direction in sequence. With only a zero-order oracle, coordinate descent is no better than methods like Rosenbrock that adaptively adjust directions. However, as we'll see later, incorporating gradient estimation leads to much better performance.

### 4. Golden Section Search (GSS, 1953)

It is well known that golden section search is a zeroth-order line search method that achieves optimal search over the interval of a unimodal function. Its optimality is, I believe, already well explained in this article:

[https://www.zhihu.com/question/27999527/answer/2386541807](https://www.zhihu.com/question/27999527/answer/2386541807)

Building on this, extending to higher dimensions requires first partitioning unimodal intervals along each dimension.

### 5. Grid Search

This is an exhaustive search method that finds the optimum by traversing all possible combinations of values for the optimization variables. The key idea is coarse-graining, but clearly the complexity grows exponentially with both the problem dimension and the granularity of the value partitioning.

### 6. Summary

In summary, the convergence of pattern search methods is acceptable in certain applications, and their simplicity and ease of use have led to continuously expanding applications and attracted a growing number of researchers to study them from various perspectives, incorporating additional strategies (clever tricks) to further improve algorithmic performance and scope of applicability. Moreover, regardless of the search method, as long as there is an optimization step, convergence can be guaranteed (under certain regularization conditions) via global convergence theorems.

However, the drawbacks of these algorithms are equally evident: whether it is the simplex method, unidirectional search, conjugate direction, or golden section search, they are all **sensitive to initialization**, making it difficult to achieve global search with a single particle; in high dimensions, due to the inherent limitations of search-based algorithms, they suffer from slow convergence and difficulty in searching when facing the **curse of dimensionality**; as iterative algorithms, they typically require tuning certain parameters to determine search directions, step sizes, and stopping criteria. **Reliance on empirical parameter tuning** is difficult and unwise; many of these algorithms involve line search, and their convergence often depends on exact line search, yet in practice one must resort to inexact line search using certain criteria. Furthermore, some heuristic strategies also make **convergence proofs difficult**.

## III. Additional Strategies

### 1. Randomization Strategies

In the exhaustive search or unidirectional search of pattern search methods, randomization strategies can be introduced to mitigate the rapid growth in complexity as dimensionality increases. Randomization can be naturally incorporated into the processes of search sampling and search direction selection.

### 2. Sample Memory Strategies

Sequential search uses sample memory strategies, where previous iterations and explorations inform and guide subsequent iterations.

## IV. Metaheuristic Algorithms

This is a rather controversial class of algorithms, and I will not delve into it in detail here, only sharing my personal views.

### 1. Classical Algorithms and Paper-Milling

First, the foundational classical algorithms such as genetic (evolutionary) algorithms (GA) [\[5\]](#ref_5), ant colony optimization (ACO) [\[6\]](#ref_6), particle swarm optimization (PSO) [\[7\]](#ref_7) [\[8\]](#ref_8), and simulated annealing (SA) [\[9\]](#ref_9) all have their merits. For instance, their underlying idea is to enhance search performance through certain strategies built upon a population of agents. However, the subsequent flood of "zoo algorithms" produced merely for the sake of publishing papers pale in comparison. That said, these algorithms were originally proposed as heuristic methods with highly flexible strategy construction, which naturally leaves considerable room for paper milling.

### 2. Applicable Scenarios

Many people today blindly believe in metaheuristic algorithms. Although they can handle arbitrary optimization problems, as mentioned earlier: **when the problem structure is known and sufficiently favorable, and nonzero-order algorithms can be used, their average performance will necessarily surpass that of zeroth-order algorithms.** Therefore, the "universal solver" claim is either foolish or disingenuous. Some fundamental theoretical issues remain unresolved. For example, there are no guaranteed bounds on the quality of suboptimal solutions found across different objective functions; complex objective functions may require very long runtimes to obtain an acceptable solution; the number of parameters is large and tuning is difficult, and the generalization ability of parameters across objectives of varying complexity is questionable. Computational speed bottlenecks limit their application to large-scale optimization problems, since the complexity of a single iteration scales with the population size. In metaheuristic algorithms, a few thousand decision variables already counts as large-scale, which is not considered large for ordinary optimization algorithms; real-world problems can sometimes involve millions of variables.

Thus, their applicable scenarios are relatively fixed: 1. hyperparameter optimization for neural networks; 2. certain combinatorial optimization or NP-hard problems with specific structures, such as large-scale resource scheduling; 3. black-box optimization problems where surrogate models are difficult to construct; 4. multi-objective optimization problems... These are all cases that are difficult for other algorithms to handle.

### 3. The Importance of Problem Structure

Moreover, metaheuristic algorithms also need to leverage problem structure more effectively to design specific strategies (this brings to mind a like-minded perspective in this post: [some traditional "rule-based" methods (but...)](https://www.zhihu.com/pin/1899432476243399609?native=1&scene=share&share_code=knfn3oabB8Z2&utm_psn=1938387623707121342), where heuristic operations are artificial constraints imposed on search patterns and the solution space). If the task-orientation is too weak, the method may perform no better than grid search; if too strong, generalization is lost. Moreover, one cannot escape the balance between global search and convergence (exploration versus exploitation). Simply increasing the population size or resorting to flashy tricks to enhance exploration will not work, as individual interactions consume substantial complexity. Such brute-force approaches do not even reduce the total number of individuals compared to grid search, and many flashy heuristic methods are far less simple than grid search.

### 4. Related Theory

Previously, theoretical analysis of evolutionary algorithms remained at a fairly rudimentary stage. Although Markov chain methods have been used to prove that evolutionary algorithms converge asymptotically with probability 1 to the global optimum, this result is of limited practical value. Runtime cannot be infinite, and when to stop searching is unknown (due to the lack of optimality criteria). The condition of convergence in probability is itself very weak—completely random search also satisfies it. It is worth noting that there are still some valuable theoretical results. For example, **distribution-based optimization methods can provide better theoretical explanations for evolutionary algorithms and simulated annealing, while consensus-based optimization can offer better theoretical foundations for particle swarm methods**. These all fall under model-based methods.
