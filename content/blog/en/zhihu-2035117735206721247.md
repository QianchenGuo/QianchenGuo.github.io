## Key Insights

-   For robots and living beings alike, the relationship between actions described in physical space and motions originating in motor space generally turns to geometry for solutions, specifically linear algebra. In life sciences, optimization principles applied to sensorimotor control have unraveled the mysteries of empirical data. Since the 1970s, the idea of describing robot actions as quasi-optimal motion has been developed in robotics.
-   Among all possible motions performing a given action, optimization algorithms tend to select the best motion according to a given performance criterion. Moreover, they also support the realization of secondary actions.
-   Optimal motion is a signature of action. How can the optimization criterion hidden beneath a given action be revealed? This question opens challenging topics in inverse optimal control.

## 1. Action and Motion

Motion is a fundamental property of living systems. To survive, both plants and animals must move. Animals differ from plants in that they must explore the world to forage. Carnivorous plants, rooted in place, capture careless insects. These plants must rely on egocentric motions. Meanwhile, a cheetah leaves its den to hunt.

Foraging is a paradigmatic action. In the physical world, any action involves egocentric motion, exploratory motion, or both. By analogy, manipulator robots use egocentric motions, mobile robots explore the world by moving, and humanoid robots combine both types of motion.

Actions take place in physical space. Motion, in turn, originates in motor control space. Robots, like any living system, access physical space only indirectly through sensors and motors. Robot motion planning and control explore the relationships among physical, sensory (task space), and motor (control) spaces; these three spaces are grounded in geometry [\[1\]](#ref_1). How, then, can actions described in physical space be transformed into motions described in motor coordinates? This inversion is the fundamental problem in robotics.

In life sciences, researchers acknowledge that optimization principles in sensorimotor control clearly explain empirical data, or at least do so better than other principles [\[2\]](#ref_2). In the 1970s, Whitney pioneered the idea of describing robot actions as quasi-optimal motion in robotics [\[3\]](#ref_3). Today, this idea has been substantially developed in classical robot control [\[4\]](#ref_4), and new paradigms have emerged alongside it in multidisciplinary approaches [\[5\]](#ref_5). Motion optimization appears to be a natural law for action selection. However, as noted in the companion article [\[6\]](#ref_6), the optimization equations are intractable in most cases, and their numerical optimization is notoriously slow in engineering.

This article aims to briefly survey recent advances in this field. First, we explain why optimization techniques for robot motion should be viewed as a motion selection principle for redundant robots. In this regard, we review the results and challenges raised by recent applications on humanoid robots. The remainder of the article is devoted to inverse optimal control. As a method, inverse optimal control allows us to better understand natural phenomena and transform them into engineering practice. This problem opens several highly challenging questions. In this direction, methods based on recently proposed polynomial optimization techniques appear complementary to classical machine learning approaches.

## 2. From Task Space to Control Space: Merits and Limitations of Linearization

There are many ways to transform robot actions into motions in the robot control space, such as the operational space formulation [\[7\]](#ref_7) and the task function approach [\[8\]](#ref_8), to name but a few. The notion of task encompasses the concept of actions described in physical space. Task space may be physical space (e.g., placing the end-effector of a manipulator at a position defined in the world coordinate frame) or sensory space (e.g., tracking an object in the robot camera frame). The so-called task function serves to establish the link between task space and control space.

Due to the underlying strong nonlinear transformation, solving the inverse problem is extremely expensive (obtaining a few seconds of motion may require minutes or hours of computation). To meet the time constraints imposed by robot control rates, researchers can only solve the problem locally by considering the tangent spaces of both task space and configuration space. This linearization involves the Jacobian matrix [\[9\]](#ref_9) and draws on all the techniques of linear algebra. Linearization is particularly attractive because the tangent space of configuration space contains configuration velocities (typically the robot control inputs). A dynamic extension of this principle also allows for torque-based control [\[7\]](#ref_7).

The Jacobian matrix varies with the robot configuration, making trajectory search nonlinear. However, for a given configuration, it defines a linear problem relating the unknown system velocity to the velocity in task space (given as a reference). From a numerical standpoint, this problem is linear and can be easily solved at each instant to obtain the system velocity. Integrating this velocity over a time interval from an initial configuration yields a trajectory that tends toward completing the task. Similarly, this velocity can be applied to the robot in real time to drive it toward the goal. At each new configuration obtained after sensor measurement updates, the linear problem is reinitialized and the process is repeated. This iterative principle corresponds to descent iteration algorithms (such as gradient descent or Newton–Raphson) used to numerically compute zeros of a given function. However, the outcome of this method goes further: assuming sufficiently small descent steps, the sequence of descent iterations is a discretization of an actual trajectory from the initial configuration to the goal. The drawback of instantaneous linearization is that it provides no look-ahead capability to the control, and near non-convex obstacles it may typically drive the robot toward local minima. This is the well-known curse of linearization.

## 3. Motion Selection: The Dimensionality Problem

The dimension of the task space may be equal to, greater than, or less than that of the control space. For simplicity, we consider the task space to be a manifold that describes the position of the end-effector of a fully actuated manipulator.

When the task space and configuration space have equal dimensions, each point in the task space defines a configuration[\[10\]](#ref_10), and the task function can be used to drive the robot to a unique configuration. No motion selection problem arises here. The Jacobian matrix is an invertible square matrix, and solving the linear problem is fairly straightforward. In this case, the task function approach was originally used to define the property of admissibility for connecting two points in configuration space while avoiding singularities[\[8\]](#ref_8).

In other cases, optimization serves as the principle of motion selection. The choice of the optimization criterion determines how the Jacobian matrix is inverted, as discussed below.

When the task space dimension is greater than the configuration space dimension, it is sometimes impossible to find a configuration that satisfies the task objective: the task function is not surjective (onto), meaning the Jacobian matrix has more rows than columns. In this case, no velocity in the configuration tangent space corresponds to a velocity in the task tangent space. For example, such situations arise in visual servoing when many points need to be tracked in the camera frame[\[11\]](#ref_11), and in SLAM when optimizing the camera pose relative to multiple landmarks[\[12\]](#ref_12). Optimization is also used to find a velocity that minimizes the error in the task tangent space. The problem then becomes one of **minimizing the distance to the reference task vector**. Typically, the reference task vector is unreachable. In the special case where the reference belongs to the image space of the Jacobian, the optimization residual is zero. This occurs in visual servoing when the target image has been acquired from the actual scene and is noise-free.

> Inverse engineering techniques can be used to identify actions from observations of motion.

Conversely, if the task space dimension is smaller than the configuration space dimension, then multiple configurations correspond to a single task. The task function is not one-to-one; that is, the Jacobian matrix has more columns than rows. For example, in the scenario of a 30-joint humanoid robot picking up a ball with its hand, the task space has dimension 3 while the configuration space has dimension 30. Several motions can accomplish the task. For this task, the system is said to be **redundant**. Optimization is then used as a criterion for selecting among all admissible motions. In this case, several vectors in the configuration tangent space produce the same effect in the task space. Likewise, certain velocities produce no effect in the task space. This subset of the configuration tangent space is the kernel of the Jacobian matrix, called the **null space** of the task. Any velocity in the null space leaves the task unchanged. Adding the null space to a given configuration tangent vector that satisfies the task yields the vector space of all velocities that satisfy the task. **Selecting a sample from this space according to some criterion (e.g., minimum-norm velocity)** gives rise to a minimization problem.

In general, the task function may be neither surjective nor one-to-one; that is, the Jacobian matrix may be neither row-full-rank (its rows not linearly independent) nor column-full-rank (its columns not linearly independent). In general, no vector in the configuration tangent space satisfies the task (because the transformation is not surjective), but infinitely many vectors minimize the distance to the task vector in the task tangent space (because the transformation is not one-to-one). The selection problem thus becomes a double minimization problem: we need to **simultaneously minimize the distance to the task and the norm of the configuration velocity**. The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse[\[13\]](#ref_13) (also known as the least-squares inverse) provides the solution to this double minimization problem. Note that by changing the metric in the tangent space, other minimization criteria can be considered within the same framework. For example, we may use a weighted pseudoinverse in which the components of the system input (columns of the Jacobian) and the task residuals (rows of the Jacobian) carry different weights. As noted earlier, the sum of the optimal vector and the null space gives the set of all optimal solutions to the first problem (minimum distance to the task), but it is only suboptimal for the second problem (minimum velocity norm).

## 4. Optimization as a Selection Principle

### 4.1 Task Stacks for Redundant Systems

When a robot is redundant with respect to a given task, it becomes interesting to assign it secondary tasks. For humanoid robots, this is the case where the robot can perform two tasks simultaneously. Consider two distinct task functions, one for the position of the right hand and one for the left. How do we check whether these two tasks conflict? A simple idea is to impose an ordering between the two tasks. At each time step of the integration process, a vector is selected from the configuration tangent space associated with the first task. The secondary task is then considered only within the restricted set of velocities lying in the kernel of the first task. The reasoning applied to the first task can also be applied to the projected secondary task: the task function may be surjective, one-to-one, or neither. Specifically, if it is not surjective, the task is said to be **singular** (in the sense that the Jacobian is rank-deficient). Two cases can now be distinguished.

- If the (non-projected) secondary task function is not surjective, the singularity is called a **kinematic singularity**: it is kinematic in nature, arising from the secondary task itself.
- Conversely, if the (non-projected) secondary task function is surjective, the singularity is called an **algorithmic singularity**: the secondary task becomes singular because of a conflict with the primary task[\[14\]](#ref_14).
- Outside the set of algorithmic singularities, the two tasks are said to be non-conflicting, and the order in which they are executed is irrelevant.

This reasoning process can be iterated for additional tasks, yielding what is known as a **task stack**[\[15\]](#ref_15). The dimension of the successive null spaces is thereby reduced. The process terminates when all tasks have been processed, or once the null-space dimension vanishes (see Figure 3). In the latter case, we can still select a minimum-norm vector among the remaining vectors in the null space.

The null space was first used within the framework of numerical analysis to guide the gradients of sequential optimization (the projected gradient method)[\[16\]](#ref_16). In robotics, it was used to perform positioning tasks with redundant robots while accounting for joint limits[\[17\]](#ref_17). Nakamura generalized it to any number of tasks[\[18\]](#ref_18), and Siciliano proposed its recursive formulation[\[19\]](#ref_19) (see also Baerlocher in the context of computer animation[\[20\]](#ref_20)).

In this article, we restrict ourselves to inverse kinematics—that is, computing robot velocities from task constraints on reference velocities. The same approach can be used in inverse dynamics to compute system torques[\[21\]](#ref_21) (typically joint torques, but also tendon forces or other actuation parameters) from homogeneous operational constraints (typically reference forces or accelerations). In this case, the Euclidean norm is irrelevant, and researchers generally prefer a weighted inverse to impose minimum energy on the motion.

### 4.2 Task Stacks, Quadratic Programming, and Inequality Constraints

A task stack is akin to quadratic programming: a quadratic program is an optimization problem involving a set of linear constraints and a quadratic cost (e.g., fitting a linear function in the least-squares sense). In this way, it resembles a stack of two tasks: the first (higher-priority) task is a constraint; the secondary task is the cost to be minimized. However, the two are not entirely analogous: constraints in a quadratic program must be admissible (at least one feasible solution must exist), which is not the case for the primary task. Moreover, the task stack can be extended to accommodate more than two tasks.

So far, we have discussed tasks corresponding to equalities in the configuration tangent space, for which the best solution satisfying the equation can be obtained in the least-squares sense. Let us now consider a region defined by a set of inequalities: the robot is free to move within the region but must remain inside it; when the task becomes infeasible, it should minimize the distance to the region in the least-squares sense. Such inequality constraints cannot be solved directly by the methods described in this article.

When inequality constraints are satisfied, the first approach historically sets a zero velocity in the task space. This is Khatib's artificial potential field method[\[22\]](#ref_22): the target region is described by a low or zero cost, with increasing cost nearer the constraint boundary, following the behavior of barrier functions used in interior-point numerical algorithms. The gradient of this function then acts as a virtual force pushing the robot toward the interior of the region when it approaches the boundary; inside the region, the function has zero or very low influence.

For robot control, when the robot is pushed against a constraint, penalty functions are generally preferred over barrier functions to prevent poor numerical behavior. For simple tasks, or when the inequality task has the lowest priority, the resulting behavior is always satisfactory: when the inequality condition is satisfied, the robot does not need to move. However, imposing a hierarchy with this approach is quite difficult. Specifically, in highly redundant scenarios (e.g., a six-joint robot arm reaching in three-dimensional space), if the inequality task is of secondary importance, the gradient projection method can be used[\[23\]](#ref_23). When the inequality task has higher priority, saturation at a boundary of the task region will correspond to the assignment of a degree of freedom[\[24\]](#ref_24), which is allocated for determining the velocity orthogonal to that boundary. Thus, this degree of freedom is no longer available to any secondary task. Moreover, when the robot moves freely inside the region (sufficiently far from all boundaries), secondary tasks may use this degree of freedom. To exploit the redundancy offered within the region defined by the inequality constraints, the corresponding degrees of freedom should be dynamically allocated. If the inequality constraint is satisfied, the degree of freedom is unallocated and available for secondary tasks: the constraint is then said to be inactive. If the constraint is violated, the corresponding degree of freedom is used to satisfy the constraint as best as possible: the constraint is then said to be active.

The set of all active constraints is called the active set. Active-set search algorithms are iterative solution schemes that search over all possibly active constraints. At each iteration, a candidate solution satisfying the active constraints is computed. Based on the status of active and inactive constraints relative to the candidate solution, the active set is modified and the process repeated. Active-set algorithms are the classic method for solving quadratic programs with inequality constraints. Priorities between multiple conflicting objectives can also be introduced, leading to hierarchical quadratic programs[\[25\]](#ref_25).

We now illustrate the task-stack framework with an example of the HPR2 humanoid robot simultaneously performing two grasping tasks while respecting balance constraints. All elementary tasks are embedded within a single global trajectory. We will see that the hierarchy introduced in the quadratic program induces a structure in the task vector space. The global trajectory then appears as a composition of elementary motions, each motion characterizing a given task (or subtask). Inverse engineering can then be used to determine the "meaning" of the motion—that is, the various tasks being incorporated within it.

## 5. Motion as a Signature of Action: A Humanoid Robot Case Study

We review here two practical applications of task stacks on the humanoid robot HRP2[\[26\]](#ref_26). The first application illustrates how complex actions can be described when all body segments are involved and physical constraints are respected. The second application illustrates how actions can be identified from motion observations through reverse engineering.

### 5.1 From Action to Motion: Using Optimization-Based Selection Principles in Practice

The task stack is a versatile tool for generating and controlling robot motion. Given an initial configuration, tasks are added to the stack, and the resulting velocities are integrated until all active tasks converge, thereby generating motion. Task stacks can be used in a variety of robotic scenarios. In humanoid robots, classical tasks include reaching (described by the placement of the end-effector), visual servoing (described by controlling the gaze toward an object's position in the image plane of the robot's camera), or quasi-static balance (described by controlling the center of mass such that its projection on the floor remains within the foot support polygon).

For example, when generating the motion in Figure 2 (left column), the two feet and the center of mass were constrained to their initial positions, and two tasks were set to control both the right hand and the gaze onto a ball in front of the robot. The robot leans forward to reach the ball. In doing so, its center of mass moves forward. Its left hand moves backward to compensate for this center-of-mass motion. The left hand's movement does not correspond to any specific action; it is merely a byproduct of maintaining balance.

This motion can be easily modified by setting new tasks or changing the desired values of active tasks. For example, the motion in Figure 2 (right column) can be generated by adding a task that controls the left hand's position and orientation, placing it at the final position from the first scenario. This new task is a reach: the left hand must touch the target. In both scenarios, the two left-hand movements look very similar, but their meanings differ. In the first scenario, the motion is unintentional: the left hand moves for the purpose of controlling the center of mass; its motion is then an incidental consequence of other tasks. In the second scenario, the motion is intentional: the left hand moves explicitly to pick up the given target. A detailed analysis of the subtle differences between the two left-hand motions resolves the ambiguity. This can be achieved through reverse engineering.

### 5.2 From Motion to Action: Reverse Engineering for Action Recognition

This hierarchical structure artificially [decouples](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%A7%A3%E8%80%A6&zhida_source=entity) the tasks in the stack to avoid any conflict between two distinct tasks. Its side effect is that trajectories entering a given active task's space are unaffected by any other task. For example, in Figure 2 (right column), the task stack decouples the left hand so that it can move independently of the other two tasks. Consequently, during motion generation, the trajectory within a given task's space characterizes that task's activity.

Consider the following problem: we observe the motion of a system whose possible controllers are known. Observing only the joint trajectories, the question is how to determine, among the possible controllers, which ones are active and with which parameters. Reproducing a task is straightforward: using the corresponding task function, the configuration trajectory is projected into all candidate task spaces. By matching the projected trajectory against the task model, the best task can be selected (matching and subsequent selection are again performed through optimization).

However, if the task stack artificially decouples the active tasks, coupling may appear between some candidate tasks: for example, due to the close proximity of the wrist and elbow [kinematic chains](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%90%E5%8A%A8%E9%93%BE&zhida_source=entity), their trajectories share many similarities. These similarities can lead to false positives in detection. To avoid this issue, the most relevant task is selected first. Then, by projecting the configuration trajectory into the null space of the detected task, the motion due to that task is canceled. The [detection algorithm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A3%80%E6%B5%8B%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) is then iterated until all tasks are identified, that is, until the residual motion after successive projections is zero[\[27\]](#ref_27).

From top to bottom: the original motion; after removing the right-hand task; after removing the center-of-mass task; after removing the gaze task; after removing the left-hand task; after removing the left-foot task; after removing the right-foot task. This detection algorithm can distinguish between the two visually similar motions performed in Figure 2 without using any background information. Figure 3 illustrates the successive projections. The tasks are removed in the order given by the detection algorithm. First, the right-hand task is removed (second row), then the center of mass (third row): this cancels most of the left-hand motion, since the coupling among the three tasks is significant; however, a small residual of the left-hand motion remains. In contrast, the head motion (which is nearly decoupled from the right hand and the center of mass) remains quite significant. It becomes exactly zero after the gaze task is removed (fourth row). The remaining left-hand motion can only be explained by a left-hand task, which is detected as active and subsequently removed. Finally, the two foot constraints are detected and removed. The effect of removing the first foot (sixth row) is quite noticeable.

The algorithm achieves very good performance in recognizing actions and distinguishing between visually similar robot motions. Beyond robotics, the method can also be applied to human action recognition. However, it has a critical prerequisite: knowledge of the optimization principle underlying the generation of intentional movements. Indeed, the algorithm relies on several action features that are typical consequences of specific [cost functions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E4%BB%B7%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity). The method also requires computational models of the coordination strategies used by humans in order to design several simultaneously occurring movement primitives. These avenues are highly promising for future research combining [computational neuroscience](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97%E7%A5%9E%E7%BB%8F%E7%A7%91%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) and robotics.

At this stage, we have seen how optimization principles and the concept of tasks help researchers lay the foundation for a symbolic representation of actions from motion: an action is viewed as the result of an optimization process, whose cost characterizes the action. The next section addresses the dual problem of identifying action features from motion.

## 6. Inverse Optimal Control

In this section, we begin with a case study drawn from humanoid robotics. Suppose we want a humanoid robot to walk like a human, that is, to follow human-like trajectories. The question is: what is the computational basis of human motion trajectories? In the first stage, we note that motion trajectories are highly stereotyped across repeated trials and across subjects. This approach is based on the statistical analysis of a large motion-capture database of trajectories (seven subjects, over 1500 trajectories)[\[28\]](#ref_28). Then, in the second stage, we assume that human motion trajectories follow some optimization principle. This is a common hypothesis in the study of human or animal movement. The question then becomes: which cost functional is minimized in human motion? In practice, we assume that humans and robots follow the same model—that is, we precisely know the [differential equations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) describing the motion under given control actions, as well as the constraints that the system state must satisfy. A database of trajectories is also available. Given this knowledge, determining the cost functional minimized in human motion becomes an inverse optimal control problem.

Pioneering work on inverse optimality in control dates back to the 1960s, applied to [economics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BB%8F%E6%B5%8E%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) and systems theory[\[29\]](#ref_29). Similarly, regarding the optimal stabilization problem, researchers know that every [value function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%80%BC%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) of the optimal stabilization problem is also a Lyapunov function of the closed-loop system. Freeman and Kokotovic[\[30\]](#ref_30) proved the [converse statement](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%80%86%E5%91%BD%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity) to be true: that is, every [Lyapunov](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9D%8E%E9%9B%85%E6%99%AE%E8%AF%BA%E5%A4%AB&zhida_source=entity) function of every stable closed-loop system is also the value function of a meaningful optimal stabilization problem.

In static optimization, the direct problem consists of finding, from a feasible set $K$, a feasible point $x$ that minimizes a given cost function $f$. We formulate the associated inverse optimization problem as follows: given a feasible point $y$ in $K$, find a cost criterion $g$ that minimizes the norm of the difference $g-f$, where $g$ is determined such that $y$ is an optimal solution of the direct optimization problem using the cost criterion $g$ (rather than $f$). When $f$ is the [zero function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9B%B6%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity), this becomes the static version of the inverse optimal control problem. Pioneering work dates back to the 1990s for linear problems and Manhattan norms. For the latter, the inverse problem again becomes a linear problem with the same norm. Similar results hold for inverse linear problems with the infinity norm. Interested readers can find a good survey in Heuberger's work, which describes inverse optimization used in linear programming and [combinatorial optimization problems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BB%84%E5%90%88%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity)[\[31\]](#ref_31). Note that in inverse optimization, the main difficulty lies in establishing a tractable global optimality characterization for a given point and a candidate cost criterion. This is why most of the aforementioned research deals with linear programming or combinatorial optimization problems, since they admit a global optimality characterization that can sometimes be efficiently used in practice. This also explains why inverse (nonlinear) optimization has previously received less attention.

Recently, some progress has been made in inverse polynomial optimization; in other words, inverse optimization problems with polynomial objective functions and [semi-algebraic sets](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%8A%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0%E9%9B%86&zhida_source=entity) as feasible sets[\[32\]](#ref_32). Powerful representations from real [algebraic geometry](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity)[\[33\]](#ref_33) characterize global [optimality](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) through certain positivity conditions. These can be expressed as [linear matrix inequalities](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%9F%A9%E9%98%B5%E4%B8%8D%E7%AD%89%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity) (LMIs) in the unknown vector of coefficients of the polynomial cost function. The feasible set in the latter is convex, and we can optimize efficiently over this set via semidefinite programming[\[34\]](#ref_34), a powerful convex optimization technique. We can then show that computing the inverse optimal solution can be reduced to solving a hierarchy of semidefinite programs of increasing size.

Returning to the inverse optimal control problem for anthropomorphic motion, we can **consider a set of basis functions** to describe candidate cost functions. The approach proposed by Mombaur et al.[\[35\]](#ref_35) relies on two main algorithms: an efficient direct multiple [shooting method](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%89%93%E9%9D%B6%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) for solving optimal control problems, and advanced optimization techniques to ensure a match between the solution of the (direct) optimal control problem and the measured data. Once the optimal cost function is identified (from a given family of basis function combinations), the direct optimal control solution can be implemented on a humanoid robot. To date, this method has proven at least reasonably efficient on a range of test problems. However, it requires the basis function class to be predefined. Furthermore, at each iteration of the algorithm, the direct shooting method only provides a local optimum. Thus, global optimality is not guaranteed.

An alternative approach is to extend the methodology developed for **inverse polynomial optimization**[\[32\]](#ref_32) to the inverse optimal control setting. Note that when the [optimal value](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity) function is known, the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation is a perfect tool for verifying the global optimality of a given state-[control trajectory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E8%BD%A8%E8%BF%B9&zhida_source=entity). The basic idea is to use a relaxed HJB-optimality equation as a certificate of global optimality for experimental trajectories stored in a database. One typically assumes that the optimal value function is continuous and can be approximated by a polynomial on a compact region. If we seek an integral [cost function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%88%90%E6%9C%AC%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) whose integrand $\int h$ is also a polynomial, then this global optimality certificate can be used to compute $h$ and its associated (polynomial) optimal value function by solving semidefinite programs. Following the approach in [\[32\]](#ref_32), we solve a hierarchy of semidefinite programs of increasing size. At each step of this hierarchy, either the [semidefinite program](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%8A%E5%AE%9A%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity) has no solution, or any optimal solution $h$ makes the trajectories in the database globally optimal for the problem with cost $\int h$. The higher the level of the hierarchy, the better the quality of the solution (but at the price of higher [computational cost](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97%E6%88%90%E6%9C%AC&zhida_source=entity)).

In addition to polynomial optimization techniques, several other methods have recently been introduced from the **geometric perspective of [optimal control theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity)**. Notable results have been achieved in the context of pointing movements[\[36\]](#ref_36): based on Thom's [transversality](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A8%AA%E6%88%AA%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) theory, the cost structure is derived from properties highlighted in experimental data. For example, these properties may characterize periods of muscle inactivity during movement. This qualitative approach has also been successfully applied to human motion[\[37\]](#ref_37).

We have presented the inverse optimal control problem from the perspective of biomimetic approaches to robot control: how to synthesize natural motion laws and derive from them optimal control models for robots. We have highlighted recent developments in inverse polynomial optimization. It should be noted that this article is far from covering all approaches to inverse optimal control. Inverse optimal control is also an active research area in machine learning (imitation learning). In the context of reinforcement learning[\[38\]](#ref_38)[\[39\]](#ref_39), **inverse reinforcement learning** constitutes another solution paradigm based on Markov decision processes, achieving impressive results on challenging problems such as [helicopter control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274278977&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%B4%E5%8D%87%E6%9C%BA%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity)[\[40\]](#ref_40). The theoretical foundations of this approach stem from stochastic control (see Kober et al.[\[39\]](#ref_39) and the references therein).

## 7. Computation: A Practical or Theoretical Problem?

In computer animation, optimization-based motion generation yields good results when measured against realism inspired by nature. For example, numerical optimization can be used to produce highly realistic walking, leaping, or running motions for humanoid artifacts. These complex body structures comprise up to 12 body segments and 25 degrees of freedom[\[41\]](#ref_41). At first glance, this approach could be applied a priori to humanoid robots. Figure 4 (top) provides an example depicting how HRP2 crosses very large obstacles[\[42\]](#ref_42).

![](/blog-assets/zhihu/2035117735206721247/image-06.jpg)

Figure 4. Results obtained with two gait motions: (top) based on whole-body trajectory optimization[\[41\]](#ref_41) (courtesy of K. Mombaur); (bottom) based on a walking pattern generator using a linearized inverted pendulum[\[42\]](#ref_42) (courtesy of O. Stasse[\[39\]](#ref_39)). Whole-body optimization enables the robot to achieve higher performance, but numerical computation remains too slow for an effective controller.

However, robotics imposes physical constraints that do not exist in the virtual world, along with stringent computational performance requirements. Bipedal walking is a typical example where technological limitations suggest the need to seek alternative formulations. The bottleneck here is the ability of control algorithms to satisfy real-time constraints.

In current model-based simulation experiments, computation time is measured in minutes. This timescale of minutes is vastly different from real-time control. For instance, to ensure the stability of a standing humanoid robot, the upper bound on computation time must be kept within a few milliseconds. Therefore, to leverage general-purpose optimization techniques for real-time control, one must either construct simplified models or develop specialized methods. This issue constitutes an active research direction at the intersection of robot control and numerical optimization.

Research on walking motion generation for humanoid robots provides one example. The most popular walking pattern generators are based on a simplified model of the human body: the linearized inverted pendulum model. It was proposed by Kajita et al. (**ZMP**)[\[43\]](#ref_43) and developed for the HRP2 humanoid robot. The method rests on two major assumptions:

- Setting a constant center-of-mass height simplifies the control model;
- The foot trajectories are known.

The advantage of assumption 1 lies in transforming the originally nonlinear problem into a linear one. The resulting model is low-dimensional and can be handled through an optimization formulation[\[44\]](#ref_44). With this formulation, assumption 2 is no longer needed. As a result, the method yields an online walking motion generator with automatic foot placement. It relies on linear model predictive control (the control loop supported by the associated quadratic program being much faster than the original control loop described by Kajita et al.[\[43\]](#ref_43)). In practice, with state-of-the-art solvers, solving the full quadratic program takes less than 1 millisecond. Moreover, in specific scenarios, one can design an optimization algorithm that reduces the computation time by a factor of 100[\[44\]](#ref_44).

Figure 4 (bottom) shows an example of this approach, enabling a real HRP2 to cross obstacles. The model-based simplification approach allows the robot to achieve real-time control. However, the simplified model does not fully exploit the robot's dynamics. The generated motions are not as optimal as those obtained by optimizing the whole-body trajectory. Consequently, the same performance cannot be achieved (in this case, the same obstacle height cannot be crossed): whole-body optimization enables the robot to reach higher performance, but only offline. To achieve the same performance online, either more powerful computers (running the same algorithms) or greater computational resources are required.

## Conclusion

The concept of optimality in robot motion is rich, with a wide variety of definitions and application domains. One goal of this article has been to summarize several viewpoints and references scattered across multiple fields: robotics, control, differential geometry, numerical optimization, machine learning, and even neurobiology.

The aim was to emphasize **the expressive power of optimal motion in modeling robot actions**, while also highlighting **the current challenges of using numerical optimization in real-time control for complex robots such as humanoids**. A second objective was to report on recent developments in **inverse optimal control**. While its stochastic form is popular in machine learning (imitation learning), other paradigms are currently emerging in differential geometric control theory and polynomial optimization.

As demonstrated in the companion paper to this article[\[6\]](#ref_6), robotics provides a rich benchmark for optimal control theory. Because of the real-time computational constraints inherent in effective application, robotics also poses challenges to numerical optimization. The difficulty for roboticists lies in finding the right trade-off between **generality and specificity**. General-purpose algorithms suffer from the classical curse of dimensionality, which has become a bottleneck for robot control. As such, they are suitable for offline motion generation, but they are insufficient for real-time applications. Real-time robot control requires very fast computation; it demands dedicated numerical optimization methods. We have seen how bipedal walking exemplifies this tension between generality and specificity. Today, roboticists look to optimization theorists for more efficient algorithms, while simultaneously developing specialized proprietary techniques of their own.

Last but not least, let us close with the controversy raised by neurobiologist K. Friston. In a recent paper[\[45\]](#ref_45), he posed the challenging question: "What is optimal about motor control?" He argues against optimal control in favor of the competing concept of active inference. Although the paper focuses primarily on motor control in the life sciences, this question is of critical importance and great appeal to roboticists, and it calls for strengthened collaboration between the life sciences and engineering sciences.
