For search, the elements of the search space are typically required to be at most countably infinite, and we initially disregard uncertainty and dynamic environments.

Connecting to practical problems, both the workspace and the [configuration space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%8D%E5%BD%A2%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) are inherently continuous. To generate a search space, we encounter the problem of [discretization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A6%BB%E6%95%A3%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity). Possible methods include generating grid maps, quadtree/octree maps, and later, sampling-based approaches that construct [sampled maps](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%87%87%E6%A0%B7%E5%9C%B0%E5%9B%BE&zhida_source=entity). For beginners, the simplest and most intuitive four-connected or eight-connected grid maps are often used for instruction, as grid maps are widely applicable and allow for convenient [collision checking](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A2%B0%E6%92%9E%E6%A3%80%E6%B5%8B&zhida_source=entity) via coordinate indexing.

## 1. Overview of Graph Search Algorithms

After considering the [discrete state space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A6%BB%E6%95%A3%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) and the set of actions, we effectively obtain a weighted or unweighted directed state transition graph.

![](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-c56a91738eacb789b84e35432896c90b_r.jpg)

This section introduces **graph search algorithms**. Compared to the [value iteration algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%80%BC%E8%BF%AD%E4%BB%A3%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) to be discussed later, graph search algorithms maintain a storage structure called the **[open list](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BC%80%E6%94%BE%E5%88%97%E8%A1%A8&zhida_source=entity)**. A specific function is used to sort the open list, forming a [priority queue](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%98%E5%85%88%E7%BA%A7%E9%98%9F%E5%88%97&zhida_source=entity). To trace back the searched path, we also require a **[closed list](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%B3%E9%97%AD%E5%88%97%E8%A1%A8&zhida_source=entity)**. Nodes in the closed list are generally not re-added to the open list, which ensures our search algorithm is systematic.

Additionally, during the search process, a **search tree** is generated. Our algorithm naturally aims to [prune](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%89%AA%E6%9E%9D&zhida_source=entity) the search tree, reaching the goal from the start quickly (i.e., expanding few nodes) without sacrificing optimality. The closed list essentially corresponds to traversing the search tree in reverse.

![](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-2079c53bffbb38841f05e2373094d417_r.jpg)

Graph search algorithms can be decomposed into a three-step loop: after initialization, the open list undergoes repeated **pop, expand (visit), and push** operations, until no nodes remain in the open list or the goal is reached.

## 2. BFS, DFS, and Iterative Deepening

The priority queue is ordered using the data structures of queues and stacks, representing the most conservative and the most aggressive approaches, respectively. These graph searches cannot handle weighted graphs. Clearly, both methods only depend on the order in which nodes enter the priority queue for popping and do not utilize any information about the start or goal positions on the map; essentially, both lack goal-directedness.

### 1. Breadth-First Search (BFS)

The open list is a first-in-first-out queue. Since all k-step searches are completed before k+1-step searches begin, BFS guarantees optimality in terms of the number of steps (i.e., optimal for unweighted graphs where all weights are 1) and remains systematic without needing to check for revisits. The [algorithmic complexity](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95%E5%A4%8D%E6%9D%82%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity) is O(|V|+|E|), where |V| and |E| are the numbers of vertices and edges in the state transition graph, respectively. |V| relates to |X|, and |E| relates to |U|.

### 2. Depth-First Search (DFS)

The open list is a last-in-first-out stack. It prioritizes exploring one possible branch to its end before considering other branches. For any finite X, it is systematic; for infinite X, it is not. The algorithmic complexity is the same as BFS.

![](https://pica.zhimg.com/v2-3effeae512b971fa913c9d66f2cf9e5c_r.jpg)

### 3. Iterative Deepening

Iterative deepening is an effective strategy when the search tree has a branching structure. It adopts the overall DFS logic but iterates the search depth: first, depth-first search nodes within distance i from the start; if the goal is not found, search nodes within distance i+1, typically starting from i=1. It can be anticipated that for branching searches, the number of states at distance i+1 greatly exceeds that at distance i, so the cost of the previous step in iterative deepening is much smaller than the next; thus, it is reasonable to abandon the search at distance i.

This method makes DFS more systematic and reduces the number of searched states compared to BFS. For many problems, iterative deepening performs better than BFS in the worst case.

## 3. Dijkstra, Greedy Algorithm, A\*, and Their Improvements

Compared to the previous two methods, these three approaches leverage more information from the map itself, rather than merely relying on the data structures used to build the open list. Consequently, they can handle weighted graphs. Beyond weighted graphs being more realistic—since different paths have different costs—computing the cost of each state in the graph often involves the following operations: if a state is revisited, its cost needs updating; costs can be used to order the priority queue, or even optimized with HOT (heap on top); costs can also generate the sequence of planning actions. Even without creating a closed list, starting from any visited state, by traversing the state transition graph backward while reducing the cost as much as possible at each step, we can find the optimal path back to the initial state—provided the costs are always positive, and this cannot be applied to [negative-weight graphs](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B4%9F%E6%9D%83%E5%9B%BE&zhida_source=entity). For cases with negative-weight edges, especially negative-weight cycles, the [Bellman-Ford algorithm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Bellman-Ford%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) is needed.

The priority ordering function of the open list is f = a·g + b·h, with a, b ≥ 0, where g represents the optimal cost-to-come (already paid cost), and the heuristic function h represents an [estimate](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity) of the cost-to-go (remaining cost). The weight on g reflects the systematic tendency of the algorithm, while the weight on h reflects its "greediness," or the strength of its orientation toward the goal. The purpose of incorporating a heuristic is to reduce the number of nodes visited, thereby speeding up the search. One could say that all graph search algorithms involve a trade-off between systematicity and goal-directedness.

### 1. Dijkstra's Algorithm (1956)

a=1, b=0. This is actually a special form of [dynamic programming](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E6%80%81%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity) (which may be covered later). For graphs without negative-weight cycles, this algorithm finds the single-source optimal path. Its strengths lie in completeness and optimality; its drawback is that, since it only considers cost-to-come, it fundamentally searches isotropically without any goal-directedness, much like BFS. Its [time complexity](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E5%A4%8D%E6%9D%82%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity) is O((|V|+|E|)log|V|) when a [priority queue](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%98%E5%85%88%E9%98%9F%E5%88%97&zhida_source=entity) is used, or O(|V|log|V|+|E|) with a Fibonacci heap, where |V| and |E| are the numbers of vertices and edges in the state transition graph.

Moreover, as long as a node remains in the open list, its g value can be updated as the path search progresses, and the priority queue is reordered accordingly. However, it can be proven that once a node enters the closed list, its g value is guaranteed to be optimal.

### 2. Greedy Algorithm (Best-First Search)

a=0, b=1. This approach is not systematic, and the solution obtained is not guaranteed to be optimal. However, due to its strong goal-directedness, it generally finds a feasible solution by searching fewer points. Yet, because it is too "short-sighted," it can easily get stuck in local minima or even "dead ends"; after entering a dead end, it may perform many useless node expansions to escape. Its complexity is O(|V|+|E|)

### 3. A\* Algorithm[\[1\]](#ref_1) (1968)

With a=1, b=1, A\* essentially combines the strengths of Dijkstra's algorithm and the greedy algorithm. It exhibits more goal-directedness than Dijkstra's algorithm, but its optimality depends on the heuristic h estimated for all state nodes. If the estimated value h < the true optimal h\*, then A\* is optimal. In practice, the most critical aspect is the design of the heuristic function H. ### 4. Weighted A\* Algorithm

We can introduce a performance metric ${K=\frac{a/b}{1+(a/b)}}$ to measure the trade-off between systematicity, consistency, and greediness in the search. K=0 corresponds to the greedy algorithm, K=0.5 corresponds to A\*, and K=1 corresponds to Dijkstra's algorithm.

Sometimes we set K to be slightly less than 0.5. This sacrifices some systematicity for faster search speed, while tolerating falling into shallow local minima. This is the core idea of Weighted A\*, which uses f = g + εh, where ε > 1. Of course, in many cases, weighting makes h inadmissible, potentially leading to local minima. This implies that Weighted A\* does not guarantee the optimal solution, although its suboptimality is bounded by:

$$
{cost\leq \varepsilon \cdot cost^*}
$$

However, correspondingly, in many cases, its search speed is vastly improved, potentially being tens of times faster than A\*.

## IV. Implementation Details

### (1) Design of Heuristic Functions[\[1\]](#ref_1) [\[2\]](#ref_2)

The heuristic function h is available before the search begins and can be considered known information for each node. It possesses the following properties:

**Admissibility & Tightness**: For each state s, the heuristic value must be less than or equal to the true optimal cost from the current state to the goal, i.e., $h(s)\le{h^*(s)}$. The smaller the value, the more admissible it is considered; when equality holds, h is said to be **tight**.

**Consistency**: $h(s_{goal}) = 0$ and for any non-goal state s', it must satisfy the [triangle inequality](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%89%E8%A7%92%E4%B8%8D%E7%AD%89%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity), i.e., $h(s)\le{cost(s, s') + h(s')}$.

Consistency implies admissibility, while admissibility usually, but not always, implies consistency. An admissible heuristic function ensures the optimality of the A\* algorithm.

Now, let's consider realistic environments with obstacles. The Euclidean distance is an admissible heuristic because it ignores obstacles when computed. Furthermore, for a 4-connected grid map, the admissibility of the Euclidean distance is even higher. The Manhattan distance is admissible on a 4-connected map but may not be admissible on an 8-connected map. Similarly, the L∞ norm is certainly admissible and highly so. Setting h=0 degenerates the algorithm to Dijkstra's, which is also highly admissible.

Although high admissibility guarantees an optimal algorithm, the effectiveness of the heuristic diminishes as admissibility increases. The search process tends to rely more on consistency, expanding many useless nodes, wasting computational resources and degrading performance, especially as the dimensionality of the space increases, leading to exponentially more useless node expansions. This motivates the concept of "tightness". A tight heuristic is both admissible and expands the minimum number of nodes. This is because tightness implies that the value of `f` does not change along the optimal path. The `f` values of all nodes not on the optimal path will be higher than those on the optimal path. Since nodes with smaller `f` are always popped first, the search will never deviate from the [shortest path](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E7%9F%AD%E8%B7%AF%E5%BE%84&zhida_source=entity).

Consequently, for highly structured maps like grid maps combined with structured action sets `u`, the diagonal heuristic emerges. For a 2D 8-connected case, ignoring obstacles, assume the cost of a straight move is 1 and the cost of a diagonal move is ${\sqrt{2}}$.

$$d_x = |x - x_{goal}| \\ d_y = |y-y_{goal}| \\ h=(d_x+d_y)+(\sqrt2-2)min(d_x,d_y)$$

For the 4-connected case, the Manhattan distance is tight. For 3D cases, we can also formulate analogous diagonal heuristic [expressions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245536500&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%A1%A8%E8%BE%BE%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity).

### (2) Tie Breaker

During path search, one may find that paths of exactly the same length can have many different shapes, creating a tie among multiple paths. This tie causes nodes from different paths to have the same `f` value used for priority ordering; they will all be added to the open list in some predefined order, leading to numerous expansion operations and consuming computational resources.

The main idea of a Tie Breaker is to create a preference between paths with equal `f` values. Approaches include, but are not limited to, the following:

1. For states with equal `f`, slightly amplify the heuristic `h` for that node.  
   $h = h \times (1.0+p) \\ p < \frac{单步最小代价}{期望最大路经总代价}$  
   *(Translator's note: The formula text renders as: $p < \frac{\text{minimum single-step cost}}{\text{expected maximum total path cost}}$)*

   Since the map size is always bounded in practical planning, the denominator has an upper bound. While this amplification might seem to risk losing admissibility for heuristics with low initial admissibility, in practice, due to the presence of obstacles, the heuristic is almost never tight, so this is generally harmless.

   ![](/blog-assets/zhihu/708321348/image-06.jpg)

   Left: without tie breaker; Right: with tie breaker

2. For states with equal `f`, further compare their `h` values and prioritize popping the path with the smaller `h`.

3. Add a random number to the heuristic function or the edge cost function. However, this random number must be predetermined, not generated randomly during the run. Each coordinate should have a distinct value, forming a hash table based on coordinates.

4. If we prefer the searched path to closely follow the straight line segment from start to goal, we can add a penalty term to `h` for deviating from this line segment. The penalty is a small number multiplied by a determinant constructed from the start, goal, and current points.

$$
d_{x1} = |x - x_{goal}| ，d_{y1} = |y-y_{goal}| \\d_{x2} = |x _{start}- x_{goal}| ，d_{y2} = |y_{start}-y_{goal}| \\ h'=h+0.001(d_{x1} \cdot d_{y2}-d_{x2}\cdot d_{y1})
$$

Furthermore, ties occurring in the presence of obstacles can increase the burden on downstream trajectory optimization. As shown in the figure below, for paths of equal cost near the red line versus the dashed path, we clearly prefer the path near the red line because it is smoother overall and presents less stress to the backend trajectory generation and optimization. However, if we use the fourth method above, which prefers paths closer to the straight line between start and goal, the search result might be the dashed path, which is not what we want.

![](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-deed6df142fc9ea441583d0204a9325d_r.jpg)

### (3) Other Considerations

1. How to generate the action sequence for planning, or establish the mapping X→U, based on searching for a feasible/optimal solution?

   This question has already been answered by the algorithms discussed earlier. We can use a closed list – a storage space that saves the states we have searched. This allows us to automatically generate a plan after the search concludes.

2. How to determine if a state has been visited?

   If the state transition graph is tree-shaped, it implies no state will be repeated during the search. One could create a table and query it at fixed steps to make this determination. However, this means traversing the table for each check, so choosing an appropriate data structure is beneficial for efficient checks. Alternatively, one can allow repeated states, but this increases the computational load during the search.

## V. Jump Point Search (JPS, 2011)[\[3\]](#ref_3)

Jump Point Search is an improvement over the algorithms in Section III. The algorithms in Section III incur significant overhead maintaining a priority queue. JPS modifies its search mechanism to completely break ties between different paths and also reduces the cost of maintaining the priority queue. When expanding each step, it determines which neighbors to expand based on the direction from the parent node and the surrounding obstacle configuration. This method is efficient but is limited to regular, unweighted grid maps. For weighted graphs, A\* must be used.

What follows describes an optimized version of JPS. In this optimization, intermediate jump points are not stored; instead, nodes called relay nodes are saved according to specific rules.

### (1) Forced Neighbors and Expansion Mechanism

When expanding the neighbors of the current point x, they are categorized into three types, each handled differently, forming our neighbor expansion mechanism.

1.  **Internal Neighbors**: If searching from x to this neighbor yields a path from x's parent to that point that is shorter than going through x (or is of equal length, creating a tie), then this neighbor is an internal neighbor. Such nodes are not added to the priority queue of the open list.
2.  **Forced Neighbors**: A neighbor that would normally be an internal neighbor if obstacles were absent, but the obstacle blocks the "shorter path from x's parent" (or the equal-length path), making it preferential to go through x. These points are called forced neighbors. We force these nodes into the open list for consideration.
3.  **Natural Neighbors**: All other neighboring nodes excluding obstacles, internal neighbors, and forced neighbors. These are not added to the open list but are handled by the jumping mechanism in the next step.

Note that during initialization, we have no parent node and can expand in all directions. Also, notice that discarding internal neighbors effectively prunes the search space.

The red arrow points from the parent node to the current node x. Gray cells are internal neighbors; white cells are natural neighbors.

![](/blog-assets/zhihu/708321348/image-10.jpg)

Black cells are obstacles; the red cell is a forced neighbor.

### (2) Special Nodes, Relay Nodes, and the Jumping Mechanism

1.  Execute the expansion mechanism, jumping for natural neighbors in a "straight lines first, then diagonal lines" order.
2.  First, search in the straight-line direction along natural neighbors. If a point with forced neighbors (called a **special node**) is found along the search line, the jump stops. Add the special node's preceding parent nodes along the jump path (called **relay nodes**) and the forced neighbors to the open list. Place the previous relay node from the open list into the closed list.
3.  If the straight-line search hits an obstacle or the map boundary without finding a special node, switch to searching diagonally. Similarly, if a special node is found along the diagonal, the jump stops. Add its relay nodes and forced neighbors to the open list, and move the previous relay node to the closed list.
4.  Then, treat the forced neighbors in the open list as the current point x in the search mechanism and expand them.
5.  When a neighbor of the current point is the goal point, it is treated as a special node, and the search ends.

In summary, our open list will contain only one continuously updated relay node and several forced neighbor nodes awaiting expansion. Of course, we must remember that the queue for the open list still maintains a priority, and the choice of the priority function `f` will also affect the results of JPS.

JPS algorithm pseudocode

Its pseudocode is essentially identical to A\*, the only difference being the strategy for expanding nodes. Other tricks for A\* can be directly applied to JPS.

### (3) Other Considerations

1.  **3D Jump Point Search (2017)**[\[4\]](#ref_4)  
    The expansion and jumping mechanisms become more complex. Refer to the article in the reference for details.

2.  **Optimality Discussion**  
    For more complex environments, such as obstacle-dense, maze-like scenarios, JPS often significantly outperforms A\*, offering substantial performance improvements. This is because JPS can quickly find critical jump points in the environment, greatly reducing the size of the open list and avoiding the process of adding individual nodes to a large open list and maintaining it.

    However, for robots, there are situations where a very large global or local map is constructed, but the robot's field of view (FOV) is limited, allowing it to only observe nearby obstacles. In such cases, JPS can be much slower than A\*. This is because during horizontal and vertical jumping, JPS must scan a considerable portion of the map to determine if a relay point exists along that line. Since there is no obstacle information beyond the FOV, it may take a long time to hit the map boundary, whereas it could quickly determine a relay point if obstacles were plentiful. Although collision queries are computationally inexpensive on grid maps, their cumulative cost in such extreme environments becomes significant.

    In general, JPS dramatically reduces the number of nodes in the open list compared to A\*, but it dramatically increases the number of collision queries.
