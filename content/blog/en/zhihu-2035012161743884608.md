Exploring the distinction between an optimal robot motion and a robot motion resulting from the application of optimization techniques.

By [Jean-Paul Laumond](https://cacm.acm.org/author/jean-paul-laumond/), [Nicolas Mansard](https://cacm.acm.org/author/nicolas-mansard/), and [Jean-Bernard Lasserre](https://cacm.acm.org/author/jean-bernard-lasserre/)

Communications of the ACM, Posted Sep 1 2014

The first book devoted to robot motion was published in 1982 with the subtitle "Planning and Control"[\[2\]](#ref_2). The distinction between motion planning and motion control is largely rooted in historical origins. Sometimes motion planning refers to geometric path planning, sometimes to open-loop control; sometimes motion control refers to open-loop control, sometimes to closed-loop control and stability; sometimes planning is viewed as an offline process, while control is real-time. From a historical perspective, robot motion planning emerged from the desire to endow robots with autonomy in movement: the field was born in computer science and artificial intelligence[\[3\]](#ref_3). Motion planning concerns determining whether a path to a given goal exists and, if so, computing a feasible one. Robot motion control, by contrast, originated in manufacturing and the control of manipulator arms[\[4\]](#ref_4). It found rapid and effective applications in the automotive industry. Motion control aims to translate tasks defined in the robot's workspace into control functions defined in the space of the robot's actuators: a typical example is finding a way to make the end effector of a welding robot follow a predefined weld line.

## Key Insights

- More than 20 years of research on wheeled mobile robots tells us that computing an optimal robot motion is a challenge.
- Geometric control theory and numerical analysis highlight two complementary perspectives on optimal robot motion.
- Most of the time, robot algorithms that compute an optimal motion merely deliver an optimized result produced by an optimization method, but not an optimal one at all.
- When an optimal motion exists, numerical algorithms often fail to account for its combinatorial structure; moreover, optimization algorithms actually circumvent—rather than overcome—the existence problem of optimal motions.

What exactly is optimality in robot motion? This question spans many facets, each studied independently in fields such as control theory, computer science, numerical analysis, and differential geometry, and has given rise to a wide array of methods, such as the maximum principle, applications of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, quadratic programming, neural networks, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, and Bayesian inference. The ultimate goal of these methods is to compute a so-called *optimal* solution, whatever the problem may be. The purpose of this article is not to survey these approaches, which have developed independently of robotics, but rather to emphasize the distinction between an "optimal motion" and an "optimized motion." In most cases, robot algorithms intended to compute an optimal motion in fact deliver not an optimal but merely an optimized motion—that is, the output of some optimization method. Computing an optimal motion is often a highly challenging problem, as illustrated by more than two decades of research on wheeled mobile robots (which we discuss below).

Note that the notion of optimality in robot motion discussed here is far from covering all dimensions of robot motion[\[5\]](#ref_5). It does not address low-level dynamic control, nor sensorimotor control, nor higher-level cognitive approaches to motion generation (e.g., those developed in the context of robot soccer or task planning).

## 1. Optimal Planning

### What Is an Optimal Solution in Robot Motion Planning and Control?

Motion planning explores the computational foundations of robot motion. It confronts the question: does an acceptable motion exist for a robot moving in an environment cluttered with obstacles? And how can a continuous problem be transformed into a combinatorial one?

The theory of this research topic[\[6\]](#ref_6) [\[7\]](#ref_7) has developed through three major stages. In the early 1980s, Lozano-Perez first transformed the problem of moving objects in physical space into the problem of moving a point in the so-called configuration space[\[8\]](#ref_8). This led to a well-defined mathematical problem: planning robot motion is equivalent to finding connected components in configuration space. Schwartz and Sharir then proved that the problem is decidable as long as these connected components can be shown to be semi-algebraic sets[\[9\]](#ref_9). Even though a series of papers in computational geometry addressed various instances of the problem, the general "piano mover's problem" remained intractable[\[10\]](#ref_10). Finally, Barraquand and Latombe relaxed the requirement of completeness to achieve probabilistic completeness, thereby introducing a new algorithmic paradigm[\[11\]](#ref_11). In the early 1990s, this gave rise to the popular probabilistic roadmap (PRM)[\[12\]](#ref_12) and rapidly exploring random tree (RRT)[\[13\]](#ref_13) algorithms.

Motion planning addresses a point-to-point problem in configuration space. Although this problem is itself computationally demanding and well understood, optimal motion planning is considerably more challenging. Beyond finding a solution to the planning problem—i.e., a path satisfying constraints such as collision avoidance and kinematics—optimal motion planning also seeks a solution that optimizes some criterion. Such criteria may include path length, time, or energy (which are equivalent under certain assumptions), or more complex ones such as the number of maneuvers required for parking.

In this context, many questions relate to optimization:

- For a given system, which motions optimize a given criterion? Does such a motion exist? The existence of an optimal motion may depend on the presence of obstacles and on the criterion to be optimized.
- When optimal motions exist, are they computable? If so, what is their computational complexity? How can exactness constraints be relaxed to compute approximate solutions? We will examine the combinatorial structure of the configuration space induced by obstacles and the metric to be optimized. We will also discuss time criteria and practical methods for optimizing time along a predefined path.
- Beyond finding a feasible solution to a given problem, motion planning also aims to optimize the solution once found. This issue is particularly critical for the motion schemes provided by probabilistic algorithms that introduce random detours. The challenge here is that optimization is no longer confined to the system's configuration space, but extends to the space of motions.

Throughout this article, the fundamental assumption of optimal motion planning is that the robot's entire environment is known and the optimization criterion is given; the goal is to find a globally optimal solution without considering practical issues such as model uncertainty or local sensory feedback.

### Existence of Optimal Motion

Before attempting to compute optimal motion, the first question to ask is whether it exists. To intuitively grasp the importance of this question, consider a mobile robot moving among obstacles. For certain reasonable safety reasons, the robot must not touch the obstacles. In mathematical terms, the robot must move within an open region of configuration space. However, the optimal motion from one position to another located behind an obstacle would necessarily touch that obstacle. Hence, such optimal motion is inadmissible. It manifests as an unattainable ideal motion. The best we can achieve is to find a collision-free motion whose length approaches that of the ideal shortest (but unacceptable) motion. In other words, the corresponding motion planning problem admits no optimal solution. The issue here is fundamentally [topological](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8B%93%E6%89%91%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity): combinatorial [data structures](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%95%B0%E6%8D%AE%E7%BB%93%E6%9E%84&zhida_source=entity) (e.g., visibility graphs) may help us compute solutions that are optimal in the closure of free space but are not solutions at all in the open free space.

Even without obstacles, the existence of optimal motion is far from guaranteed. In deterministic continuous-time optimal control problems, we typically seek a time-varying [control function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) that optimizes some integral functional over a time interval. Addressing the existence question requires us to draw upon geometric control theory [\[14\]](#ref_14); for example, [Filippov's theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Fillipov%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity) proves the existence of time-optimal trajectories. The Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP) and the Boltyanskii conditions provide necessary and sufficient conditions for trajectory optimality, respectively. However, it is often difficult to extract useful information from these tools. The PMP may help characterize optimal trajectories locally, but it typically reveals little about their global structure. Later, we will show how subtle this problem can be in various examples involving wheeled mobile robots.

The class of optimal control problems for which the existence of optimal solutions can be guaranteed is limited. The minimum-time problem for controllable linear systems (with bounded controls) belongs to this class: optimal solutions exist, and the optimal controls are of bang-bang type. However, the so-called Fuller problem may arise: since the optimal solution is bang-bang with infinitely many switchings, it is utterly impractical. Other examples include the celebrated linear-quadratic-Gaussian problem (where the [cost function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%88%90%E6%9C%AC%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) is quadratic and the dynamics are linear in both the control and state variables), as well as systems with bounded inputs whose dynamics are [affine](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%BF%E5%B0%84&zhida_source=entity) in the control variable. In the former case, a closed-loop optimal solution can be computed by solving the [algebraic Riccati equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0Riccati%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity), while in the latter case, under some suitable assumptions, the existence of optimal [control trajectories](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E8%BD%A8%E8%BF%B9&zhida_source=entity) can be guaranteed.

> *Even without obstacles, the existence of optimal motion is far from guaranteed.*

In more general cases, we can only hope to approximate the [optimal value](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity) as closely as possible through a sequence of control trajectories. Indeed, when the considered [function space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) of controls is too narrow, no optimal solution exists. This was recognized as early as the 1960s. Once we enlarge the space of admissible control functions, a precise meaning can be assigned to the limit of such a sequence of control trajectories. For example, in problems where the controls are affine and the integral functional is an L1 norm, the optimal control is a finite [sequence of impulses](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%84%89%E5%86%B2%E5%BA%8F%E5%88%97&zhida_source=entity) rather than a function of time (see, e.g., Neustadt [\[15\]](#ref_15)). In certain problems, such as satellite control, this solution is reasonable because it can be approximated by gas thruster firings. However, owing to the physical limitations of actuators, such a solution is generally not implementable.

Modifying the mathematical formulation of the problem (e.g., considering a larger space of candidate controls) may help in finding an optimal solution. In the satellite control example, the initial formulation is reasonable because the "ideal" impulsive solution can indeed be approximated with gas jets. In other cases, however, the initial problem formulation may be incorrect because the ideal impulsive solution is unrealizable. In fact, if we "sense" that a smooth optimal solution should exist within the initially considered function space, yet none exists, then either the dynamics and/or constraints do not adequately reflect the physical limitations of the system, or the cost functional is inappropriate to guarantee the existence of an optimal solution in that function space. To the best of our knowledge, this issue is rarely discussed in optimal control textbooks or courses.

### Optimal Path Planning

Given that motion is a [continuous function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%9E%E7%BB%AD%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) of time in configuration space (or workspace), the image of a motion is a path in that space. The "piano mover's problem" refers to the path planning problem, which is the geometric instance of robot motion planning.

![Handwritten note](/blog-assets/zhihu/2035012161743884608/image-02.jpg)

Figure 1 A modern take on the "piano mover's problem": two characters must move a shared piano while avoiding surrounding obstacles.

Obstacle avoidance constraints are considered here. In this setting, optimality refers to the length of the path, without regard to time or control. The crux of the problem lies in finding the [shortest path](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E7%9F%AD%E8%B7%AF%E5%BE%84&zhida_source=entity) between two points.

Depending on the metric used in configuration space, the shortest path may be unique (e.g., for the Euclidean metric) or non-unique (e.g., for the Manhattan metric). From a topological standpoint, all configuration-space metrics are equivalent (that is, if a sequence of Euclidean paths connecting two points exists, then a sequence of Manhattan paths connecting them also exists). However, different metrics induce different combinatorial properties in configuration space. For instance, for the same obstacle arrangement, two points may be connected by a Manhattan collision-free path but not by a collision-free straight segment: under the Manhattan metric, the two points are mutually visible, whereas under the Euclidean metric they are not. Consequently, depending on the given metric, there may or may not exist a finite number of points that "watch" the entire space [\[16\]](#ref_16). These combinatorial issues are particularly important for designing sampling-based motion planning algorithms.

Now consider a common configuration space equipped with the Euclidean metric. Exploring the visibility graph data structure readily solves the problem of finding shortest path length bounds among [polygonal](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%9A%E8%BE%B9%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity) obstacles. This is all well and good, but the situation changes if we consider three-dimensional spaces populated by [polyhedral](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%9A%E9%9D%A2%E4%BD%93&zhida_source=entity) obstacles. Indeed, in this case, finding the shortest path becomes an NP-hard problem [\[17\]](#ref_17). Thus, in general, even if we solve the problem of computing optimal paths in the absence of obstacles and allow the piano robot to touch obstacles, there is no hope of obtaining an algorithm that can compute optimal paths in the presence of obstacles.

Owing to the unsatisfactory results above, optimal path planning is typically addressed using numerical methods. Among the most common are discrete [search algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%90%9C%E7%B4%A2%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) based on bitmap representations of the workspace or configuration space [\[11\]](#ref_11). We can only obtain approximately optimal paths, that is, paths that are "not far from" the presumed (or ideal) estimated optimal path. Another approach models obstacles with repulsive potentials. The goal is then represented by an attractive potential, and the system tends to reach the goal via gradient descent [\[18\]](#ref_18). The solution is only locally optimal. Moreover, the method may become trapped in [local minima](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B1%80%E9%83%A8%E6%9C%80%E5%B0%8F%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity) and fail to find a final solution, or it may be impossible to determine whether a solution exists. It is therefore not complete. Several extensions can be considered. For example, one may explore harmonic potential fields [\[19\]](#ref_19). Alternatively, one might design clever navigation functions (artificial potential fields) [\[20\]](#ref_20). These methods can provide globally optimal solutions; however, they require an explicit representation of obstacles in configuration space, information that is often unavailable. It is thus evident that the presence of obstacles renders optimal path planning a difficult problem.

### Optimal Motion Planning

In addition to obstacle avoidance, constraints arising from robot control or dynamics add further difficulty. The goal here is to compute a time-optimal motion from an initial state (configuration and velocity) to a target state while avoiding obstacles and satisfying velocity and acceleration constraints. This constitutes the so-called kinodynamic motion planning problem [\[21\]](#ref_21). The pioneering algorithm is based on the [discretization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A6%BB%E6%95%A3%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) of both [state space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) and workspace.

It has spawned numerous variants, including non-uniform discretization, randomization techniques, and extensions of the A\* algorithm (see LaValle [\[7\]](#ref_7)). To this day, they remain the best algorithms for computing approximately optimal motion.

In the field of robot motion planning, the application of numerical methods to optimal robot control is not widespread [\[22\]](#ref_22). Numerical methods for solving optimal control problems fall into three main categories. [Dynamic programming](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E6%80%81%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity) implements Bellman's principle of optimality, which states that any sub-motion of an optimal motion is itself optimal. This leads to a [partial differential equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%81%8F%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) (the so-called HJB equation in continuous time), whose solution can sometimes be computed numerically. However, dynamic programming suffers from the well-known curse of dimensionality. Direct methods constitute the second category. They discretize the control and state trajectories in time, thereby transforming the original optimal control problem into a standard static [nonlinear programming](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity) (optimization) problem, which may be large-scale and amenable to a variety of solution techniques. However, typically only locally optimal solutions can be obtained. Moreover, when no optimal solution exists in the initial function space, potential [chattering effects](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8A%96%E6%8C%AF%E6%95%88%E5%BA%94&zhida_source=entity) may be concealed within the ultimately obtained optimal solution. Finally, the third category comprises indirect methods based on the optimality conditions provided by the maximum principle, ultimately yielding a two-point [boundary value problem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BE%B9%E5%80%BC%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity) (e.g., solved via [shooting methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%89%93%E9%9D%B6%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity)) that can be extremely difficult. Furthermore, the presence of singular arcs requires special treatment. Thus, although the problem to be solved may be very large in scale, direct methods are generally simpler than indirect ones. In fact, their inherent structural sparsity can be exploited effectively.

At this stage, we may conclude that exact solutions to optimal motion planning remain out of reach, and only numerical approximations can be sought.

## II. Optimized Planning

### Time-Optimal Motion Planning Along a Prescribed Path

To circumvent (rather than overcome) the inherent complexities of kinematic and numerical methods, a pragmatic approach is to introduce a decoupled method that solves the problem in two stages: first, perform (optimal) path planning to generate a collision-free path; then, compute the time-optimal trajectory along that path while accounting for [robot dynamics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%BA%E5%99%A8%E4%BA%BA%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) and control constraints. Of course, the resulting trajectory is not globally time-optimal; it is merely the best trajectory for the predefined path. From a computational standpoint, this problem is far simpler than the original global problem, because the search space (known as the [phase plane](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%B8%E5%B9%B3%E9%9D%A2&zhida_source=entity)) is reduced to two dimensions: the curvilinear abscissa along the path and its [time derivative](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E5%AF%BC%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity). Since the introduction of the dynamic programming approach by Shin and McKay in configuration space [\[23\]](#ref_23), and concurrently by Bobrow et al. in Cartesian space [\[24\]](#ref_24), numerous related methods have been developed. Many variants have been considered, including the refinement by Pfeiffer and Johanni [\[24\]](#ref_24) that combines forward and backward integration, as well as more recent work by Verscheure et al. [\[25\]](#ref_25) that casts the problem as a convex optimization problem.

### Optimization in Motion Space

Once the supporting path is allowed to deform, the path-tracking approach can be extended. Here, we assume that some motion planner provides an initial path (or trajectory). Depending on the planner, this path may be far from optimal. Probabilistic motion planners, for instance, introduce many useless detours. This is the price of their efficiency. The initial path must therefore be reshaped, i.e., optimized according to some criterion. Geometric paths need to be shortened according to a given metric. The simplest technique is to pick pairs of points along the path and connect them by shortest paths: if the shortest path is collision-free, it replaces the corresponding portion of the initial path. Through iterative operations, the path becomes progressively shorter. The process stops when it no longer significantly improves path quality. This technique works well in practice.

Beyond this simple trick, several variational methods operating in trajectory space have been proposed. One of the earliest is the method of Barraquand and Ferbach [\[26\]](#ref_26), which proposes replacing a highly constrained problem by a sequence of converging, less constrained subproblems that impose increasing penalties on motions violating the constraints. Each subproblem is then solved using a standard motion planner. This principle has recently been successfully extended to humanoid robot motion planning [\[27\]](#ref_27).

Another approach, due to Quinlan and Khatib, models the motion as a mass-spring system [\[28\]](#ref_28) (the elastic band method). The motion behaves as an elastic band whose shape evolves according to the application of an energy-function optimizer. Provided the nonholonomic metric is known, the method applies to [nonholonomic systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E5%AE%8C%E6%95%B4%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity)[\[29\]](#ref_29), as well as to real-time obstacle avoidance in [dynamic environments](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E6%80%81%E7%8E%AF%E5%A2%83&zhida_source=entity)[\[30\]](#ref_30).

More recently, following this same principle of optimizing an initial guess in motion space, several successful refinements have been proposed. Zucker et al. apply [gradient descent](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A2%AF%E5%BA%A6%E4%B8%8B%E9%99%8D%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) in trajectory space using a simple function that simultaneously encodes smoothness and obstacle avoidance [\[31\]](#ref_31) (CHOMP). The key insight of this method is to model the trajectory as a geometric object, making it invariant to [parameterization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%82%E6%95%B0%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity). Within the same framework, Kalakrishnan et al. propose replacing gradient descent with a derivative-free stochastic [optimization technique](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E6%8A%80%E6%9C%AF&zhida_source=entity) (STOMP), allowing non-smooth cost functions to be considered [\[32\]](#ref_32).

### What We Know and Don't Know About Optimal Motion for Wheeled Mobile Robots

Mobile robots constitute a unique class of systems for which the optimal motion problem is better understood. Since Dubins' seminal work in the 1950s [\[33\]](#ref_33), optimal motion planning and control for mobile robots has attracted considerable attention. This section briefly reviews how some challenging optimal control problems have been solved, and what remains open.

We consider four control models of mobile robots based on the car model ([Figure 2](https://cacm.acm.org/research/optimality-in-robot-motion/#F2)).

Two of them are simplified car models: the so-called Dubins car (Figure 3) and the Reeds-Shepp car (Figure 4).

The Dubins car can only move forward, while the Reeds-Shepp car can move both forward and backward. Both travel at constant speed with [absolute value](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BB%9D%E5%AF%B9%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity) 1. These models account for a lower bound on the turning radius, a typical constraint for cars. Such a constraint does not exist for two-wheel differential-drive mobile robots. Such robots can turn in place, whereas cars cannot. We consider two simple control schemes for two-wheel drive mobile robots [\[34\]](#ref_34): in the first system (Hilare-1), the wheel linear velocities are controlled; in the second (Hilare-2), the accelerations are controlled (i.e., the second system is a dynamic extension of the first).

**Time-optimal trajectories.** The car-like robots in Figures 3 and 4 represent two examples of [nonlinear systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) for which we know the exact structure of optimal trajectories. Note that in both examples, the norm of the linear velocity is assumed constant. In these cases, time-optimal trajectories are supported by the corresponding shortest paths. Dubins solved the problem for a car moving only forward [\[33\]](#ref_33). More than thirty years later, Reeds-Shepp [\[35\]](#ref_35) solved the problem for a car moving both forward and backward. The problem has been thoroughly revisited using modern geometric techniques from [optimal control theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity)[\[36\]](#ref_36) [\[37\]](#ref_37): the application of PMP shows that optimal trajectories consist of arcs of minimum turning radius (bang-bang control) and straight line segments (singular trajectories). The complete trajectory structure can be derived from geometric arguments characterizing the switching functions. Dubins and Reeds-Shepp cars are among the few nonlinear systems for which optimal control has been fully understood.

Figure 6 Hilare-1: the control is the wheel velocity; the optimal control is bang-bang. Optimal trajectories consist of pure rotations and straight line segments.

![](/blog-assets/zhihu/2035012161743884608/image-08.jpg)

Figure 7 Hilare-2: the control is the wheel acceleration; the optimal control is bang-bang; optimal trajectories consist of Euler spirals and involutes of circles.

The same approach applies to velocity-controlled differential-drive vehicles (Hilare-1 in Figure 6). In this case, optimal trajectories are bang-bang, consisting of pure rotations and straight line segments. The switching functions have also been fully characterized [\[38\]](#ref_38). However, this is not the case for the dynamic extension of the system, namely the acceleration-controlled differential-drive vehicle (Hilare-2, as shown in Figure 7). Only partial results are known: optimal trajectories are bang-bang (i.e., no singular trajectories exist) and consist of arcs of Euler spirals and involutes of circles [\[39\]](#ref_39). However, the switching functions remain unknown. The optimal control synthesis for the Hilare-2 system remains an open problem.

Although the four systems presented here demonstrate the existence of optimal trajectories, one more result is worth mentioning. If we consider the optimal control problem for the Reeds-Shepp car in the presence of obstacles, it has been shown that shortest paths may fail to exist, even when contact with obstacles is allowed [\[40\]](#ref_40).

**Motion planning.** These results are extremely useful for motion planning in the presence of obstacles. Figures 3 and 4 illustrate the reachable sets for the Dubins and Reeds-Shepp cars, respectively. The reachable set of the Reeds-Shepp car is a neighborhood of the origin, whereas that of the Dubins car is not. In other words, the Reeds-Shepp car is small-time controllable, while the Dubins car is only controllable in finite time. This has significant implications for motion planning. For the Reeds-Shepp car, any collision-free (not necessarily feasible) path can be approximated by a sequence of collision-free feasible paths. Optimal paths can be used to construct approximations, yielding efficient motion planning algorithms [\[41\]](#ref_41). Not only does such an algorithm fail to apply to the Dubins car, but we still do not know whether the motion planning problem for the Dubins car is decidable.

In Laumond et al. [\[41\]](#ref_41), we proved that the number of maneuvers required for parking is inversely proportional to the square of the clearance. This result follows directly from the shape of the reachable set. Thus, the combinatorial complexity of the (nonholonomic) motion planning problem is closely related to the shape of the reachable set in optimal control and in the underlying (sub-Riemannian) geometry [\[42\]](#ref_42).

## III. Conclusion

When optimal solutions are unavailable—whether for theoretical reasons (e.g., no optimal solution exists) or practical ones (e.g., intractability)—we have seen that the problem can be reformulated by considering discrete representations of space and/or time, slightly altering the optimality criterion, or resorting to [numerical optimization algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%95%B0%E5%80%BC%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity). In all these cases, the resulting solution is only an approximation of the original problem.

In summary, the existence of optimal robot motion seems rarely guaranteed. Even when it does exist, finding the optimal solution has never been shown to be a decidable problem in the way that the motion planning problem is. Therefore, "optimal motion" should generally be understood as "optimized motion," i.e., the output of a numerical optimization algorithm. Nevertheless, motion optimization techniques, closely following advances in numerical optimization, have achieved effective practical results on real robotic platforms, even in the absence of new theoretical contributions.

This discussion of the distinction between optimal and optimized motion does not cover all aspects of optimality in robot motion. In a companion article [\[43\]](#ref_43), we treat the motion optimality problem as a principle of action selection and discuss its connections with machine learning and recent inverse [optimal control methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=274264302&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity).
