Let me start with the conclusion: this question is essentially meaningless, because it lacks a clear sense of purpose.

First, based on the description of Tongji's 7th edition in the question, the asker is almost certainly not a mathematics major. This immediately means you most likely have no need to care about the mathematical analysis textbooks used by math departments.

Second, the question seeks "comprehensiveness." But without a purpose to anchor it, the word "comprehensive" is vague.

That said, it comes down to the following three cases.

If your goal is simply to score well on midterm and final exams at most schools, then I think Tongji's 7th edition is already more than sufficient for you.

If you find certain parts of Tongji's 7th edition hard to digest—feeling like you're swallowing dates whole without really understanding them—and you're looking for a better grasp of some concepts, then I'd recommend a few approaches:

1. Find a mathematical analysis book that suits you as a reference (note: purely as a reference). There are already many answers on Zhihu about how to choose a mathematical analysis book. I'll also offer my own thoughts later (to be added when I have time).

2. Ask more questions—whether to your teacher (if they aren't so exam-oriented that they only care about test scores, they should be quite willing to help), to knowledgeable peers around you, or by searching Zhihu for questions like "how to understand..." (in my opinion, Zhihu has fairly thorough discussions on popular subjects like advanced mathematics).

3. Since you're not a math major, mathematics will at most be a hobby rather than your main focus. Most of the conceptual difficulties you encounter will only require a slightly deeper understanding. After steps 1 and 2, you can take notes as you see fit. These notes don't need to be systematic—since your questions are scattered, you can organize them as mini topical notes.

Note: These methods should ideally be combined.

If you're deeply passionate about mathematics and feel a strong interest in it, but have no plans to study it professionally, and you find Tongji's 7th edition unsatisfying, then I'd suggest directly picking up a mathematics department's analysis textbook and reading it. As for whether mathematical analysis is more comprehensive than advanced mathematics, there are relevant questions on Zhihu, so I won't elaborate here. You could also pair it with video lectures (after all, self-study and having a teacher are quite different things).

On a lighter note, mathematics has no notion of "completeness"—endlessly chasing "comprehensiveness" is a path with no end. And if you truly love it, why not consider switching to a math major? (doge)

Now let me share some books I'd recommend as so-called comprehensive mathematical analysis texts.

1. Fichtenholz's *[Course in Differential and Integral Calculus](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=479635722&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=%E5%BE%AE%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86%E5%AD%A6%E6%95%99%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity)*—a classic. It can be regarded as the culmination of classical calculus textbooks that do not introduce modern perspectives, terminology, or content such as point-set topology or calculus on manifolds. It's truly vast and comprehensive, and in a sense satisfies the asker's requirements.

2. Amann's *Analysis* trilogy—a heavyweight in analysis, covering essentially the full undergraduate analysis curriculum in a mathematics department, with a very elevated viewpoint. Naturally, it is also quite comprehensive on the topic of mathematical analysis itself.
