Although the video quality was poor, one could truly feel that the professor laid out the broad framework and major results of control theory. However, control researchers go further and focus on detailed and rich stability theory and design methodology.

A major issue in control theory is that many people only engage in "Ptolemaic" innovations [\[1\]](#ref_1) (which is, of course, a common trait of the impetuous atmosphere under the current AI wave) without thinking about the ideas and methods that truly solve problems. Therefore, the core of this article is to get straight to the essence; one then discovers that among complex nonlinear systems, effective ideas and techniques are actually quite few.

### (2) Controllability and Observability

For a [system dynamics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) and its observation equation, there are two crucial mappings: one is the controllability operator from u to x, and the other is the observability operator from x to y. **Controllability means the former is [surjective](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%BB%A1%E5%B0%84&zhida_source=entity)**; changing u can make x take all values in R^n. **Observability means the latter is injective**, so that any y uniquely determines an x. For details, see my article on linear-system controllability/observability criteria; the core is to transform the injectivity and surjectivity of the operators into a [prior estimate](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%88%E9%AA%8C%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1&zhida_source=entity) of some operator:

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/671215828](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/671215828)

Take the linear case as an example. The controllability Gramian writes out the above mapping, finds it to be a [matrix exponential](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9F%A9%E9%98%B5%E6%8C%87%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity), and then requires it to have full row rank. If the system is not controllable, the matrix obtained from the above mapping [naturally induces](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%87%AA%E7%84%B6%E8%AF%B1%E5%AF%BC&zhida_source=entity) a decomposition into a controllable subspace and an uncontrollable subspace. The same idea can be extended to nonlinear systems.

**2. Observability and Detectability**[\[2\]](#ref_2)

The key difference is that in linear-system observability, the output caused by the input u can be computed, so when analyzing observability in linear systems, one may assume u ≡ 0 (this is essentially the separation principle). Nonlinear observability is different; its definitions vary widely. Nonlinear systems can be classified into uniformly observable and non-uniformly observable systems depending on whether observability depends on the input; the former includes the uniformly differential observability used in high-gain observers. ### (3) Feedback Linearization Theory

**1. Isidori Canonical Form and Relative Degree**

A nonlinear system's [state equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) and output equation are as follows:

$$
\dot{x}=f(x,u,t)\\ y=h(x,u,t)
$$

where the state variable $x\in \mathbb R^{n}$, the control input $u\in \mathbb R^{m}$, and the output (measurement) $y\in \mathbb R^{m}$.

From the feedback linearization theory of single-input single-output (SISO) nonlinear systems, by repeatedly taking Lie derivatives of the output and applying an appropriate [diffeomorphism](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E5%90%8C%E8%83%9A&zhida_source=entity), one can ultimately transform a nonlinear system with [relative degree](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E7%9B%B8%E5%AF%B9%E9%98%B6&zhida_source=entity) r into a Byrnes-Isidori canonical form, consisting of an r-th-order [subsystem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AD%90%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) and an (n−r)-th-order subsystem. The former can explicitly design the control input u using the excellent properties after decoupling, thereby reducing it to an r-th-order integrator chain, which can then be transformed into a controllable canonical form. The latter has no general [design method](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%AE%BE%E8%AE%A1%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) and is the uncontrollable part, called the internal dynamics (zero dynamics) of the nonlinear system; it is an inherent property of the system.

If the internal dynamics are unstable, the system is called a non-minimum-phase system; otherwise, it is called a **minimum-phase system**. If r = n, the system can be fully input-output linearized and completely decoupled into an [integrator chain](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86%E9%93%BE&zhida_source=entity), with all states being controllable and stabilizable. Real systems may not have an output that satisfies r = n; in that case, one can find a virtual output $y_f$ such that the relative degree r = n is achieved through the virtual output.

For multi-input multi-output (MIMO) nonlinear systems, although they cannot be written in Byrnes-Isidori canonical form, the same idea of seeking a virtual output with relative degree r = n can also yield similar results.

Furthermore, if the virtual output and its derivatives

$$
y_f,\dot y_f,\cdots,y_f^{(n)}
$$

are linearly independent, then the definition of differential flatness emerges.

**2. Differential Flatness**[\[7\]](#ref_7)

For the above general nonlinear system, if there exists a set of flat output variables $y_f$ determined by x and finite derivatives of u, such that the state x and the input variables u can all be algebraically represented by this set of outputs and their finite derivatives—i.e., a special form of feedback linearization with relative degree n—then the system is called **differentially flat**.

For a MIMO nonlinear system, define $\mathbf{y}_{f} = (y_{f1}, \cdots, y_{fp})^T$, $\mathbf{y}_{p}^{[k_i]} = (y_{p1}, y_{p2}, \cdots, y_{p}^{(k_i)})^T$, where the relative degrees corresponding to the p integrator chains are $k_i, i = 1, 2, \cdots, p$, satisfying $\sum\limits_{i=1}^p k_i \geq n$. The above definition is equivalent to the fact that the original system can be fully input-output feedback linearized into an integrator chain system, which can be transformed into Brunovsky canonical form, and can be expressed with differential [parameterization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%82%E6%95%B0%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) as:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \psi_x(\mathbf{y}_{f1}^{[k_1-1]}, \mathbf{y}_{f2}^{[k_2-1]}, \cdots, \mathbf{y}_{fp}^{[k_p-1]})
$$

$$
\mathbf{u} = \psi_u(\mathbf{y}_{f1}^{[k_1]}, \mathbf{y}_{f2}^{[k_2]}, \cdots, \mathbf{y}_{fp}^{[k_p]})
$$

$$
\mathbf{y} = \psi_y(\mathbf{y}_{f1}^{[k_1]}, \mathbf{y}_{f2}^{[k_2]}, \cdots, \mathbf{y}_{fp}^{[k_p]})
$$

That is, there is no [homogeneous ordinary differential equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%BD%90%E6%AC%A1%E5%B8%B8%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) satisfying $\phi(\mathbf{y}_{f1}^{[k_1]}, \mathbf{y}_{f2}^{[k_2]}, \cdots, \mathbf{y}_{fp}^{[k_p]}) = 0$. After feedback linearization, the $i$-th integrator chain can be expressed as:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{fi}^{[k_i-1]} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{I}_{k_i-1} \\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0}^T \end{pmatrix} \mathbf{y}_{fi}^{[k_i-1]} + \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} v_i
$$

where $v_i$ is the i-th component of the new external input introduced after feedback linearization.

When [Cheng Daizhan](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A8%8B%E4%BB%A3%E5%B1%95&zhida_source=entity) was pursuing his PhD under Isidori, he derived the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of an external controllable linear equivalence [\[8\]](#ref_8). Ten years later, in 1995, Michel Fliess used algebraic theory to define an equivalent concept: differential flatness [\[9\]](#ref_9) (although he did not cite Professor Cheng's work), clarifying that this is a property of nonlinear systems that allows endogenous dynamical linearization, thereby enabling the system to be transformed into a controllable canonical form via dynamic feedback. In other words, for linear systems, differential flatness is equivalent to controllability, while for nonlinear systems, differential flatness is a sufficient condition for controllability. Fliess later wrote a paper on flatness using geometric methods (Lie–Bäcklund transformations) in IEEE TAC.

Martin, Murray, and Rouchon have a paper titled *Flat Systems* that is accessible for beginners. One can also read Jean Levin's [\[10\]](#ref_10) *Nonlinear System Analysis*, which is based on flatness and is a good book for understanding the topic, though it requires considerable time to study.

A highly mathematical journal paper [\[11\]](#ref_11] presents one of its main perspectives as "flatness is equivalent to the strong closedness of the ideal of 1-forms representing the differentials of all possible trivializations, using an approach which is invariant by endogeneous dynamic feedback." The conclusion is interesting, but the proof is very involved; the end of the paper does include a few relatively direct examples. However, the results do not actually solve the decision problem.

To date, there is no general method for deciding the differential flatness of nonlinear systems; the literature on necessary and sufficient conditions is highly mathematical, with no practical, constructive results. Moreover, when the model contains uncertainty, very few systems are truly flat; the best one can often do is to find a flat surrogate/simplified model and then handle it with robust methods [\[12\]](#ref_12). Given known information, one can find suitable outputs to maximize control capability.

**3. Applications of Differential Flatness**

There are numerous papers on applications of flatness; a typical example is the quadrotor.

- The state x may lie on a nonlinear manifold; after differential parameterization, there is a diffeomorphism corresponding to the flat output space, and this linearization is global. Moreover, the integrator chains obtained after linearization are fully controllable linear systems, for which the theory is very mature and a large body of results can be directly applied.
- When designing the controller, the key parameters are all taken from the reference trajectory, ensuring that the error system has the concise form of trajectory-linearization rather than the complex nonlinear form based on actual states.
- Differential parameterization allows one to obtain the [inverse system](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%80%86%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) by directly computing [algebraic equations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) rather than analyzing the system's nonlinear dynamics, making it very convenient to plan or control directly in the flat space and then use the parameterization to analytically convert the trajectory of $y_f$ into x and u. Typically, the dimension of the flat output space is smaller than the dimension n of the original [state space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity), greatly reducing the dimensionality of the problem.
- Motion planning algorithms for the corresponding nonlinear system can be designed directly for the integrator chain system after the diffeomorphic transformation. For the two-point boundary value optimal control problem (OBVP) of the integrator chain system, if the cost functional uses a single squared time integral, one can prove that the solution trajectory can be obtained as a polynomial via [algebraic operations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0%E8%BF%90%E7%AE%97&zhida_source=entity). This is why quadrotors often use polynomial [trajectory parameterization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BD%A8%E8%BF%B9%E5%8F%82%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity).
- However, when there are complex constraints on the state x and control input u, planning in the flat output space can become more difficult due to the nonlinearity of the flat mapping. Yet it is foreseeable that if the flat output space used for planning is consistent with the state space containing the constraints, this impact will be much smaller. It can be shown that for multirotors, the flat output space and the state space are consistent.

### (4) High Gain

### (5) I&I and Sliding Mode Control[\[13\]](#ref_13) [\[14\]](#ref_14)

[Fundamentalist](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8E%9F%E6%95%99%E6%97%A8%E4%B8%BB%E4%B9%89&zhida_source=entity) sliding mode control is a rather naive and crude idea, suitable only as a teaching example. After the emergence of Immersion & Invariance theory and subsequent [nonlinear control theories](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity), fundamentalist sliding mode control has been superseded by superior alternatives. It can be fully explained as constructing a (existing) [invariant manifold](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%8D%E5%8F%98%E6%B5%81%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity) and using high gain to crudely handle both finite-time convergence and robustness. A discontinuous switching term associated with the sliding variable is introduced in a brute-force manner, with its amplitude related to an upper bound of the known disturbance (high gain), and the reaching time of the sliding surface is considered. This forcibly separates the two [time scales](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E5%B0%BA%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity), and from the perspective of singular perturbation, the system is divided into fast and slow subsystems, thereby achieving a decomposition effect.

After reaching the sliding surface, the switching term acts as high gain to suppress unknown disturbances in a time-averaged sense. The high-frequency switching function brings drawbacks to the system: rapid actuator switching causes mechanical wear and excessive impact; many sliding mode control papers assume disturbances that do not match the control input channel, and the disturbance is amplified by high gain, which is inferior to disturbance observers in this regard[\[15\]](#ref_15); and even stability cannot be guaranteed.

First, as a special case of I&I, I&I is a coordinate-independent theory that offers greater freedom than sliding mode in the design of target systems and manifold surfaces. The invariant manifold does not have to be reached in finite time, and control based on invariant manifolds does not have to rely solely on high gain and discontinuous inputs (the advantage of sliding mode in comparison is its simplicity—doge). The drawback is that finding the PDE for the diffeomorphism is difficult to handle (for general sliding mode, it is just a linear relationship that avoids solving the PDE). Moreover, many fundamental concepts can be covered by the Isidori normal form plus high-gain feedback. The so-called sliding surface is the manifold where zero dynamics reside; requiring the stability of sliding dynamics is equivalent to requiring zero-dynamics stability, and the so-called finite-time reaching of the sliding surface is equivalent to the finite-time convergence of the integrator chain in the normal form. However, sliding mode of this kind essentially degenerates into a method for finding a zero-dynamic exponentially stable output for minimum-phase systems.

Thus, the truly nontrivial issues for sliding mode control are:

-   The sliding surface may not be an invariant manifold, making it impossible to prove stability using positive semidefinite Lyapunov functions. Theoretically, this manifests as the Filippov solution also being unstable.
-   Discontinuous control laws have a unique role[\[16\]](#ref_16) and require unique analytical methods. Stability proofs for sliding mode are highly nontrivial; proofs that do not account for discontinuous analysis are not merely rigorous but often lead to incorrect stability conclusions[\[17\]](#ref_17). Chattering in many systems is not only caused by feedback delay, feedback error, or high-gain amplified [disturbances](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%A7%E6%89%B0%E5%8A%A8&zhida_source=entity), but is fundamentally unstable in theory.

Second, avoiding the chattering problem caused by high-frequency switching of the [control signal](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E4%BF%A1%E5%8F%B7&zhida_source=entity) is a key consideration in the [algorithm design](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95%E8%AE%BE%E8%AE%A1&zhida_source=entity) and implementation of sliding mode control. Most people prefer the boundary layer approach with PD-like control inside, which is an engineering treatment; Utkin has used filtering of the control input, and there is also a survey specifically discussing how to eliminate chattering in applications. Accurate disturbance upper bounds and handling of mismatched disturbances are also important; second-order and higher-order sliding modes (including the super-twisting controller[\[18\]](#ref_18)) require the sliding variable and its derivatives to all converge to zero, so that the high-frequency switching term appears in the derivative, and the actual control input is an integral of these terms, effectively suppressing chattering.

Based on the characteristics of the controlled system (such as the relative degree of the sliding variable with respect to the control input, and the variables that can be measured in real time) and the performance requirements of the system, further problems have been posed on top of traditional sliding mode control. Unfortunately, many of these address chain reactions caused by the erroneous introduction of sliding mode, rather than tackling the root cause.

**1. Limitations of Linearization and Passivity-Based Structures**

In feedback linearization, the Isidori normal form is always mentioned, but it is not necessary to follow the feedback linearization path. First, it uses a nonlinear control law to brutally cancel the nonlinear parts of the system, which leads to robustness issues when cancellation fails in the presence of system errors. Second, the system nonlinearities may contain stabilizing components, and blindly canceling them can be counterproductive. Third, to cancel nonlinearities, the control effort essentially suppresses nonlinear structures with high gain, which often violates saturation constraints and deteriorates disturbance robustness.

Based on the lower-triangular normal form and the symmetric upper-triangular cascade structures, since the 1990s it has been discovered that Lyapunov functions can be constructed in a cascaded manner for control design. This additive cascade structure fundamentally originates from passivity[\[19\]](#ref_19). The simplest example is backstepping.

Parly, Sepulchre, and Kokotovic, among others, proposed in the 1990s methods for constructing Lyapunov functions for nonlinear systems with special structures, particularly for systems with a "cascade structure."

-   Parly's core idea is to construct a Lyapunov function as a nonlinear "weighted" combination of known sub-Lyapunov functions of two subsystems, using a "nonresonance" condition similar to the Sylvester equation[\[20\]](#ref_20).
-   Sepulchre, Kokotovic, and others focused on constructing the cross-terms in Lyapunov functions for [cascaded systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%A7%E8%81%94%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity). These cross-terms are obtained via [numerical integration](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%95%B0%E5%80%BC%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86&zhida_source=entity), and this approach directly leads to the Forwarding design[\[21\]](#ref_21). ### (9) SoS[\[22\]](#ref_22) and the Moment Problem

This is a method for finding Lyapunov functions using numerical optimization, leveraging polynomial sum-of-squares (SoS) programming[\[23\]](#ref_23). Following LMI, this is another direction where papers are easy to produce, but in reality, the implications are quite profound.

**1. [SoS Programming](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=SoS%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity)**

The following discussion addresses the general problem of finding/verifying nonnegative polynomials.  
A sufficient condition for a polynomial $p(x)\ge 0,\forall x$ is that it can be expressed as a sum of squares of several polynomials: $p(x) = \sum_{i=1}^k(g_i(x))^2$. When $x$ is a single variable, or when the polynomial's highest degree is at most two, this condition is not only sufficient but also necessary. In general, although not necessary, most nonnegative polynomials can be expressed in sum-of-squares form. Reference Hilbert's 17th problem.

Thus, we can search for sum-of-squares polynomials to find polynomials that are always nonnegative. It can be shown that this is a semidefinite programming problem. The following lemma holds:  
$p(x) \text{ is sum-of-squares}\Leftrightarrow \exists Q\succeq 0, \text{ s.t } p(x)=z(x)^TQz(x)$, where $z(x)$ contains all monomials in $x$.

Currently, SOS methods are widely applied in optimization, combinatorics, theoretical [computer vision](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97%E6%9C%BA%E8%A7%86%E8%A7%89&zhida_source=entity), graph theory, [quantum information](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%87%8F%E5%AD%90%E4%BF%A1%E6%81%AF&zhida_source=entity), and many other important areas. If you are interested in using sum-of-squares, consider the MATLAB implementation SPOTLESS[\[24\]](#ref_24) available on GitHub. There are also many examples in the Drake software[\[25\]](#ref_25).

**2. Applications of SoS in Control**

For a system $\dot{x}=f(x)$ (assuming for now that $f(x)$ is polynomial in $x$), if we seek a Lyapunov function $V(x)$ (also assumed polynomial in $x$), the problem can be cast as finding polynomials $V(x),-\dot{V}(x)$ that are always nonnegative. To further ensure negative definiteness of $\dot V(x)$, we look for $-\dot{V}(x)-\varepsilon x^{T}x$.  
Many pioneering works on the application of sum-of-squares in control were carried out by Pablo Parrilo; refer to his thesis[\[26\]](#ref_26). As numerical semidefinite programming became more mature, this method found applications in many other fields. See the papers by Russ Tedrake and Andrew Packard[\[27\]](#ref_27) [\[28\]](#ref_28) [\[29\]](#ref_29).

However, this approach is clearly only applicable to polynomial systems with a polynomial Lyapunov candidate. It cannot find more general function forms, and it is difficult to find a suitable $-\dot V(x)$ that is both a positive semidefinite polynomial and linked to $V$ and $f$. Moreover, as the number of polynomial terms grows, the dimension of the optimization problem increases. The corresponding semidefinite matrix has dimension (n+d), where n is the number of variables and d is the highest degree of the polynomial terms. An increase in the number of terms affects the sparsity of this semidefinite matrix. Many papers study how to exploit this sparsity; see the literature on facial reduction. The main obstacle to applying sum-of-squares currently is the difficulty of scaling to high-dimensional systems. Anirudha Majumdar used a subset of sum-of-squares, strengthening the semidefinite constraints to second-order cone constraints, allowing systems with more than 20 degrees of freedom to be solved[\[30\]](#ref_30); there are also results applied to a 30-state humanoid robot[\[31\]](#ref_31).

**3. The Moment Problem**

The SOS method was independently proposed by Lasserre and Parrilo; Lasserre approached it from the measure perspective, while Parrilo directly constructed SOS polynomials. In my view, Lasserre's work is undoubtedly deeper. Significant subsequent progress includes Henrion's work[\[32\]](#ref_32) [\[33\]](#ref_33) and Lasserre's hierarchy[\[34\]](#ref_34), which uses the Liouville equation to bridge differential equations and linear measure equations.

In control theory, the moment problem is used to compute occupation measures, addressing the measure relaxation problem in optimal control. The dual of the moment problem is the subsolution of the HJB equation, and sum-of-squares provides a truncated implementation of such subsolutions. Thus, the dual of SoS is the moment problem, which can be used to compute global optima of constrained polynomial optimization problems. Interested readers may refer to Lasserre's 45-minute talk at ICM 2018.

### (1) Adaptive Control – Parameter Identification – Integral Control – Extended State/Disturbance Observers

For an introduction to adaptive control, Daniel Liberzon's lecture notes are a good starting point [[35]](#ref_35); they are clear and well-focused.

**1. Technical Nature** [[36]](#ref_36)

Adaptive control is undoubtedly the most technique-oriented research area in systems and control. After going through various complex derivations to obtain an adaptive law, one often cannot discern any inherent physical meaning, nor is there much elegance to it. It involves various special tricks such as normalization and projection. These "special tricks" convey the message that a host of tricks in adaptive control are merely used to prove stability; without using these tricks, many questions—such as whether the system is stable, and what the mechanism of stability/instability is—remain unclear. A controversial comment on adaptive control, made over thirty years ago, still rings true today [[37]](#ref_37).

**2. Convergence and Robustness: Observability/Persistence of Excitation**

Many adaptive-related directions can be analyzed within a unified framework [[38]](#ref_38): adaptive estimation of parameters/disturbances largely treats slowly varying parameters as augmented states, thus avoiding the need for a separation principle to prove overall system stability. Checking observability (the so-called persistence of excitation (PE) condition) ensures stability (which also enhances robustness). An ideal system could prove stability without PE, but robustness would be extremely poor—this is the Rohrs counterexample. The additional PE condition brings robustness to adaptive control and also makes the construction of Lyapunov functions possible. The core here is that the constructed observation-to-parameter mapping is bijective. However, the subsequent design of augmented-state observers or adaptive laws is quite diverse; as mentioned above, the "tricks of the trade" are abundant. Here we briefly explain common issues for beginners to understand the underlying ideas.

One design goal of adaptive update laws is convergence or stability: ensuring the output error $e(t)=y(t)-y^*(t)$ is sufficiently small while keeping states bounded. The most intuitive approach is to search along the direction $-\partial e^2(t)/\partial \tilde{\theta}(t)$. The result of this direction often contains unknown functions, so the question becomes whether one can choose to search along an approximation of this gradient direction; this depends on the error equation dynamics.

**(1) Memoryless Estimation** [[39]](#ref_39)

The linear case is much more intuitive. Estimating a parameter vector $θ∈\mathbb R^n$ from $y∈\mathbb R$ satisfying the linear regression $y(t)=ϕ(t)^⊤θ$. For a unique solution, the matrix $(ϕ(t_1),…,ϕ(t_n))$ must be full rank at at least $n$ time instants—this is the so-called identifiability. The most fundamental problem in adaptive control and system identification is to estimate online continuously from system dynamics, ensuring robustness while tracking slowly varying parameters online. Persistence of excitation essentially maintains identifiability over time. Defining $\tilde \theta =\theta -\theta^*$, gradient descent yields the following linear time-varying error system:

$$
\dot{\tilde \theta} =−γϕ(t)ϕ(t)^⊤\tildeθ
$$

- From the perspective of dynamical system stability, the origin of the above system is exponentially stable if and only if $ϕ(t)$ is persistently exciting, i.e., $\exists T,k, \ \ s.t. \forall t \ \ ∫_t^{t+T}ϕ(τ)ϕ(τ)^⊤dτ≥kI_n$. The best estimate of the exponential convergence rate is due to Loria and Elena; in some cases, as the gain $\gamma$ tends to infinity, the convergence rate may tend to zero (this does not occur when $θ$ is a scalar). In 2017, Barabanov and Praly independently provided weaker asymptotic stability conditions.
- From a geometric perspective, within each time interval $[t,t+T)$, $ϕ(t)$ rotates through directions spanning the full space. Since $rankϕ(t)ϕ(t)^⊤=1$, the PE condition holds only if, within each interval $[t,t+T)$, $ϕ(t)$ rotates through $n$ linearly independent "directions".
- From the perspective of averaging theory, PE ensures that the (time-varying) system matrix remains positive definite. The averaged dynamics of the error system [[40]](#ref_40) take the form: $\dot{\bar {\tilde {θ}}}=−γ\bar A(t)\bar{\tilde{θ}}, \ \ \bar A:=\frac {1}T∫_t^{t+T}ϕ(τ)ϕ(τ)^⊤dτ$, where $\bar θ$ is the averaged variable of $θ$. If the averaged system matrix $\bar A(t)$ is positive definite under the PE condition, convergence follows.
- From a frequency-domain perspective, the PE condition requires that the spectral richness of the system input be no less than $n$. The simplest case is when the regression matrix $ϕ(t)$ is generated by an SISO-LTI system: the input $u$ contains no fewer than $n$ spectral components, i.e., $u$ can be decomposed as $u=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{sin⁡(ω_it+ψ_i)}$ with $m≥n/2$ [[41]](#ref_41), if and only if the regression matrix $ϕ(t)$ is persistently exciting. This was first proposed in [[42]](#ref_42) [[43]](#ref_43); a recent reference [[44]](#ref_44) points out inaccuracies in the consistency problems discussed in related literature. The PE condition achieved through a sufficiently rich spectral input $u$ has parameters $T,k$ that depend on initial conditions and are not uniform, which differs slightly from the definition of the PE condition. The resulting adaptive closed-loop system can only guarantee global asymptotic stability, not exponential stability.

It can be seen that, within this framework, the signal reflecting system dynamics at each instant is only used to update the direction of parameter estimation (the derivative) at that moment; once the signal no longer contains rich information about system dynamics, parameter updates cease.

**(2) Sliding-Window Estimation** [[45]](#ref_45)

By extending the linear regression to a higher dimension, decoupling of multiple parameter estimates becomes possible. This decoupling relaxes the conditions for parameter convergence, because a new regression model is obtained and the operational space expands, providing many means to enhance the excitation condition of the original regression [[46]](#ref_46) [[47]](#ref_47). For example, the concurrent learning method [[48]](#ref_48) yields the error equation

$$
\dot{\tilde \theta}=-\gamma_1 \phi \phi^T \tilde{\theta} - \gamma_2 \Phi \Phi^T \tilde{\theta}
$$

where $\Phi$ contains system information collected at multiple past time instants. The PE condition can be removed and parameter convergence still guaranteed, as long as $\Phi \Phi^T$ is full rank. The essence is to use not only current information but also previously collected historical data to update the parameter estimation direction. Even if real-time signals no longer contain rich information from some point onward, parameter updates can still proceed through historical data.

The question arises: if historical data can be reused indefinitely, why not simply invert the regression matrix composed of historical data and solve for the true parameter values in one step? This is the dilemma adaptive control faces. Direct inversion offers no theoretical contribution and precludes the use of Lyapunov methods, making publication difficult. Without inversion, PE is overly ideal, too stringent, and impractical. Hence, various edge-case variants have emerged.

For instance, initial excitation or interval excitation was proposed [[49]](#ref_49), introducing a historical sliding window—essentially no different:

$$
\dot{\tilde \theta}=-\gamma_1 \phi \phi^T \tilde{\theta} - \gamma_2 \int_\mathcal{T} \phi \phi^T \tilde{\theta} \text{d}t
$$

Ortega proposed DREM (dynamic regressor extension and mixing) [[50]](#ref_50) [[51]](#ref_51) [[52]](#ref_52), which computes the determinant and adjugate of the regression matrix, stopping just one step short of actual inversion. DREM allows the extended regressor to be full rank at certain instants, whereas the original $\phi\phi^T$ cannot be full rank at any instant, thus accelerating convergence. If gradient descent is applied to the original regressor, the convergence rate has a complicated dependence on parameter variations.

**(3) Integral Control and the Passivity Direction** [[53]](#ref_53)

If the integral direction in integral control is chosen well—for example, using passivity: $\dot V=\frac {∂V}{∂x}f(x)+\frac {∂V}{∂x}g(x)u=L_fV+L_gVu≤y^Tu$. Integrating along the passivity direction $L_gV$ yields PI control: $\dot \theta = \gamma \left[ L_gV \right]^\top$, which also constitutes an adaptive control law. State feedback (P) combined with integral control (I) provides stronger rejection of slowly varying disturbances and guarantees zero steady-state error.

**3. Robustness: Separation Principle**

If parameters are unknown but all states are measurable, designing a controller to keep system trajectories bounded is entirely feasible. When only outputs—not states—are available, adaptive control is needed, but output-only control of nonlinear systems becomes very difficult, largely due to the separation principle.

The most mainstream approach in adaptive control remains connecting a parameterized controller with an estimator (tuner, adaptive law, etc.). Besides parameter identification, the parameter estimator is generally used to eliminate additive noise introduced by estimation error. If the PE condition is ignored, the adaptive control in most papers essentially defines a Lyapunov function as the sum of squares of tracking/state estimation/parameter estimation errors, then constructs an adaptive law, substitutes it into the derivative of the Lyapunov function to cancel terms and prove stability, and sets the corresponding coefficients to zero for whichever term is troublesome. Convergence is guaranteed and the paper looks nice, but the final result is usually no more than a non-strict Lyapunov function; the closed-loop system cannot even be analyzed for ISS with respect to disturbances. Robustness is questionable, and real systems are easily destabilized. Moreover, many theoretical results are only applicable to estimating parameters with affine structure. Sometimes the parameter structure is not that simple. Sometimes numerical fitting works far better than Lyapunov-based methods, mainly because high gain is necessary for theoretical stability. In design, using lower gain for estimation dynamics [[54]](#ref_54)—although the estimation error may not converge to zero—allows the parameter estimate to be treated as "frozen" when analyzing system dynamics. This is the most widely known idea of **certainty equivalence** in parameter estimation and adaptive control, and in observer design it is called the **separation principle**.

In practice, it is often better to separate parameter estimation from controller design as much as possible: it is simple and intuitive, and more sophisticated algorithms can be used to estimate parameters alone without simultaneously considering system stability. Once essentially separated, parameter estimation can even become a parameter fitting problem (e.g., MHE), independent of any Lyapunov function construction; one can first derive the regression equation and then perform closed-loop stability analysis, which often works better than directly computing adaptive laws via Lyapunov functions. However, for the separation principle to hold approximately, this low-gain design leads to slow adaptation, which creates issues in transient performance. Thus, $L_1$ adaptive control advocates increasing the gain to improve transient performance, though this is also subject to criticism [[55]](#ref_55):

- Low-pass filters cause instability and lead to state-space realizations that do not depend on unknown parameters;
- High-gain adaptive coefficients cause numerical instability in solving differential equations, sensitivity to unmodeled dynamics, and may lead to the freezing property of the estimation error norm [[56]](#ref_56);
- Practical scenarios where all states of a linear system are known but parameters are unknown are very rare.

The waterbed effect of gain forces one to consider more structure, so even without considering drift, a host of techniques such as projection are introduced.

But tracing back to the root: rather than designing such a "beautiful" adaptive law to handle parametric uncertainty—which appears to converge but has poor robustness—it is better to measure the parameters carefully in the first place. If parameter identification is to be done, it is best done offline, leaving only statistical analysis. If online identification on a real system is unavoidable, sliding-window inversion suffices. Alternatively, more robust control techniques can be employed. However, most of the identification results discussed above can only serve as monitoring. Using parameters to update the controller in real time is another long story entirely.

### (2) Robust Control

**1. Linear Robust Control** [[57]](#ref_57)

Robust control in its early days dealt primarily with linear systems. It emerged in the late 1970s to address control problems in aerospace. The methods used were pure mathematical techniques based on linear operator theory. Later, in 1989, Doyle, Glover, Khargonekar, and Francis proposed a state-space approach to solving linear $H_\infty$ control problems, which remains one of the two major methods for solving $H_\infty$ problems to this day.

Since the 1990s, a body of highly practical and elegant methods emerged: $H_\infty$ mixed-sensitivity, the $\mu$ method, and $H_\infty$ loop-shaping. These linear robust control techniques directly and indirectly advanced the US aerospace industry; numerous aircraft engines and spacecraft attitude control systems are based on these methods, collectively referred to in industry as the DGKF approach. Several monographs fully discuss the DGKF approach; the best among them is Professor Kemin Zhou's *Robust and Optimal Control*, which presents frequency-domain robust control theory with exceptional elegance, combining both theoretical depth and practical utility.

The linear matrix inequality (LMI) approach to solving $H_\infty$ control problems became mainstream in the late 1980s and early 1990s, gaining prominence and development alongside the maturation of robust control. Many researchers contributed, particularly those from the robust control community, with representative figures including P. Apkarian, C. Scherer, T. Iwasaki, and A. Rantzer. This is a more effective and simpler method, numerically more efficient and more concise than the DGKF approach based on solving two Riccati equations. Boyd himself, as a master of interior-point methods, authored a monograph that became the bible of this direction. Linear robust control thus entered a new era of rapid development, with a host of time-domain techniques independent of frequency domain being established: state-feedback-based, output-feedback-based, quadratic-index-based, linear uncertain time-delay systems, and many others—a wealth of time-domain theorems that effectively solve control problems for linear uncertain systems. LMI can also be readily applied in the frequency domain to implement DGKF methods, among others. This period produced Dullerud's *A Course in Robust Control Theory*, Professors Kang-Zhi Liu and Yu Yao's *Linear Robust Control* (available in both Chinese and English), and Skogestad's *Multivariable Feedback Control: Analysis and Design*.

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/111546379?utm_psn=1703781355819773952](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/111546379?utm_psn=1703781355819773952)

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/562083866](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/562083866)

Nearly thirty years since the $H_\infty$ control problem was first posed, it still plays a pivotal role in many high-end control technologies. Many precision instruments—such as computer disk drives—run algorithms based largely on $\mu$-synthesis and loop-shaping. Linear robust control is an extremely important and powerful tool that efficiently solves many control problems. It is also highly mature; as Professor Hongye Su once remarked, doing robust control research nowadays is like a student doing homework. Still, some members of the control community continue to churn out papers using LMI.

Ever since the finite-dimensional state-space $H_\infty$ problem was formulated, infinite-dimensional control problems have existed alongside it. The original operator-based methods can address infinite-dimensional problems, but they are very complex and far less practical than DGKF and LMI approaches. This is because robust control was born to handle model uncertainty. Model uncertainty can be classified into two types: one where the physical system itself is so complex that an accurate model cannot be obtained; the other where the physical system can be precisely described by a mathematical model, but the model is too complex to be suitable for designing controllers with existing control techniques [[58]](#ref_58). For this reason, robust control of distributed parameter systems has not seen widespread application. In fact, complex distributed parameter systems can be approximated by high-order finite-dimensional models, with model error treated uniformly as model uncertainty.

**2. Nonlinear Robust Control**

Similarly, the success of linear robust control turned attention to robust control of nonlinear systems. Applying linear techniques to essentially nonlinear systems forces overly conservative controller designs: to guarantee stability, bandwidth is pushed very low, degrading tracking and disturbance rejection performance, failing to meet requirements for high precision, high stability, and tracking performance. Nonlinear control already has many mature techniques, and the effectiveness of linear MIMO robust control has fueled the desire for equivalent results in nonlinear systems. The self-duality of the L2 norm makes the equations semilinear, reducing to Riccati equations for LQR-type problems; however, the nonlinear $H_\infty$ problem ultimately transforms into the HJI equation, a nonlinear PDE. Analytic solutions are unattainable, yet various approximation methods have been developed to obtain nonlinear robust controllers.

Currently, many control theorists developing robust controllers or observers rely on blanket assumptions and piling terms onto Lyapunov functions to handle so-called disturbances and uncertainties; robustness is essentially achieved by boosting controller gains. The design philosophy itself is flawed, and the resulting controllers are necessarily conservative. Here, conservatism manifests as extremely large bandwidth—or high gain. As long as the control period is sufficiently small, bounded stability to a desired accuracy can always be achieved. The limiting case is sliding mode control, where the gain is nearly infinite; so-called chattering suppression merely artificially reduces the gain to a finite value. These controllers have generated an avalanche of papers in the theory community, but high gain makes the system vulnerable to measurement noise and high-frequency uncertainties in the plant or actuators, resulting in poor engineering performance.

Thus, in practice, nonlinear robust controllers are almost nowhere to be seen; where they do appear, it is often "crying wine and selling vinegar." What is truly used is disturbance observers: precisely modeling disturbances, estimating them online, fitting them offline, and then compensating via feedforward.

**1. System Analysis**[[59]](#ref_59) [[60]](#ref_60)

Geometric theory also originated from linear systems. Research on linear systems is broadly divided into four approaches: the common [state-space method](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity), the algebraic theory represented by Kalman, the geometric theory represented by Wonham[[61]](#ref_61), and polynomial frequency-domain theory. The core of the geometric viewpoint is to describe system properties through the properties of subspaces. For example, controllability is described by the controllability subspace V, where V is an [invariant subspace](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%8D%E5%8F%98%E5%AD%90%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) of A, is also the image space of B, and is the minimal such subspace; then V is the controllability subspace of the system, and the rank of V serves as a measure of controllability. Observability is defined analogously.

When the object becomes a nonlinear system, such as a system on a Lie group, the system is described by vector fields on manifolds, and system properties can be studied through the distributions generated by these vector fields. From this viewpoint, one can also study controllability, observability, and Kalman decompositions of nonlinear systems. On this basis, studying the properties of vector fields or distributions on manifolds constitutes the theory of system analysis.

Linear systems determine controllability via a rank criterion by evaluating the rank of the controllability matrix; in geometric control theory, there is a corresponding tool (via Lie bracket operations). In effect, the results of linear control theory are special cases of geometric control theory—the special case where the distribution does not depend on x. This part is covered in the first portion of Isidori's *Nonlinear Control Systems*.

**2. System Design**

Further, researchers such as Bullo and Marsden work on geometric mechanics and control, while others like Foni and Sepulchre use geometry to characterize Lyapunov theory. Studies of the relationship between Riemannian geometry, [geodesics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B5%8B%E5%9C%B0%E7%BA%BF&zhida_source=entity), and Lyapunov functions are not directly related to distributions, but require the study of Riemannian metrics.

There is also the topic of controlled geometric reduction, which concerns how to simplify [complex systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%8D%E6%9D%82%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) through conserved quantities, so that trajectories in high dimensions correspond to trajectories on lower-dimensional manifolds. The advantage is that [trajectory planning](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BD%A8%E8%BF%B9%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity) can be performed in the lower-dimensional space. Zero dynamics and symmetry-based geometric reduction are examples of this. This has many applications in locomotion research, e.g., Grizzle's papers on hybrid zero dynamics.

### (4) MPC

MPC is the concrete application of predictive control, with two core aspects: stability and numerical solution of optimal control under a receding horizon. In fact, since MPC is used not only for trajectory tracking but also for trajectory generation via predictive ideas, it should more properly be called a planner. Thanks to optimization tools, MPC excels at handling complex systems with complex constraints by optimizing the [objective function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%AE%E6%A0%87%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) over the prediction horizon.

Currently, robust model predictive control (RMPC), which fuses the robustness of traditional feedback control with predictive control ideas, is also a promising control theory.

The most classical approach uses LMIs to handle model uncertainty[[62]](#ref_62). Unfortunately, most people have since produced a flood of papers on invariant-set-based robust methods following the seminal works, typically applying the Schur complement lemma to formulate LMIs. Moreover, much of this robustness, as mentioned earlier, simply amounts to cranking up the high gain.

Another classical approach uses tubes to handle additive disturbances[[63]](#ref_63). The essence is to let MPC serve as a planning algorithm that plans only for a virtual state, with a tracking controller following that virtual state. This has been used in planning-and-control for 20–30 years. Building on this, the homothetic tube method[[64]](#ref_64), and the further developed parameterized tube[[65]](#ref_65) and elastic tube[[66]](#ref_66) methods, each made different improvements. In recent years, these ideas have been reinvented by researchers in embodied intelligence and VLA as "action chunking" and "trajectory switching" techniques to address "inference latency" and achieve "smooth execution."

### (5) Observers[[67]](#ref_67)

In this area,

[@天天天蓝](//www.zhihu.com/people/59fa7ac9d5297eae502f7c96339aa000)

is the undisputed authority on Zhihu.

[What are the classic textbooks or papers for learning nonlinear system observers?](https://www.zhihu.com/question/47976241/answer/108612311?utm_psn=1709357719914516480)

Nonlinear system state observers, classified by stabilization mechanism and ordered by popularity:

**1. High-Gain Observer**

Appearing in nonlinear system state estimation starting in 1992 (traceable to the 1970s in linear systems), this is currently the most widely studied class of nonlinear observers. It is typically used for systems in observer canonical form and lower-triangular structure (non-lower-triangular systems have also been studied in the past five years). A common application scenario is the estimation of "dirty derivatives," such as velocity and acceleration.

[Discussing the High-Gain Observer](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/513485436?utm_psn=1709359144035897344)

Stabilization mechanism: high-gain injection or domination.

Simple structure, easy design, strong robustness; as long as the system can be transformed into canonical form, it can be applied. However, it is extremely sensitive to measurement noise—a common drawback of high gain.

**2. KKL Observer (Kazantizis-Kravaris-Luenberger observer)**

Also called the nonlinear Luenberger observer; the two names reflect an academic dispute. The core idea originates from a special [coordinate transformation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9D%90%E6%A0%87%E5%8F%98%E6%8D%A2&zhida_source=entity) in Luenberger's seminal observer paper from the 1960s. It was later shown that for linear systems the transformation reduces to the identity, and the idea gradually faded from academic memory; it is generally not mentioned in linear systems textbooks. In a 1998 paper by Kazantizis and Kravaris, the idea was "rediscovered" in the nonlinear systems community. Over the following two decades, it received sustained attention, but the design difficulty is high, and the literature is much sparser than for high-gain observers.

Stabilization mechanism: coordinate transformation yielding linear error dynamics.

Insensitive to measurement noise; the constructed dynamic extension is linear, allowing frequency-domain rejection of specific noise bands. For observable autonomous nonlinear systems, existence is guaranteed under very mild assumptions, and the associated PDE always admits analytic solutions. The theory is elegant and admits systematic numerical algorithms. However, for non-autonomous systems, the existence of KKL observers remains an open problem.

**3. I&I Observer (Immersion and Invariance observer)**

Introduced in 2008, popular in mechanical and mechatronic systems, with relatively fewer studies for general nonlinear systems; it is a generalization of KKL. As far as I recall, the global asymptotic convergence observer problem for [nonholonomic](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E5%AE%8C%E6%95%B4%E7%BA%A6%E6%9D%9F&zhida_source=entity) mechanical systems was first solved by this method.

Stabilization mechanism: designing an attractive invariant manifold.

The framework is broad, "in principle" widely applicable, and generally insensitive to noise. However, solving the PDE is difficult and requires considerable physical insight.

**4. Moving Horizon Observer**

The dual problem of MPC, no further elaboration needed. Quite a few researchers work on it, largely overlapping with the MPC community. Stabilization mechanism: online optimization, constituting a feedback process.

Numerical algorithm; perhaps less demanding theoretically? Weak interpretability, high computational cost.

**5. Parameter Estimation-Based Observer (PEBO)**

Proposed in 2015, it is a representative example of application-driven theory, originating from motor state estimation and later developing into a systematic state estimation methodology. The core idea is to convert the time-varying state estimation problem into online constant identification, typically combined with DREM parameter estimation.

Stabilization mechanism: online parameter identification.

Parameter identification is much easier than state estimation, offering great flexibility; in mechatronic and [power systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%94%B5%E5%8A%9B%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity), the associated PDEs are easy to solve. However, parameter estimation typically requires conditions like PE, and pure integrators accumulate measurement noise (although not as severely as in high-gain and sliding mode observers).

**6. Sliding Mode Observer**

The dual of SMC; applicable to essentially the same systems as high-gain observers because the underlying idea overlaps heavily with high gain. Quite a few researchers work on it, and it is relatively easy to get started with. In sliding mode controller design, the goal is to make the control system insensitive to unknown bounded disturbances, with the sliding surface being a function of state or output variables. In sliding mode observer design, the aim is to make the observed states insensitive to bounded noise; the sliding surface is typically a function of the output estimation error. When the observer dynamics operate on the sliding surface—i.e., the output estimation error is zero—the state [estimation error](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235880691&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%A7%82%E6%B5%8B%E8%AF%AF%E5%B7%AE&zhida_source=entity) converges to zero.

Stabilization mechanism: high-gain injection/domination.

Simple structure, strong robustness. Drawbacks are the same as high-gain observers: extreme sensitivity to measurement noise.

**7. Passivity-Based Observer**

Introduced about a decade ago, this is the dual of PBC. The goal is to render the redesigned input (typically the measurement noise channel) passive with respect to a designed invariant manifold. It has received little attention, with only sporadic publications over the past decade or so.

Stabilization mechanism: passivation.

Very robust to measurement noise, and the robustness is easy to tune. However, design difficulty is high; the PDE involved is harder to handle than in the I&I method.

**8. Monotonicity-Based Observer**

This is a somewhat vague concept with no systematic definition or analysis in the literature. It is often seen in application-oriented papers, exploiting monotonicity properties of nonlinear mappings in the dynamics to avoid the use of high gain.

**9. Linearization by Output Injection**

I could not think of a good translation for this method. It was proposed by Krener and Isidori in 1983. The method is very complex, and the PDEs that must be solved are unsatisfiable for the vast majority of physical systems. Nevertheless, it is the origin of the nonlinear observer problem, and research over the past three decades has been influenced by it to varying degrees.

# Part 6: Overview of Nonlinear Control Theory Development

## Course Description

This course serves as an advanced graduate course in the control theory field, primarily oriented toward doctoral students. It systematically introduces the fundamental theories and methods of nonlinear control systems, covering core content including nonlinear system modeling, stability analysis, feedback linearization, adaptive control, robust control, and nonlinear observer design. Through both theoretical derivation and case analysis, the course aims to help students establish a comprehensive nonlinear control theoretical framework, fostering the ability to independently conduct research on nonlinear control problems.

---

## Key Learning Objectives

1. **Master core theories**: Deeply understand the basic theories of nonlinear systems, including Lyapunov stability theory, LaSalle's invariance principle, and input-output stability theory.
2. **Grasp key methods**: Skillfully apply feedback linearization, backstepping design, sliding mode control, and other major nonlinear control synthesis methods.
3. **Familiarity with advanced topics**: Understand the knowledge framework in frontier areas such as adaptive control, robust control, and nonlinear observers, and be able to conduct preliminary research in related fields based on actual engineering background.
4. **Engineering application capability**: Combine with typical engineering examples in actual industrial scenarios to analyze and design nonlinear controllers.

## Course Notes

### Key Mathematical Tools: Lie Derivatives and Lie Brackets

- **Lie derivative**: For a scalar function $\lambda(x)$ and a vector field $f(x)$, the Lie derivative is defined as:

$$
L_f \lambda(x) = \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial x} f(x)
$$

- **Recursive definition**: $L_f^k \lambda(x) = L_f \left( L_f^{k-1} \lambda(x) \right)$, with $L_f^0 \lambda(x) = \lambda(x)$.
- **Lie bracket**: For two vector fields $f$ and $g$:

$$
[f, g](x) = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} f(x) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} g(x)
$$

- **Applicability condition of exact linearization**: It is required that the distribution $\text{span}\{ g, \text{ad}_f g, \ldots, \text{ad}_f^{n-1} g \}$ has dimension $n$ in a neighborhood of $x_0$, and the set $\{ g, \text{ad}_f g, \ldots, \text{ad}_f^{n-2} g \}$ is involutive.

### Lyapunov Stability Judgment : Key Points

- **Positive definite function**: $V(x) > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$, and $V(0) = 0$.
- **Stability**: If $\dot{V}(x) \leq 0$, then the equilibrium point is stable.
- **Asymptotic stability**: If $\dot{V}(x) < 0$ (for $x \neq 0$), then the equilibrium point is asymptotically stable.

> **Note**: When using Lyapunov functions, the selection of the function directly affects the ease of the judging process. A good Lyapunov function often embodies the "energy" characteristics of the system.
