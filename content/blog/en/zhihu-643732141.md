I have just started my junior year and am about to begin coursework in automation. Over the past while, I have absorbed various bits of content on mathematics and control theory from different sources. I deeply feel my own ignorance and smallness, yet this has also sparked many thoughts, which I record here. I also list many learning resources.

I hope this can be helpful to everyone!

First, it is fairly obvious that control theory is closely tied to mathematics. Although many claim that doing control theory means no job prospects outside of academia (×*ω×*), let us not delve too deeply into that issue for now. I believe we need to find a balance between theory and practice, a ground where the two converge, and they should corroborate and reinforce each other.

The description of dynamical systems relies on modeling them as differential equations. Different systems can be described by different differential equations. Lumped-parameter systems are described by ordinary differential equations (ODEs), while distributed-parameter systems are described by partial differential equations (PDEs). Typical examples of distributed-parameter systems include fields, fluids, elastic bodies, and large-scale natural or social systems. The modeling process draws on various fields of physics, such as electromagnetism, thermodynamics, and fluid mechanics.

We focus mainly on lumped-parameter systems. ODEs can be linear or nonlinear, giving rise to linear and nonlinear systems; they can have constant or time-varying coefficients, giving rise to time-invariant and time-varying systems; there are also continuous-time and discrete-time systems.

### Classical Control Theory

For [classical control theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231182309&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E5%8F%A4%E5%85%B8%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity), which is the main part of the automatic control principles that undergraduates learn (some less demanding majors offer a "Fundamentals of Control Engineering" course instead). This deals with single-input single-output (SISO) continuous-time linear time-invariant (LTI) systems. The main object of study is the scalar (corresponding to SISO) [constant-coefficient linear ODE](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231182309&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B8%B8%E7%B3%BB%E6%95%B0%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity), with the core method being to derive the transfer function via the [Laplace transform](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231182309&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Laplace%E5%8F%98%E6%8D%A2&zhida_source=entity).

Besides the time-domain description of systems by differential equations, there is the complex-domain description via the transfer function and the convolution-based signal description. Studying the solutions of differential equations—that is, the system response—through the transfer function is a main component of classical control theory. From this, time-domain, complex-domain, and frequency-domain methods of system analysis were developed. The first two are organically linked; the frequency-domain method also stems from the transfer function. As for the convolution description, I think Signals and Systems is the foundational course for systematically learning this aspect.

The transfer function is a rational function on the complex plane, which can be subjected to zero-pole factorization and partial fraction decomposition of rational functions. This can be split into decomposition over real irreducible polynomials (linear and quadratic factors) and decomposition over complex irreducible polynomials (linear factors). These decompositions are fully exploited in the subsequent system analysis.

The poles are actually the characteristic roots of the system, corresponding to the exponential parts of the time-domain response: the real part represents exponential growth, and the imaginary part represents oscillation (which, via [Euler's formula](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231182309&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AC%A7%E6%8B%89%E5%85%AC%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity), can be converted into trigonometric functions). This shows that the poles of the transfer function influence the modes of the response—that is, the overall behavior. Clearly, only if all poles have negative real parts can stability be guaranteed, because then all terms decay exponentially over time; if any term grows exponentially, the system diverges.

Through partial fraction decomposition, one finds that the undetermined coefficients in the numerator depend on both the zeros and the poles, which is why it is said that the values of the zeros affect the magnitudes of the modes. Moreover, a zero and a pole of the same order that are close together form a dipole pair whose effects can cancel. To me, this is somewhat reminiscent of the Riemann [removable singularity](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231182309&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%AF%E5%8E%BB%E5%A5%87%E7%82%B9&zhida_source=entity) theorem—something worth further thought. In any case, since the zero lies in the numerator, its influence is more abstract and not as intuitive as the pole, which is an eigenvalue.

This shows that zeros and poles affect the time-domain response—that is time-domain analysis. If we represent the zero-pole locations on the s-plane, we can directly visualize system properties; moreover, for transfer functions with parameters, we can see how adjusting parameters affects pole locations—this is complex-domain analysis, the [root locus method](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231182309&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A0%B9%E8%BD%A8%E8%BF%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity). This is also why I said time-domain and complex-domain analyses are organically linked.

The decomposition into real irreducible polynomials allows the transfer function to be split into canonical elements for study, with linear, quadratic, and constant terms in numerator and denominator. This yields proportional, inertial, oscillatory, derivative, first- and second-order derivative, and integral elements. This canonical decomposition greatly facilitates frequency-domain analysis. Frequency-domain analysis derives the system response to sinusoidal inputs at different frequencies and shows that the transfer function G(s) can be analyzed by writing it as G(jω) (in fact, taking the Laplace transform on the imaginary axis gives the Fourier spectrum). Since G(jω) is a complex number, when canonical elements are multiplied, magnitudes multiply and phases add. The [Bode plot](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231182309&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Bode%E5%9B%BE&zhida_source=entity) uses logarithmic coordinates, which conveniently represents variables spanning large orders of magnitude and simplifies the magnitude-frequency characteristics of cascaded elements (the logarithm turns multiplication of magnitudes into addition, meaning Bode curves simply add). Finally, through the [argument principle](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231182309&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B9%85%E8%A7%92%E5%8E%9F%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity), we link the zeros and poles of open-loop and closed-loop transfer functions to the geometric features of curves on the s-plane, giving rise to various stability criteria.

Incidentally, students in communications and information engineering, who mainly study Signals and Systems, tend to view things from a signal-processing perspective. For instance, they prefer spectral analysis via integral transforms, that is, they favor frequency-domain analysis. Systems and transfer functions appear to them more as filters.

Finally, there are the system design methods. My current understanding of these is limited to determining parameters from known requirements or performing zero-pole placement. Zero-pole placement essentially introduces elements to adjust the overall zero-pole locations of the system, thereby improving performance; this involves various compensators. In textbooks, this content is split into a system design section within system analysis chapters and a separate chapter on system compensation. However, in classical control theory, all of this is presented largely from a frequency-domain perspective, with the Bode plot as the primary tool.

### Linear Systems

Since the author has not yet studied this part closely, I will speculate based on my own understanding, which may be quite fanciful—please bear with me.

Another core undergraduate course for automation-related majors is [modern control theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231182309&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8E%B0%E4%BB%A3%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity). In my private opinion, the name "modern control theory" is not very apt, much like the name "abstract algebra"—neither describes its subject well. What is called "modern" is not all that modern in some sense; it is merely more modern than classical. At the graduate level, there is a course called [linear system theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=231182309&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity), which straightforwardly states its object of study.

Modern control theory deals with multi-input multi-output (MIMO) linear time-invariant systems. One can say that the main object of classical control theory is a special case of modern control theory, and linear system theory is a further systematization and generalization. These systems are described by constant-coefficient linear ODEs with vector-valued functions. The theory of linear ODEs is already well established in the mathematics undergraduate course on ordinary differential equations.

Here I feel we can completely transplant the classical control theory framework, viewing it from three perspectives.

### Nonlinear Systems

What follows is my speculation after taking Teacher 647's ODE course, combined with my preview of automatic control and things I have browsed on Zhihu. It is nothing but fanciful thinking...

The theory of ODEs includes not only solution methods but also qualitative theory and geometric theory. As is well known, very few ODEs can be solved in closed form; linear differential equations are one such class. Most equations (perhaps one you write down casually) cannot be solved, and one can only resort to power series methods. Hence, qualitative theory is of great importance: its core idea is to study the properties of solutions without solving the equations. This involves existence and uniqueness of solutions, dependence of solutions on parameters and initial conditions, continuation of solutions, and stability of solutions. For systems, we care mainly about stability, controllability, observability, etc.

These are all necessary steps toward geometric theory, that is, dynamical systems. Differential equations and dynamical systems can be said to view differential equations and their solutions from another angle through phase space, and this perspective is especially common for nonlinear systems. Trajectories in phase space reflect the properties of the solutions; we study the existence, number, and stability of equilibrium points, periodic orbits, and so on.

Of course, mathematics mostly studies autonomous equations, whereas control theory also cares about the control input u.

As for the local-versus-global debate, this is another important dialectical relationship in geometry, which I will not discuss at length due to my limited level. But I know that many theoretical results show that geometric structures are locally similar, meaning local theory is no longer of much interest (for mathematicians—and I say this because mathematicians generally do not care about practical problems or computation; they feel that if local structures are similar, there is no need to distinguish them, while applications may still require digging into many deeper implications). Many branches of mathematics work at the global level, such as differential topology and other large-scale analysis fields. Moreover, once global geometric properties are involved, tools from algebraic topology inevitably come into play.

[https://m.douban.com/book/review/10562763/](https://m.douban.com/book/review/10562763/)

For nonlinear system theory, I have found that in this vast land grow fields such as sliding mode control, robust control, adaptive control, and others. At the same time, geometric control introduces the more powerful tool of manifolds, becoming the general theoretical framework for nonlinear systems. All at once, I seemed to discover the extraordinarily close connection between control theory and mathematics—just like the connection between mechanics and mathematics. System dynamics is embedded in control theory.
