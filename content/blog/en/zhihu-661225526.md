Recently, some students have asked me about learning pathways and methods. I have been self-studying courses from mathematics departments since graduating from high school. Looking back, even though I planned my studies with great caution, I now realize I still took some detours. During this period, I also repeatedly pondered certain questions. For instance: as an engineering student, what role should my passion for mathematics play in my life; and as an engineering student who loves mathematics—specifically one in [automation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%87%AA%E5%8A%A8%E5%8C%96%E4%B8%93%E4%B8%9A&zhida_source=entity)—how should I plan my studies toward theory or mathematics? For core learning routes, which "bible"-level books and resources should one consult directly? Such methodological reflection is highly beneficial for cultivating one's own learning ability.

So this article serves as my current, stage-by-stage answer, and I hope it resonates with theory enthusiasts in automation-related fields as well as students simply looking to broaden their horizons. Note that this piece is largely aimed at senior undergraduates and graduate students, so I welcome criticism and corrections from all experts orz.

## 1. How to Understand Mathematics, Mathematics and Control

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/600292787?utm_psn=1696339472319438848](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/600292787?utm_psn=1696339472319438848)

This is an article I abandoned long ago, and due to my insufficient knowledge of algebra, I cannot complete it anytime soon. Of course, reading similar articles on Zhihu is also fine. The first point I want to make is that **"mathematics, as it has developed in the modern era, is so vast that even scholars in different sub-branches within the same field cannot fully understand each other's work."**

Therefore, as engineering students, on the one hand we should hold mathematics in awe. From a curriculum perspective, an undergraduate in engineering knows only slightly more mathematics than the general public—just calculus, linear algebra, and probability theory. Graduate students add a bit of matrix theory, [numerical analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%95%B0%E5%80%BC%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90&zhida_source=entity), stochastic processes, and [functional analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B3%9B%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90&zhida_source=entity). If a lack of breadth leads to an inadequate appreciation of what mathematics truly is, that carries many hidden risks.

Moreover, from the perspective of control-related disciplines (especially automation), control theory itself is a branch of applied mathematics, whereas [control engineering](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E5%B7%A5%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) leans toward practical engineering. This is what makes automation unique among engineering majors—it sits precisely at the interface between theory and practice, and can be seen as the culmination of [engineering mathematics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B7%A5%E7%A8%8B%E6%95%B0%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity). In fact, I strongly dislike the characterization of automation as a "jack-of-all-trades" major, which implies that it dabbles in everything without mastering anything—that is profoundly wrong. Automation has its own core competitiveness, and that lies in control science. At a higher level of competence, it is the ability to apply theory to analyze practical problems, and further, the ability to understand theory and practical problems both efficiently and deeply.

I just mentioned "core competitiveness." Some people, upon hearing "control science," may think, "Oh, all those control theories nowadays are just meaningless tricks to churn out papers," or "Control is just PID, isn't it?" But these are again deep misunderstandings. Regarding the former, in any community, people's abilities vary. One should not generalize from a few low-quality practitioners and drag down all those working in control theory. Regarding the latter, to be objective, it is true that in the vast arena of production and daily life, PID and its variants account for the largest share. But that does not mean we should overlook the truly cutting-edge minority just because of a high share. Meanwhile, lumping PID variants into plain PID is irresponsible; taken to the extreme, all control would become P control. In reality, many PID variants, when analyzed theoretically, have long departed from the PID framework (e.g., cascaded PID is essentially [backstepping](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%8D%E6%AD%A5%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity)). So, many misunderstandings stem from knowing too little and failing to grasp the essence of control.

In short, control science plays an irreplaceable and unique role, and it contains valuable, useful ideas.

## 2. The Four "Branches" of Control Theory

Life is short, and we do not have the energy to spend on a pile of mediocre courses. So how do we grasp the main veins of control? One way is to use mathematics as the backbone to string things together and spread outward.

![](/blog-assets/zhihu/661225526/image-02.jpg)

The mathematical skeleton of pan-control theory

I believe the mathematical threads relevant to engineering can be roughly organized as above. Of course, control actually uses more mathematics than this, but we should take mathematics as the backbone, grasp the main line, and thus discard some side branches. After studying the recommended materials, we will discover the power of holding the main line—overwhelming force over skill.

The selection criteria for recommended materials are: **the content should be as systematic and comprehensive as possible, get straight to the essence, and avoid compromises made for the sake of course syllabi or low quality due to the author's limitations.** This may sacrifice some readability, but if your mathematical foundation is decent, and with the help of online resources and large language models, you can certainly go for better materials.

### 1. The "Frequency-Domain Line" (yellow)

This line consists of calculus & linear algebra/matrix theory → classical calculus of variations → (linear) functional analysis → integral transforms. Before [state space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) was introduced into control science by Kalman, all human progress in control was concentrated along this line. On this line, we focus on the input–output signals themselves; a signal can be viewed as a function in the time domain. Looking back with hindsight, the key idea of this line is that signals should be studied within the entire space of signals.

Once this line is established, it can immediately sweep away a large array of so-called courses—circuits, [signals and systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BF%A1%E5%8F%B7%E4%B8%8E%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity), digital signal processing, digital image processing, automatic control principles, stochastic processes, [time series analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E5%BA%8F%E5%88%97%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90&zhida_source=entity), classical filtering (e.g., Wiener filtering), and a host of theories with terms like "frequency domain" or "[convolution](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%B7%E7%A7%AF&zhida_source=entity)", as well as special function theories such as [spherical harmonics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%90%83%E8%B0%90%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity). In truth, these are all about [harmonic analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B0%83%E5%92%8C%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90&zhida_source=entity) or its analogues on some group, or directly related to it. Whether it's Fourier series (FS), the Fourier transform (FT), the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), the discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT), the fast Fourier transform (FFT), or the [Laplace transform](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Laplace%E5%8F%98%E6%8D%A2&zhida_source=entity) (LP), in the end it all comes down to harmonic analysis on $\mathbb{R}$, on $\mathbb{Z}$, or on $\mathbb{S}^{1}$—or what is called [spectral analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B0%B1%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90&zhida_source=entity). For instance, automatic control studies why the spectrum of [unbounded self-adjoint operators](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%A0%E7%95%8C%E8%87%AA%E4%BC%B4%E7%AE%97%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity) always lies in the left half-plane; stochastic processes add a [probability space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A6%82%E7%8E%87%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) on top but still study the spectrum of operators. Then add some computational and engineering tricks.

In short, this line is relatively old and, without delving into deeper frequency-domain theory, does not have that much substance. Although the above may look intimidating, if we ignore technical details, all we really need is a "spatial awareness." If, on top of calculus and linear algebra, you can view functions as infinite-dimensional vectors in some [linear space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity), and then apply some [linear algebra](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) ideas, you will find that much knowledge emerges naturally. For this, I recommend **Professor Zhang Hao's courses: Modern Digital Signal Processing, Stochastic Processes**, and then quickly skim textbooks such as Signals and Systems or Digital Signal Processing from those courses. Line up the various Fourier transforms I listed, and you will see they all look the same. Going deeper usually means understanding [function spaces](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity), realizing that those formulas all look alike because they are all [linear expansions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E5%B1%95%E5%BC%80&zhida_source=entity), and then using calculus techniques to work through classical [calculus of variations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%98%E5%88%86%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity) problems to get a feel for it.

In my view, what truly matters for control science along this line lies in the field of [robust control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%B2%81%E6%A3%92%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity). Only when you get there will you experience what modern frequency-domain control really is.

[https://www.zhihu.com/question/12246502556/answer/2005237965799051828?share_code=UDGBFjdVY5Jq&utm_psn=2005246317354583195](https://www.zhihu.com/question/12246502556/answer/2005237965799051828?share_code=UDGBFjdVY5Jq&utm_psn=2005246317354583195)

The post above suggests starting with Astrom and Murray's **"Feedback Systems: An Introduction for Scientists and Engineers"**, and when reaching the last two chapters, simultaneously reading Skogestad's **"Multivariable Feedback Control: Analysis and Design"**. While working through Skogestad, one can also begin to try more theoretical books, such as Chi-Tsong Chen's **"Linear System Theory and Design"** and Kemin Zhou's **"Essentials of Robust Control"** (Professor Zhou's other books are also fine, and you can refer to Bilibili videos). If you can understand all these books, you will know why separating classical and modern control theory in your studies is not a good approach.

Of course, to save time, you do not need to read all the books above. The first one, for example, is very introductory and can be skimmed quickly. The later ones can be read selectively; the key point is that afterward you can move to the third line to study [nonlinear robust control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E9%B2%81%E6%A3%92%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity).

But be sure not to use domestic textbooks on automatic control principles or similar works, and use Zheng Dazhong's book cautiously as a text for multivariable frequency-domain theory. The former excises much frequency-domain content while fussing over trivial details with a kind of "artisan spirit" (even a human root-locus machine cannot beat a few lines of MATLAB code), while the latter is close to force-feeding and unsuitable as a self-study text. On the contrary, some well-known foreign textbooks occupying the same niche as domestic automatic control texts integrate classical frequency-domain control with robust control much better. Treat those domestic "classic" texts as final-exam and grad-school-exam review manuals instead.

### 2. The "Optimal Control Track" (Blue)

This track consists of Calculus & Linear Algebra/Matrix Theory – Computational Mathematics & Optimization & Ordinary Differential Equations – (Nonlinear Control) – Optimal Control and its Numerical Methods. It effectively originates from the emergence of optimal control in the 1960s, when Pontryagin's Maximum Principle (PMP), Bellman's Dynamic Programming (DP) principle, and Kalman filtering formed the three pillars of early optimal control. The numerical solution methods for optimal control also flourished alongside this, with Model Predictive Control (MPC) leveraging the receding-horizon solution of optimal control to construct implicit closed-loop predictive control. These directions have progressed together with the continuous development of computational mathematics since World War II, right up to the present day.

This track is arguably the most active one today — because optimal control has had a tremendous impact and demonstrated immense potential over the past few decades, spanning robotics, autonomous driving, aerospace, navigation and guidance, industrial processes, and more. In my column, I have summarized the theory of decision planning, which is essentially a complete set of theories for the approximate solution of the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation in optimal control.

In the direction of **numerical solution**, you'll find that computational mathematics topics such as numerical linear algebra, numerical integration, numerical methods for differential equations, and optimization theory lay the groundwork for subsequent numerical optimal control. So-called indirect methods originate from PMP and related boundary value problems; the DP framework stems from Bellman's principle and its various approximations; and direct methods come from formulating the problem directly as an optimization problem. Once you've worked through a series of numerical optimal control methods, you'll again discover that decision planning is also engaged in approximate and numerical solution. It's also clear that the currently popular reinforcement learning can roughly be regarded as a special case within the DP framework of optimal control — one that simplifies the value space and policy space in certain ways.

Going further into the **feedback stability** introduced by the receding horizon, we can uncover the subtle relationship between value functions and Lyapunov functions, and thereby appreciate the unique advantages of (optimal) predictive control over feedback control. Furthermore, one can realize that all solution approaches such as HJB or PMP revolve entirely around globally or locally solving the value function or some surrogate thereof. In essence, the problem thus returns to system stability, which connects to Track "3". A central aspect of this area currently involves passivity-based methods for system design and the theory of interconnected systems, which bring a methodology for system decomposition and provide a reference for the scientific hierarchical layering of decision, planning, and control.

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1922973164197122321?share_code=4PujEM8AgdPf&utm_psn=1982926940911444188](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1922973164197122321?share_code=4PujEM8AgdPf&utm_psn=1982926940911444188)

For this track, first, regarding DP and adaptive dynamic programming (reinforcement learning), I recommend **Bertsekas's *Reinforcement Learning and Optimal Control* and *Dynamic Programming and Optimal Control***. The former is a condensed version of the latter (though equally complete in structure). You can read the former to grasp the main thread and refer to the latter as needed; if you have the time or can follow the main thread on your own, you can also tackle the brick directly. There are also explanatory videos on Bilibili. This book systematically explains the theory of approximate HJB equation solving, with threads woven throughout and a pedagogical style that is well suited for self-study. Its only two shortcomings are, first, a lack of technical depth in numerical methods — it approaches things more from an architectural standpoint, and thus doesn't form a more systematic theory of approximate solution; and second, it only serves as an introductory book to reinforcement learning — more advanced topics such as occupation measures and passivity-based system decomposition are not covered.

### 3. The "Nonlinear Control Track" (Green)

This track consists of Calculus & Linear Algebra/Matrix Theory – Ordinary Differential Equations and Qualitative Theory – Nonlinear Control.

It's worth noting that the modern control theory (linear systems theory) taught at the undergraduate (and graduate) level can be fully subsumed into this track. In fact, there is no need to study linear theory in detail beforehand; it is better to interleave it with nonlinear material. The core of linear theory is controllability/observability and their decompositions, which directly lead to the essential concepts in control and provide the geometric meaning of systems evolving within controllable (observable) subspaces.

Regarding **system analysis** along this track, you don't need to study ODEs and qualitative theory exhaustively — the classic nonlinear control texts generally review all the necessary basics. In nonlinear control theory, both analysis and design theories are rich. I recommend the following "bible"-level books: **Slotine's *Applied Nonlinear Control* or Khalil's *Nonlinear Systems***. Both are foundational nonlinear control textbooks. I haven't read the former myself, but it's safe to say it is somewhat simpler than the latter; the latter is very systematic, with emphasis on the analysis component. Either one can serve as an entry point for system analysis.

For **system design**, feedback linearization and passivity are two core nonlinear control design philosophies (others like Jacobian linearization and gain scheduling are more naive, integral adaptation and Lyapunov redesign are comparatively just technical points, and sliding mode & high-gain approaches border on pseudo-concepts). The former, however, is somewhat criticized because it relies on canceling nonlinear terms (which can suffer from inaccuracies in model-based cancellation, difficulties under input constraints, and the risk of canceling stabilizing nonlinearities). ***Constructive Nonlinear Control*** systematically presents the theory of nonlinear design, emphasizing the structure induced by passivity and how it aids in constructing Lyapunov functions (well worth studying — its value far exceeds that of feedback linearization). Another book, ***L2-Gain and Passivity Techniques in Nonlinear Control***, also systematically expounds this branch. ***Nonlinear System Design: Differential Geometry, Adaptive and Robust Control***, which was translated with the participation of a former president of Harbin Engineering University, is of good quality and systematically presents results on output and state feedback linearization. Both are design-oriented textbooks, originally published in the 1990s — the golden era of nonlinear control development. Finally, there is **Isidori's *Nonlinear Control Systems***, a classic in geometric control, which represents the pinnacle of feedback linearization on complex manifolds. This book focuses on controllability and observability of systems on manifolds, a full set of feedback linearization theory, and corresponding results on (semi)global stabilization and robust stabilization.

### 4. The "Stochastic/Distribution Track" (Orange)

This track consists of Probability Theory – Bayesian Statistics – Statistical Inference/Computational Statistics – Probabilistic Graphical Models, with some calculus, linear algebra, and optimization along the way. The core of this track, however, is probability. The main idea is to model systems via Bayesian methods as probabilistic graphical models, and then perform inference on the graph. It covers traditional statistical inference, optimal estimation (including least squares, minimum variance, Kalman filtering, sigma-point Kalman filtering, particle filtering, Bayesian filtering, and other filtering methods), SLAM, and state observation problems.

Broadly speaking, inference is a problem of computing probability distributions. Exact inference involves variable elimination (VE), belief propagation (BP), and the junction tree algorithm — three levels of increasing sophistication. BP, also known as the sum-product algorithm, grows out of VE by incorporating message-passing ideas and applies to graphical models with tree structure. The junction tree algorithm, in turn, extends BP beyond ordinary tree structures. For approximate inference, there are many algorithms spanning deterministic and stochastic approximations. Variational inference (VI) is the representative deterministic approximation, while stochastic approximations are sampling-based, including importance sampling and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). For approximate inference on probabilistic graphical models, the loop belief propagation algorithm, derived from BP, handles graphs with cycles.

In practice, we often make assumptions such as Gaussian assumptions and independence between observations and states, which reduce the underlying Bayesian network problem to nonlinear least squares / graph optimization or factor graphs. On this part, I'd suggest referring to this article — I must say the author's writing style is quite similar to mine.

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/661895443](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/661895443)

Most of the above concerns observation or estimation problems: our goal is clear — to reconstruct the observed quantities from historical information. The dual control problem, by contrast, determines the current control input based on desired future performance. The real difficulty therefore lies, on the one hand, in connecting the graphical model with the desired characteristics, and on the other hand, in the fact that control requires forward rollout of policies, which confronts robustness issues caused by inaccurate environment predictions.

On this topic, there is a bible-level book, ***Graphical Models, Exponential Families, and Variational Inference***, as well as a paper titled ***An Optimization Centric View on Bayes Rule: Reviewing and Generalizing Variational Inference***. For filtering theory, there is ***Principles of Nonlinear Filtering Theory***. For the geometry of inference, you can consult books on information geometry — though Amari's book is somewhat hand-waving and feels less rigorous. You can also refer to the **references in the article linked above**, though in my view, this area lacks materials that systematically tie everything together.

## 3. Interrelations and Two Major Controversies

Having proposed several baselines, we now examine their interconnections and divergences. This leads to the following discussion: studying the relationships and disagreements among them provides a firm grasp of history while yielding new insights through contradictions. After all, contradiction is the driving force behind the development of all things.

### (1) Interrelations

First, we address the methodological tools. In Line 1, one can design based on simple [time-domain response](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E5%9F%9F%E5%93%8D%E5%BA%94&zhida_source=entity), typically via dominant-pole approximation for model reduction; frequency-domain analysis and design (loop shaping, H∞ control, μ-synthesis); the system [H2 norm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=H2%E8%8C%83%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) theory, which is directly related to [power spectral density](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%9F%E7%8E%87%E8%B0%B1%E5%AF%86%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity), while the optimization of the feedback system's H2 norm can be solved via LMIs. Nonlinear systems can likewise be analyzed for L2 gains with respect to inputs at different frequencies. In Lines 2 and 3, we essentially rely on the design of Lyapunov functions to achieve stability and other desired performance properties. In optimal control (Line 2), the cost-to-go is fundamentally an approximation of a Lyapunov function; by employing MPC with multi-step look-ahead and policy rollouts, we relax the accuracy requirements on the cost-to-go approximation and use better optimizers to construct Lyapunov functions "implicitly." In feedback control (Line 3), we have methods such as sliding mode, high-gain backstepping, forwarding, and passivity-based approaches for "explicit" design. In Line 4, the primary tools are variational and sampling techniques from approximate probabilistic inference, corresponding respectively to variational inference (VI), Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), and hybrid approaches such as [SVGD](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=SVGD&zhida_source=entity) and Wasserstein gradient flows. The variational (optimization) and sampling paradigms are gradually converging.

For complex non-convex, nonlinear problems, Line 1 is largely incapable of handling them due to its restriction to LTI systems. As for Line 2, the difficulty stems from the nonlinearity of [system dynamics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) and cost functions; since Line 2 primarily studies open-loop solutions, it is easier than Line 3 when dealing with nonlinearities. On the one hand, shooting/collocation methods can fully handle nonlinear systems, and we can bring in more powerful non-convex optimization tools. For Line 3, the toolbox is even richer: state/input-output feedback linearization is arguably the most prominent approach. We can also confront the nonlinearity of the underlying manifold directly—for instance, in pose estimation problems, we design Lyapunov functions tied to Riemannian metrics. As for Line 4, the problem is generally equivalent to sampling from [multimodal](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%9A%E6%A8%A1%E6%80%81&zhida_source=entity) (non-log-concave) distributions or performing inference on multimodal distributions, typically addressed via mixture distributions (e.g., the basic [GMM](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=GMM&zhida_source=entity)) and more powerful sampling/non-convex optimization algorithms such as annealing and tempering techniques.

### (2) The Dispute between Lines 3 and 4

Interestingly, especially in the field of [state estimation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1&zhida_source=entity)/observation, nonlinear control and probabilistic inference diverge considerably. On one hand, the theory of various [state observers](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E8%A7%82%E6%B5%8B%E5%99%A8&zhida_source=entity) is remarkably rich; on the other hand, new estimators capable of handling complex distributions continue to emerge. However, as noted above, the former approach proceeds by analyzing nonlinearities through expansion while suppressing noise or disturbances—relatively elegant. The latter attempts to overcome nonlinearity through generic optimization and sampling while modeling noise as an intrinsic part of the model—comparatively brute-force.

The reason this divergence is most pronounced in state estimation/observation is historical. Early state estimation was applied to radar tracking and navigation, where random noise in the data was (as they perceived it) the primary challenge, giving rise to a series of filtering theories centered on probability and statistics. In contrast, observers in the control community served mostly deterministic electromechanical systems—particularly in [motion control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%90%E5%8A%A8%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity) and motor-related fields—where the central issue was system observability. Technologically, control seeks an executable control law; it is a forward problem with performance objectives and other goals. Estimation and observation, by contrast, are inverse problems: describing a plausible cause from outputs/observations, which naturally admits multiple inferential frameworks. Philosophically, observation/estimation is about understanding the world, while control is about transforming it. The former is fundamentally epistemic, the latter decisional—so it is hardly surprising that estimation frameworks more readily diverge into different epistemic perspectives.

Looking back, in 1960 Kalman published *A New Approach to Linear Filtering and Prediction Problems*, introducing the Kalman filter via [minimum mean-square error](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E5%B0%8F%E5%9D%87%E6%96%B9%E8%AF%AF%E5%B7%AE&zhida_source=entity) and orthogonal projection methods, without yet relying heavily on stochasticity or a Bayesian perspective. Then in 1964, [Yu-Chi Ho](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%95%E6%AF%93%E7%90%A6&zhida_source=entity) published *A Bayesian Approach to Problems in Stochastic Estimation and Control*, formally integrating the Bayesian framework with the Kalman filter. From there, the two lines—3 and 4—gradually diverged.

### (3) The Dispute between Reinforcement Learning and Optimal Control in Line 2

Within Optimal Control Line 2, an important subfield is dynamic programming, pursued by two major camps: on the control science side, Werbos followed by Bertsekas; on the computer science side, Sutton and [Barto](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Barto&zhida_source=entity). The former developed adaptive dynamic programming (earlier also called "heuristic dynamic programming" or "[approximate dynamic programming](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%91%E4%BC%BC%E5%8A%A8%E6%80%81%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity)" or "neuro-dynamic programming"), while the latter developed reinforcement learning. Unlike the dispute between Lines 3 and 4—which concerns different methodologies and techniques for the same problem—the contest within dynamic programming is far more theatrical: the same problem, the same framework, and the same techniques, but different groups coined different terminologies and then competed for [discursive authority](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%AF%9D%E8%AF%AD%E6%9D%83&zhida_source=entity).

Historically, trial-and-error learning can be traced to the 1850s, with research on electromechanical systems or computational models along similar lines continuing intermittently into the 1950s. During this period, no substantial research program formed; many results remained confined to psychology and education. It was not until 1957 that Bellman, by extending the 19th-century Hamilton–Jacobi theory, developed the Bellman optimality principle, proposed the Bellman equation (value iteration), introduced the discrete stochastic Markov decision process (MDP), and articulated the concept of the "curse of dimensionality" (which set the stage for dynamic programming's perpetual struggle with this issue). Ronald Howard then devised policy iteration in the 1960s, and the foundation of this branch was firmly laid.

During the 1960s and 1970s, genuine trial-and-error learning became rare, largely because many scholars found it difficult to distinguish among different types of learning. On the control science side, however, Werbos introduced heuristic dynamic programming, pioneering the use of function approximation to combat the curse of dimensionality. Bertsekas consolidated a body of results and published *Dynamic Programming and Optimal Control*, which became a classic in the field.

In the 1980s, Sutton and Barto, building on the animal-learning principle that "learning rules are driven by temporally successive prediction changes," developed a classical psychological model of adjustment based on temporal-difference (TD) learning. They subsequently developed a method combining TD learning with trial-and-error learning, termed the actor–critic architecture. This computer science camp initially arose from animal learning theory. The connection between temporal-difference learning and optimal control was fully integrated in 1989 with [Q-learning](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Q-learning&zhida_source=entity): Chris Watkins introduced the Q-learning algorithm in his doctoral thesis, later shown to be equivalent to action-dependent heuristic dynamic programming. This gave "animal learning theory" its theoretical foundation, laid decades earlier by Bellman. Meanwhile, in 1989 Bertsekas published *Adaptive Aggregation Methods for Infinite Horizon Dynamic Programming*, exploring aggregation approximation methods. Overall, the control science camp maintained stronger theoretical rigor than its computer science counterpart. **Thus we see how valuable it is for a research branch to possess not only mathematics and formulas, but also accessible, appealing behavioral, educational, and psychological narratives (laughs).**

In 1996, Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis co-authored *Neuro-Dynamic Programming*, the first systematic account of using [neural network](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A5%9E%E7%BB%8F%E7%BD%91%E7%BB%9C&zhida_source=entity) and other approximation architectures for high-dimensional dynamic programming. Then in 1998, Sutton and Barto published their monograph *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction*, which became the canonical text on the reinforcement learning side. This also consolidated the terminology of the computer science camp, and reinforcement learning formally entered the historical stage. In 2005, Bertsekas published a survey interpreting MPC from the perspective of adaptive dynamic programming. In 2006, at a workshop organized by the [National Science Foundation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BE%8E%E5%9B%BD%E7%A7%91%E5%AD%A6%E5%9F%BA%E9%87%91%E4%BC%9A&zhida_source=entity), the control science branch was (only then) uniformly named "adaptive dynamic programming." **This again illustrates how important it is to establish a unified and catchy name from the very beginning (laughs).**

Moving to the 2010s, with the success of [convolutional neural networks](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%B7%E7%A7%AF%E7%A5%9E%E7%BB%8F%E7%BD%91%E7%BB%9C&zhida_source=entity) in computer vision, Volodymyr Mnih et al. introduced the Deep Q-Network (DQN) in a 2013 paper, applying deep learning to reinforcement learning problems and marking the birth of "deep reinforcement learning" (although the control science camp had long since developed neural-network-based approximation methods). Subsequently, the success of AlphaGo stunned the world, employing [deep neural networks](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B7%B1%E5%BA%A6%E7%A5%9E%E7%BB%8F%E7%BD%91%E7%BB%9C&zhida_source=entity) combined with Monte Carlo tree search for policy rollouts and decision-making. What followed is well known: reinforcement learning from the computer science side gradually claimed greater discursive dominance. New algorithms proliferated and gained widespread fame, while the stability and [robustness analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=235067521&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%B2%81%E6%A3%92%E6%80%A7%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90&zhida_source=entity) from control theory were cast aside. Meanwhile, the other camp gradually waned; despite maintaining theoretical rigor (even performing bound analysis and robustness analysis when using neural network approximations), it produced no further major milestones and instead devolved into a vehicle for paper-farming (though reinforcement learning itself later became an equally notorious paper-farming ground).
