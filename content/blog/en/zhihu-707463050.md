Robot planning is an important research area in robotics and a key intersection of artificial intelligence, [robotics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E6%9C%BA%E5%99%A8%E4%BA%BA%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity), and control theory. The term "planning" generally refers to devising a sequence of actions before acting, and the fundamental task of robot planning is: **to automatically generate, within a specified time and workspace, a [control program](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%A8%8B%E5%BA%8F&zhida_source=entity) consisting of action sequences, motion paths, and trajectories that take the robot from its initial operational state to a goal state**. Additionally, supervising and adjusting the actual execution of a known plan is also an inherent function of robot planning.

## 1. Hierarchical Decomposition [\[1\]](#ref_1) [\[2\]](#ref_2) [\[3\]](#ref_3) [\[4\]](#ref_4)

Abstraction and hierarchical decomposition represent the fundamental and mainstream approach humans use to solve complex problems—deconstruction.

### (1) High-Level Planning and Low-Level Planning

Robot planning generally comprises two levels of subproblems: high-level planning and low-level planning. High-level planning, i.e., decision-making, also known as intelligent planning or [task planning](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%BB%E5%8A%A1%E8%A7%84%E5%88%92&zhida_source=entity), is discrete in nature. Low-level planning, also called execution-level planning, is continuous and is what is commonly meant by planning in the narrow sense.

One additional remark here: the reason decision-making and planning are split into two layers is primarily the complexity of the problem. We often decompose the entire problem into a decision problem at the behavioral level and a planning problem at the trajectory level. Moreover, decision-making is not a category of [algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) but rather a type of problem. Planning algorithms, in turn, are the scientific theory for solving decision and planning problems. Additionally, other approaches can be used for decision-making, such as rule-based methods or learning-based methods (for a discussion of these, see "Section 2: Based on What" below).

**1. Task Planning (Decision-Making)**

Given a task command, it automatically generates the robot execution program to accomplish the task, with the goal of producing an action sequence. For instance, if a task is understood as a change in the workspace state, then **decision-making** generates the [operation sequence](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%93%8D%E4%BD%9C%E5%BA%8F%E5%88%97&zhida_source=entity) that transforms the initial state step by step into the goal state. Years ago, many mobile robot decision-making systems used [finite state machines](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%89%E9%99%90%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E6%9C%BA&zhida_source=entity), and the development trend has been to couple decision-making with planning—that is, to account for robot motion factors during decision-making. It's easy to see that when making decisions, we need to know the cost of each [action sequence](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=3&q=%E5%8A%A8%E4%BD%9C%E5%BA%8F%E5%88%97&zhida_source=entity), which is closely tied to whether and how the action sequence can be realized. This actually highlights the significance of tight coupling between decision-making and planning: if the two are decoupled, the action sequence produced by decision-making may lack feasibility at the lower planning level, leading to overly conservative robot behavior or even accidents.

**2. Low-Level Planning**

This invokes the workspace model and robot model information. First, it converts the decision result into a collision-free robot motion path, known as **path planning** or path search (though a minority of scholars consider path planning part of high-level planning); then, based on the path, it optimizes the positions, velocities, accelerations, etc., of the path points to improve robot motion performance and task execution efficiency, yielding a motion trajectory—this is called **trajectory planning**.

### (2) Various Types of Planning

We classify path planning under low-level planning and attempt to refine the planning hierarchy as much as possible. The following breaks this down from top to bottom.

![Handwritten note](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-841794ee80e76d8fc42d31b21424a363_r.jpg)

1. **Route Planning**

This is the highest-level planning and is only meaningful for mobile robots, especially autonomous driving in large-scale environments; it is also the coarsest-grained planning. Given the locations of the start and destination, it returns a path. This layer corresponds to what navigation apps do in our daily lives and can be solved with [shortest path](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E7%9F%AD%E8%B7%AF%E5%BE%84&zhida_source=entity) or shortest-time graph [search algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%90%9C%E7%B4%A2%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity). There are essentially no open problems left at this level.

2. **Behavior Planning / Behavior Decision-Making**

This belongs to high-level planning and sometimes refers to only a part of decision-making. It generates behaviors (or action sequences) based on the surrounding environment and the robot's own state. Since the goal is to plan behaviors rather than paths or trajectories, behaviors must be defined or abstracted from the trajectory [state space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity), and planning then takes place in a "behavior space." Naturally, such planning is discrete (because behaviors should form a [finite set](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%89%E9%99%90%E9%9B%86&zhida_source=entity) or a countable set, and they should also be complete; otherwise, we will perpetually face long-tail problems). In practice, it is in [dynamic environments](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E6%80%81%E7%8E%AF%E5%A2%83&zhida_source=entity) that planning places significant demands on decision-making or behavior planning. Because the optimal behavior varies over time in a time-varying environment, poorly executed behavior planning can lead to inconsistent trajectories, loss of [optimality](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity), or even a poor-quality solution.

3. **Path Planning**

Also known as path search, this belongs to low-level planning. Based on the results of behavior planning, it refines the granularity of route planning (though route planning can also be viewed as coarse-grained path planning without decision-making). It is typically performed in a relatively low-dimensional space and, as a front-end, is not expected to incur excessive computational cost.

Given the [environment model](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8E%AF%E5%A2%83%E6%A8%A1%E5%9E%8B&zhida_source=entity), the robot model, and the current and goal positions, it outputs a feasible (e.g., at minimum collision-free) [purely geometric](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%AF%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity) path. Here the current state (especially position) is known, while the goal state may not be known in advance (sometimes it must be determined during the planning process).

4. **Trajectory Planning**

Also a low-level planning task, it outputs time information at path points in addition to the path itself, yielding a [time sequence](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E5%BA%8F%E5%88%97&zhida_source=entity) that includes the path points. This result is called a trajectory. With time information, we naturally obtain the [differential](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86&zhida_source=entity) information of the path points (enabling notions of velocity and acceleration). It is typically performed in high dimensions using [optimization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) methods, hence it is also called trajectory optimization.

Apart from the additional time output, the inputs and outputs are the same as path planning. That is, given the environment and robot model, current and goal positions along with their higher-order derivatives, it outputs a trajectory with time information. Since the trajectory encodes derivatives of all orders of position, it must account for [robot dynamics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%BA%E5%99%A8%E4%BA%BA%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) constraints.

5. **Motion Planning**

Some sources consider motion planning to be the collective term for path planning and trajectory planning, but a mainstream view is that motion planning combines trajectory planning with downstream [trajectory tracking control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E8%BD%A8%E8%BF%B9%E8%B7%9F%E8%B8%AA%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity). Trajectory planning and trajectory tracking control can be treated separately, or they can be combined into a single [constrained optimization problem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%A6%E6%9D%9F%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity). This also shows that planning is upstream of control, and the two are closely related.

Similarly, the inputs are the environment and robot model, current and goal positions along with their higher-order derivatives, and the output is the system control input (given). Again, the goal state is sometimes unknown, requiring real-time planning.

It is clear that our most fundamental requirements are safety (collision avoidance), smoothness (energy savings, higher stability and smoothness, reduced mechanical burden), and dynamic feasibility (physically executable and controllable).

It is worth noting that, although we have provided a detailed hierarchical decomposition of the general planning problem above, the layers must be organically integrated and interact with each other. Otherwise, we encounter the issues of detached decision-making and planning mentioned earlier, or problems with planning completeness [\[6\]](#ref_6).

For example, coupling decision-making with (low-level) planning has given rise to task and motion planning ([TAMP](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=TAMP&zhida_source=entity)) algorithms. The difficulty of these algorithms lies in reconciling the differences between high-level discrete planning and low-level continuous planning. When decision-making and planning are integrated holistically and the algorithm performance is sufficient in theory, TAMP enables stronger task execution capabilities.

As another example, there are also many kinodynamic path planning methods that begin to account for [system dynamics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) constraints during path planning. On the one hand, this relieves the pressure of trajectory optimization at the lower level; on the other hand, an "optimal" path that ignores all dynamics may be unreasonable for the system. Paths that satisfy and fail to satisfy dynamic constraints may belong to different homotopy classes, and it is nearly impossible for lower-level optimization to bridge across distinct homotopy classes.

In summary, planning is a coarse-to-fine process. The functions assigned to the upper and lower layers should neither be rigid nor static, and the two layers should not be completely decoupled from each other.

## 2. Based on What[\[7\]](#ref_7)

### (1) Principle-based

A [deductive system](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%BC%94%E7%BB%8E%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) built on assumed axioms. The fundamental assumptions of different environments are not static; they require data to learn and verify. Principles serve as the criterion for judging quality. A balance must be struck: the system should have strong expressive power (e.g., Newton's laws of motion for low-speed macroscopic motion); otherwise it is a trivial system of no use and will be discarded. Yet assumptions are still necessary—there is no "[theory of everything](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%87%E6%9C%89%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity)." Evidence for this is Gödel's incompleteness theorem: no universal [axiom system](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%AC%E7%90%86%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) exists.

### (2) Model-based

As long as a model exists, one simply operates within its allowed scope; the meaning is quite vague. The model can be any [mathematical-physical](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%95%B0%E5%AD%A6%E7%89%A9%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity) model, a [statistical](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BB%9F%E8%AE%A1%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) model, or some model from another discipline. This is essentially the concept of a "model system" in the [philosophy of science](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A7%91%E5%AD%A6%E5%93%B2%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity).

### (3) Rule-based

A rule is a class of rules proposed from intuition and experience. Unlike axiom-based deductive systems, rule-based methods commonly include empirical law systems and hypothetico-deductive systems. Rule-based approaches can be grounded in data, experience, or proposed hypotheses; they use a mapping defined by humans that takes problem data as input and outputs a solution.

The difference from principle-based methods is that rule-based approaches do not rely heavily on reasoning through an axiom-deductive system to arrive at solutions. Also, rules typically have limited generalization and require case-by-case patching; sometimes, due to biases in data or experience, they even produce erroneous deductions.

For example, Aristotle's claims that "the Earth is the center of the universe, spherical in shape, and composed of four elements—earth, water, air, and fire; the heavens are made of a [fifth element](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%AC%AC%E4%BA%94%E5%85%83%E7%B4%A0&zhida_source=entity), 'aether'" and that "motion requires a mover, ultimately traceable to the 'prime mover'" are hypotheses. His theory of falling bodies held that heavier objects fall faster in proportion to their weight—a rule he proposed based on certain data and experience, later refuted by Galileo's experiments. Kepler's three laws of planetary motion are likewise rules summarized from astronomical observations by Tycho and others—objective descriptions of planetary motion but with limited applicability.

However, this term has long been misused by some as a rhetorical tool to criticize any method that does not use learning-based approaches. They criticize everything derived from human intelligence as "rule-based," claiming that only what is learned from data is not a rule. But as discussed earlier, axiom-deductive systems and [logic](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%80%BB%E8%BE%91%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) are the [moat](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8A%A4%E5%9F%8E%E6%B2%B3&zhida_source=entity) of human intelligence; so-called rules are nothing more than heuristic mappings.

### (4) Learning-based / Data-based

Neither principle-based nor rule-based approaches are mutually exclusive with learning-based/data-based methods. In both principle-based and rule-based frameworks, data is used to learn or validate the principles or rules. Rules can also be optimized through data.

For instance, Newton drew extensively on prior astronomical data from Tycho and Kepler's three laws. Similarly, deductive systems in [aerodynamics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A9%BA%E6%B0%94%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity), mechanical structures, thermodynamics, etc., rely heavily on empirical parameters that must be identified or calibrated before use.

In motion planning modeling, take autonomous driving as an example. A principle-based approach must articulate principles such as human preferences regarding safety, efficiency, risk, traffic rules and liability allocation, social norms, their relative importance, and a reasonable characterization of how other agents react to one's own behavior. Modeling all of these requires data-driven learning—either through analysis and summarization by R&D personnel followed by [data validation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%95%B0%E6%8D%AE%E9%AA%8C%E8%AF%81&zhida_source=entity), or through machine learning methods.

In practical full-stack agent development, current learning-based methods are primarily used for modeling and perception. The perception side must associate all environmental information with certain risks or preferences, while also attending to generalization, correctness, and cost. As for perception itself, the underlying mechanism is not yet fully understood by researchers.

![](/blog-assets/zhihu/707463050/image-04.jpg)

In summary, principle-based approaches constitute a highly technical part of model-based methods, since they start from self-evident axioms, and the system has sufficient strength with results that follow systematically. Compared with rule-based and purely learning-based methods, principle-based approaches typically offer much stronger [generalization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B3%9B%E5%8C%96%E8%83%BD%E5%8A%9B&zhida_source=entity) and lower [development cost](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BC%80%E5%8F%91%E6%88%90%E6%9C%AC&zhida_source=entity).

For a navigation-type task like autonomous driving, the decision and planning subtask—driving strategy and trajectory—can currently be fully addressed using principles built on domain knowledge and data, through systematic [mathematical logic](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%95%B0%E7%90%86%E9%80%BB%E8%BE%91&zhida_source=entity) reasoning and optimization to determine one's own behavior. The entire process is rational and interpretable, and thus enjoys much stronger generalization and lower development cost. However, some tasks cannot be solved by principle-based methods alone for various other reasons. The underlying causes are numerous and will be discussed later.

## 3. Basic Performance Metrics[\[8\]](#ref_8)

These performance metrics apply to general algorithms, especially those involved in global optimization (MILP, MINLP, CP) and global search algorithms.

### (1) Algorithm Completeness

1.  **Complete**: For any input, given infinite computation time and a solution that exists, such methods are guaranteed to find the global optimum; or, within finite time, determine whether a solution exists, and if so, find a global suboptimal solution within a [given error tolerance](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BB%99%E5%AE%9A%E8%AF%AF%E5%B7%AE&zhida_source=entity). Incomplete algorithms rely solely on [heuristic methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%90%AF%E5%8F%91%E5%BC%8F%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) to search for the global optimum, with no theoretical guarantee regarding whether the search may get stuck near a [local minimum](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B1%80%E9%83%A8%E6%9C%80%E5%B0%8F%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity).
2.  **Asymptotically Complete**: Given infinite computation time, these methods are guaranteed to find the global optimum, or the probability of finding it is 1. However, such methods cannot determine whether the currently found solution is globally optimal.
3.  **Resolution Complete**: Deterministic methods capable of dense sampling/partitioning. Density means that as [sample points](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%87%87%E6%A0%B7%E7%82%B9&zhida_source=entity) or partitions tend to infinity, any shape can be approximated—i.e., the closure of the sample set is the sampling space (in robot planning, approximating any [configuration space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%8D%E5%BD%A2%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity)). That is, if a solution exists, the algorithm will find it in finite time; if no solution exists, the algorithm may run forever. For grid-map-based planning algorithms, this means the resolution can be arbitrarily increased.
4.  **Probabilistically Complete**: The algorithm is based on random sampling and is dense with probability 1—i.e., given enough samples, the probability of finding a solution is 1.
5.  **Exact Algorithm**: Can find a global optimum satisfying a given error tolerance. However, for near-degenerate problems, these solutions may not satisfy the given tolerance.

Therefore, to guarantee finding a global optimum within a given error bound in finite time, you need complete or exact methods. Pure heuristics cannot achieve this, and no chaotic mechanism can guarantee it either. If you merely want a suboptimal solution, many options exist.

### (2) Feasibility and Optimality

A **feasible solution** only needs to satisfy all requirements of a problem—e.g., in planning, a plan that reaches the goal state (typically satisfying the most basic collision-free requirement); we do not care about its efficiency or other properties. An **optimal solution** corresponds to an optimization problem, which includes a performance metric of interest. The optimal solution must not only reach the goal state but also satisfy some optimal performance criterion.

Analogous statements apply to algorithms, where the performance metrics are typically execution speed, [algorithmic complexity](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95%E5%A4%8D%E6%9D%82%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity), etc.

### (3) Systematieness of Search Algorithms

**Systematieness** means the algorithm visits every reachable state and returns, in finite time, whether a solution exists. For finite search spaces, systematieness implies the algorithm must mark visited states to avoid redundant search; otherwise, the search may loop indefinitely among certain states. For infinite search spaces, the definition is weaker and equivalent to resolution completeness: the algorithm may run forever when no solution exists, and systematieness means that as the number of searches tends to infinity, every reachable state is guaranteed to be visited.

Search spaces computable on a computer are at most countably infinite. From the definitions above, it is intuitively clear that in a finite search space, any algorithm (as long as it marks visited states) can be systematic, and systematic algorithms also exist for countably infinite search spaces.

### (4) Online vs. Offline: Real-Time Performance

In essence, any planning algorithm should be online, since a planning algorithm must consider future choices based on the current state. However, planning algorithms are also based on some [simulator](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%BF%E7%9C%9F%E5%99%A8&zhida_source=entity) model, and the simulator and the physical system can run at different times, allowing a distinction between offline and online planning. In short, offline and online form an amortized-optimization relationship—complementary to each other—and are fundamentally a difference in [time scales](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E5%B0%BA%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity).

[https://www.zhihu.com/question/524278760/answer/2418383931](https://www.zhihu.com/question/524278760/answer/2418383931)

Two factors influence the necessity and importance of offline processing: the static versus dynamic nature of the environment, and the specific form of the task.

For example, in a static environment (e.g., a maze), a path planner can search for an initial solution to be used by downstream algorithms. But for a large-scale environment, the algorithm may be computationally expensive. If some environmental information is known in advance, the search can be done offline. Even when the environment is only partially known, simple problems can be solved offline to obtain a feasible solution. However, in dynamic environments, offline results may be entirely inadequate for real-time changes, offering limited reference value online—or even causing severe behavioral inconsistency or inappropriate behavior.

## 4. Overview and Essence

### (1) Non-Convex Combinatorial Optimization[\[9\]](#ref_9)

Motion planning is fundamentally a non-convex combinatorial optimization problem—this is the source of its inherent difficulty. A simple example illustrates this. The shortest-path problem seeks a path for a point robot from a start position to a goal position without colliding with obstacles.

**Example (Non-convexity of motion planning):** Based on the problem description above, it can be formulated as an optimization problem. Let $x_{i}$ denote fixed discrete state points. An $L_{1}$ ball is used for collision avoidance.

$$
\begin{gather*} \min_{x_{0},...,x_{N} }\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left| \left| x_{n+1} -x_{n} \right|\right|_{a}^{b} \\s.t.\   x_{0} = x_{start} \\\hspace{2em}x_{N} = x_{goal} \\ \hspace{2em}\left| \left| x_{n}\right|\right|_{1} \geq 1  \tag{1}, \forall n \end{gather*}
$$

![](https://pica.zhimg.com/v2-07ef95a21b091e581ef089f970923bae_r.jpg)

In the left figure, the global optimum can be found; in the right figure, only a local optimum on the right side of the obstacle can be found. If the initial solution is on the right side, the optimization can only improve the solution on the right and cannot cross the obstacle to find the left-side solution. Crossing the obstacle violates the collision-free constraint: cost must increase before a lower-cost solution can be found. Hence the problem is non-convex. Mathematically, the exterior of the $L_{1}$ ball is also non-convex.

**Example (Combinatorial nature of motion planning):** Based on the problem description, a non-convex region can be decomposed into a union of convex subregions. By introducing **disjunctive constraints** and **indicator variables**, the problem can be formulated as a mixed-integer convex program (convex MINLP). Let $x_{i}$ denote fixed discrete state points.

$$
\begin{gather*}  \min_{x_{0},...,x_{N} }\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left| \left| x_{n+1} -x_{n} \right|\right|_{a}^{b} \\s.t.\   x_{0} = x_{start}  \\\hspace{2em}x_{N} = x_{goal}  \\ \hspace{2em}\left( 1,1 \right)^{T}x_{n} \geq 1 \text{ or } \leq -1 \\ \hspace{2em} \text{or } \left( 1,-1 \right)^{T}x_{n} \geq 1 \text{ or } \leq -1, \forall n  \end{gather*}  \tag{2}
$$

![](/blog-assets/zhihu/707463050/image-06.jpg)

Optimization problems with discrete variables are called combinatorial optimization problems. Unlike continuous optimization, which typically seeks a set of real numbers or a function, combinatorial problems seek an object from a (countably) infinite set—typically an integer, a set, a permutation, or a graph—often involving ordering, classification, and filtering. Classic combinatorial optimization problems include the traveling salesman problem, production scheduling, the 0-1 [knapsack problem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%83%8C%E5%8C%85%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity), bin packing, [graph coloring](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9B%BE%E7%9D%80%E8%89%B2%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity), clustering, and the maximum clique problem. These problems are simple to describe and have strong engineering significance. However, they are notoriously difficult to solve, sometimes exhibiting "[combinatorial explosion](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BB%84%E5%90%88%E7%88%86%E7%82%B8&zhida_source=entity)" that renders them intractable on existing computers.

### (II) [Computational Complexity Theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97%E5%A4%8D%E6%9D%82%E5%BA%A6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity)[\[10\]](#ref_10) [\[11\]](#ref_11)

Two recommended articles:

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/40140362?utm_psn=1842579221324636161](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/40140362?utm_psn=1842579221324636161)

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/420772986?utm_psn=1851593878307753984](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/420772986?utm_psn=1851593878307753984)

**1. The core issue is what computers can easily do versus what is nearly impossible**

How fundamentally hard is a given hard problem? This is a fundamental task for computer scientists, who aim to classify problems into so-called "complexity classes." These classes encompass all computational problems that require less than a certain amount of computational resources, such as time or memory.

Based on complexity classes, hard problems can be organized into the hierarchy below, where some classes contain all other hard problems, and certain hard problems demand additional computational resources.

![](https://pica.zhimg.com/v2-b70e8e7c41c1edf611c602b70e39b3b0_r.jpg)

Consider a simple example with the large number 123456789001 and two questions: Is this number prime (i.e., divisible only by 1 and itself)? Computer scientists can solve this using a fast algorithm—one that does not get bogged down as the number grows arbitrarily large. What are the prime factors of this number? Aside from using a [quantum computer](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%87%8F%E5%AD%90%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97%E6%9C%BA&zhida_source=entity), there is currently no fast algorithm for this.

From a computer scientist's perspective, these two questions belong to different complexity classes.

Many distinct complexity classes exist, but in most cases, researchers cannot yet prove that one class is fundamentally different from another. Proving these separations is among the hardest and most important open problems in the field. The differences between complexity classes can be subtle or stark, and maintaining the orthodoxy of the classes is a challenge. For this reason, the focus below is on seven most fundamental complexity classes.

**2. P**

(1) P stands for Polynomial time. A P problem is one that is easy for classical (i.e., non-quantum) computers to solve. Precisely, an algorithm in P must halt and provide a correct answer within at most n^c time, where n is the input length and c is some constant.

(2) Classic P problems include: Is a given number prime? What is the shortest path between two points?

(3) The open question researchers ask is: Are P and NP equivalent? If so, it would upend computer science and render most [cryptography](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AF%86%E7%A0%81%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) obsolete overnight. Almost no one believes this is the case.

**3. NP**

(1) NP stands for Nondeterministic Polynomial time. An NP problem is one whose solution can be quickly verified on a classical computer once it is provided. More precisely, if a problem is in NP and the answer is "yes," there exists a short proof certifying that the answer is correct. Given an input string X, to determine if the answer is "yes," there is a corresponding short proof string Y that can be used to verify in polynomial time that the answer is indeed "yes." (Y is sometimes called a "short witness"—all NP problems have rapidly verifiable short witnesses.)

(2) Classic NP problems include:

The clique problem. In a graph with edges and nodes, if two nodes are related, they are connected by an edge. A clique is a subgraph where every pair of nodes is connected by an edge. Given a graph, what is the size of the largest clique? Does a clique of a certain number of nodes exist? This is an NP-complete problem, meaning it is among the hardest problems in NP. However, if a subgraph is given, checking whether it forms a clique is easy.

The traveling salesman problem. Given a set of cities and the distances between every pair, find the shortest tour that visits each city exactly once and returns to the origin. From a graph-theoretic perspective, this is finding a minimum-weight Hamiltonian cycle in a weighted complete [undirected graph](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%A0%E5%90%91%E5%9B%BE&zhida_source=entity). Because the set of feasible solutions is all permutations of the vertices, adding vertices leads to a "combinatorial explosion," and this problem is also NP-complete.

(3) The open question is whether P equals NP. Computer scientists are far from resolving this.

**4. PH**

(1) PH stands for Polynomial Hierarchy. PH problems generalize NP, encompassing all NP problems and adding extra layers of complexity. More precisely, PH contains problems with alternating quantifiers, which make them more intricate. An example with alternating quantifiers is: "Given X, does there exist a Y such that for every Z, there is a W for which R holds?" The more quantifiers a problem has, the more complex it is, and the higher it sits in the polynomial hierarchy.

(2) A classic PH problem is: determining whether a 50-person clique exists but a 51-person clique does not.

(3) Computer scientists cannot yet prove that PH differs from P. This is equivalent to the question of whether P equals NP: if (unfortunately) P = NP, then the entire PH collapses to P, i.e., P = PH.

**5. PSPACE**

(1) PSPACE stands for Polynomial Space. PSPACE problems are those solvable within a reasonable amount of memory. More precisely, PSPACE cares only about the memory required to run an algorithm, not time.

(2) Computer scientists have proven that PSPACE contains PH, which in turn contains NP, which contains P. Thus, classic PSPACE problems include every P, NP, and PH problem.

(3) There is also a grand open question: Is PSPACE different from P?

**6. BQP**

(1) BQP stands for Bounded-error Quantum Polynomial time. BQP problems are those easy for quantum computers to solve. Precisely, they are problems a quantum computer can solve within polynomial time.

(2) A classic BQP problem is: identifying the prime factors of a positive integer.

(3) Computer scientists have shown that BQP is contained in PSPACE and contains P. It is uncertain whether BQP is in NP, but researchers strongly believe NP and BQP are incomparable—some problems are in NP but not BQP, and vice versa.

**7. EXPTIME**

(1) EXPTIME stands for Exponential Time. EXPTIME problems are those classical computers can solve within an exponential amount of time. Precisely, EXPTIME contains all previous classes—P, NP, PH, PSPACE, and BQP.

(2) Researchers have proven EXPTIME differs from P, since there exist EXPTIME problems not in P. Classic EXPTIME problems include typical board games and checkers. If the board can be of arbitrary size, determining whether a player has an advantage at a given board position is EXPTIME-hard.

(3) Computer scientists hope to prove that PSPACE is not contained in EXPTIME. They believe some EXPTIME problems lie outside PSPACE, as they may require a large amount of memory to solve.

**8. BPP**

(1) BPP stands for Bounded-error Probabilistic Polynomial time. BPP problems are those solvable quickly by algorithms that incorporate random numbers. Precisely, BPP is identical to P except that the algorithm may include randomized decision steps. Algorithms in BPP only need to give a correct answer with probability close to 1.

(2) A classic BPP problem is: Given two different formulas, each generating a polynomial in many variables, do they compute exactly the same polynomial? This is known as the polynomial identity testing problem.

(3) Computer scientists want to know whether BPP = P. If true, every [randomized algorithm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9A%8F%E6%9C%BA%E5%8C%96%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) could be derandomized. Many believe that for every problem with an efficient [randomized algorithm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9A%8F%E6%9C%BA%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity), a deterministic one exists, but they cannot prove it.

### (III) Complexity Results for Mobile Robot Planning Problems[\[12\]](#ref_12) [\[13\]](#ref_13)

**1. Path Planning**  
The path planning problem is to find a path $\sigma(\alpha):[0,1] \rightarrow \mathcal{X}$ in the robot's configuration space from an initial configuration to a target region, subject to given global and local constraints.  
Let $\chi$ be the configuration space, and let $\sum(\mathcal{X})$ denote the set of all [continuous functions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%9E%E7%BB%AD%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) $[0,1] \rightarrow \mathcal{X}$; the initial configuration is $\mathbf{x}_{\text {init }} \in \mathcal{X}$, and the path must end in the target region $X_{\text {goal }} \subseteq \mathcal{X}$. The set of all allowable configurations is called the free configuration space, denoted $\chi_{\text {free }}$. These are typically collision-free configurations, but may also represent other holonomic constraints on the path. Differential constraints on the path are represented by the predicate $D\left(\mathrm{x}, \mathrm{x}^{\prime}, \mathrm{x}^{\prime \prime}, \ldots\right)$, capturing derivative constraints on velocity, acceleration, etc., as well as a degree of path smoothness, such as bounds on path curvature or curvature rate. Let $J(\sigma): \sum(\mathcal{X}) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a cost functional. The optimal path planning problem can then be stated generally as follows:  
**Optimal Path Planning**: Given a quintuple $\left(\mathcal{X}_{\text {free }}, \mathrm{x}_{\text {init }}, X_{\text {goal }}, D, J\right)$, find $\sigma^*=\arg \min _{\sigma \in \sum(X)} J(\sigma)$ subject to:  

$$
\begin{aligned}  &\sigma(0)=\mathrm{x}_{\text {init }}, \quad \sigma(1) \in X_{\text {goal }} \\  &\sigma(\alpha) \in \mathcal{X}_{\text {free }}, \quad \forall \alpha \in[0,1] \\ & D\left(\sigma(\alpha), \sigma^{\prime}(\alpha), \sigma^{\prime \prime}(\alpha), \ldots\right), \quad \forall \alpha \in[0,1] \end{aligned}
$$


The problem of finding an optimal path under holonomic and differential constraints as stated above is considered PSPACE-hard[\[14\]](#ref_14). Therefore, if $\mathrm{P} \neq \mathrm{NP}$, no efficient (polynomial-time) algorithm can solve all instances of this problem. Consequently, researchers shifted attention to [approximation methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%91%E4%BC%BC%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) or to subsets of the general path planning problem.

(1) Feasible path planning for holonomic models in polygonal/polyhedral environments  
Early work focused on feasible (non-optimal) path planning for holonomic robot models in polygonal/polyhedral environments, assuming obstacles are polygonal/polyhedral and that no differential constraints are imposed on the generated path. In 1970, Reif found that collision-free paths for holonomic robots whose footprints can be described as a single polyhedron can be found in polynomial time in both two- and three-dimensional environments[\[14\]](#ref_14). Canny proved that feasible path planning in free spaces represented by polynomials is PSPACE-hard, making the decision problem for feasible path planning without differential constraints PSPACE-complete[\[15\]](#ref_15).

(2) Shortest path planning  
The goal of the optimal path planning problem is to find a shortest collision-free path. It was recognized early on that shortest paths for holonomic robots in two-dimensional environments with polygonal obstacles can be found in polynomial time[\[16\]](#ref_16) [\[17\]](#ref_17). More precisely, this can be computed in $O\left(n^2\right)$ time, where $n$ is the number of vertices of the polygonal obstacles[\[18\]](#ref_18). The solution can be obtained by constructing and searching a so-called "visibility graph"[\[19\]](#ref_19). In contrast, Lazard, Reif, and Wang showed that finding curvature-bounded shortest paths (i.e., paths for car-like robots) in the plane amid polygonal obstacles is NP-hard[\[20\]](#ref_20), indicating that no known polynomial-time algorithm exists for finding shortest paths for car-like robots among polygonal obstacles. A related result is that a path with curvature constraints amid polygonal obstacles can be determined in exponential time (EXPTIME)[\[21\]](#ref_21).

(3) Curvature-bounded shortest paths in obstacle-free environments  
A special case amenable to efficient computation is curvature-bounded shortest paths in obstacle-free environments. Dubins showed that, given two points $p_1, p_2$ and tangent directions $\theta_1, \theta_2$ at those points, the shortest path between them with a maximum curvature bound $k$ is a curve composed of at most three segments, each being a circular arc or a straight line[\[22\]](#ref_22). Reeds and Shepp extended this to vehicles capable of moving both forward and backward[\[23\]](#ref_23). Another notable case is the $O\left(n^2 \log n\right)$ algorithm proposed by Agraval et al. for finding a curvature-bounded shortest path inside a [convex polygon](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%87%B8%E5%A4%9A%E8%BE%B9%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity)[\[24\]](#ref_24). Similarly, Boissonnat and Lazard proposed a polynomial-time algorithm[\[25\]](#ref_25) for finding an exact curvature-bounded path in a specific environment where obstacle boundaries all have bounded curvature.  
Since most problems relevant to autonomous driving lack exact algorithms with practical computational complexity[\[20\]](#ref_20), it is necessary to turn to more general numerical methods. These typically do not find an exact solution but instead aim to find a satisfactory solution or a sequence of feasible solutions converging to the optimum.

**2. Trajectory Planning**  
The solution to trajectory planning is a trajectory, i.e., a time-parameterized function $\pi(t):[0, T] \rightarrow \mathcal{X}$ that specifies how the configuration evolves over time.  
Let $\Pi(x, T)$ denote the set of all continuous functions $[0, T] \rightarrow \mathcal{X}$, and let $x_{\text {init }} \in \mathcal{X}$ be the initial configuration. The target region is $X_{\text {goal }} \subseteq \mathcal{X}$. The set of all allowable configurations at time $t \in[0, T]$ is denoted $x_{\text {free }}(t)$, representing holonomic constraints such as avoiding collisions with static and potentially dynamic obstacles. Differential constraints on the trajectory are expressed by the predicate $D\left(\mathrm{x}, \mathrm{x}^{\prime}, \mathrm{x}^{\prime \prime}, \ldots\right)$ and can be used to impose dynamic constraints on the trajectory. Additionally, let $J(\pi): \Pi(\mathcal{X}, T) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a cost functional. Under these assumptions, the optimal trajectory planning problem can be formulated very generally as:  
**Optimal Trajectory Planning:** Given a sextuple $\left(X_{\text {free }}, \mathrm{x}_{\text {init }}, X_{\text {goal }}, D, J, T\right)$, find $\pi^*=\arg \min _{\pi \in \Pi(X, T)} J(\pi)$ subject to:  

$$
\begin{aligned}  &\pi(0)=\mathrm{x}_{\text {init }}, \quad \pi(T) \in X_{\text {goal }} \\ &\pi(t) \in X_{\text {free }}, \quad \forall t \in[0, T] \\  &D\left(\pi(t), \pi^{\prime}(t), \pi^{\prime \prime}(t), \ldots\right), \quad \forall t \in[0, T] \end{aligned}
$$


Because trajectory planning in dynamic environments generalizes path planning in static environments, the problem remains PSPACE-hard. Moreover, trajectory planning in dynamic environments has been proven harder than path planning: certain problem variants that are tractable in static environments become intractable in dynamic settings. Typically, the shortest path for a point robot in a static two-dimensional polygonal environment can be found efficiently in polynomial time; in contrast, Canny and Reif's result[\[26\]](#ref_26) shows that finding a bounded-velocity collision-free trajectory for a holonomic point robot amid moving polygonal obstacles is [NP-hard](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=NP-hard&zhida_source=entity). Similarly, although path planning for a robot with fixed degrees of freedom in a three-dimensional polyhedral environment is easy, Reif and Sharir showed that trajectory planning for a robot with 2 degrees of freedom amid translating and rotating three-dimensional polyhedral obstacles is PSPACE-hard[\[27\]](#ref_27).  
For trajectory planning problems, the absence of tractable exact algorithms makes numerical methods a popular choice. These may apply [calculus of variations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%98%E5%88%86%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) directly in the time domain or transform the trajectory planning problem into a path planning problem in a configuration space augmented with a [time dimension](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=245345446&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E9%97%B4%E7%BB%B4%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity)[\[28\]](#ref_28).  
The transformation from a trajectory planning problem $\left(X_{\text {free }}^T, \mathrm{x}_{\text {init }}^T, X_{\text {goal }}^T, D^T, J^T, T\right)$ to a path planning problem $\left(X_{\text {free }}^P, x_{\text {init }}^P, X_{\text {goal }}^P, D^P, J^P\right)$ is typically implemented as follows.

Define $X^P:=X^T \times[0, T]$ as the configuration space for path planning. For any $y \in \mathcal{X}^P$, let $t(\mathrm{y}) \in[0, T]$ denote the time component and $c(\mathrm{y}) \in \mathcal{X}^T$ denote the configuration component of point $y$. For a path $\sigma(\alpha):[0,1] \rightarrow \mathcal{X}^P$ and a trajectory $\pi(t):[0, T] \rightarrow \mathcal{X}^T$, if the time components at the start and end of the path satisfy  

$$
\begin{aligned} & t(\sigma(0))=0 \\ & t(\sigma(1))=T \end{aligned}
$$


and the path is monotonically increasing, i.e., expressible as the differential constraint  
$t\left(\sigma^{\prime}(\alpha)\right)>0, \quad \forall \alpha \in[0,1]$  
then this path $\sigma(\alpha)$ can be transformed into the trajectory $\pi(t)$ . Furthermore, the free configuration space, initial configuration, target region, and differential constraints are mapped to the path planning problem as follows:  

$$
\begin{aligned} & x_{\text {free }}^P=\left\{(x, t): x \in X_{\text {free }}^T(t) \wedge t \in[0, T]\right\} \\ & \mathrm{x}_{\text {init }}^P=\left(\mathrm{x}_{\text {init }}^T, 0\right) \\ & X_{\text {goal }}^P=\left\{(x, T): x \in X_{\text {goal }}^T\right\} \\ &D^P\left(\mathrm{y}, \mathrm{y}^{\prime}, \mathrm{y}^{\prime \prime}, \ldots\right)=D^T\left(c(\mathrm{y}), \frac{c\left(\mathrm{y}^{\prime}\right)}{t\left(\mathrm{y}^{\prime}\right)}, \frac{c\left(\mathrm{y}^{\prime \prime}\right)}{t\left(\mathrm{y}^{\prime \prime}\right)}, \ldots\right) \end{aligned}
$$


A path planning algorithm capable of handling differential constraints can then be used to solve this path planning problem, and the solution is ultimately transformed back into a trajectory.
