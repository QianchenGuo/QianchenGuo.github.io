I work in motion planning, so I'll take a crack at this question—though I suspect my answer may be somewhat off-topic. Consider this a modest attempt to spark further discussion (and note that I won't restrict the discussion to convex cost functions).

## Finite Groups

First, you haven't specified whether the decision space (state space) in question is a finite or infinite group.

If it's a finite group, we only need to study the group's structure to obtain a complete algorithm for finding solutions (enumeration works in principle, but it's hardly elegant). Such problems typically exhibit symmetry and combinatorial structure. For example, the [Rubik's cube problem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=750598552&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=%E9%AD%94%E6%96%B9%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity) fits your description perfectly.

This introduces [commutators](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=750598552&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=%E5%AF%B9%E6%98%93%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity) and studies their effect on the state.

## Discrete Infinite Groups

If the group is infinite, we can further distinguish between discrete groups and Lie groups. Let's start with discrete infinite groups.

Compared to finite groups, optimization over infinite groups places even greater demands on understanding the group's structure. For such decision spaces, we can employ coarse-graining methods (which are highly effective at reducing search complexity). For instance, we can search over subgroups, perform coset decompositions, or find generators and conduct generator-based search. Certain computational group theory algorithms aim to uncover the structure of a given group—for example, the [Schreier-Sims algorithm](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=750598552&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=Schreier-Sims+%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) finds a base and strong generating set for permutation groups and computes the group order.

Based on these decompositions, one can adopt branch-and-bound strategies or heuristic search methods, including randomized approaches and genetic algorithms.

## Lie Groups

This is the most thoroughly studied and mature theory, with widespread applications in robotics. The main reason is that Lie groups have exceptionally nice structure—they are manifolds endowed with many favorable properties. Optimization on Lie groups therefore inherits many methods from optimization on manifolds. That said, I know relatively little about manifold optimization, so I can only offer a few superficial observations.

First, on [Riemannian manifolds](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=750598552&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=Riemann%E6%B5%81%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity), one can intrinsically define the [Riemannian gradient](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=750598552&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=Riemann%E6%A2%AF%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity) (a generalization of the natural gradient on probabilistic parameter manifolds). This is essentially equivalent to first finding the gradient direction in the tangent space (using the Riesz representation theorem to identify gradients with tangent vectors) and then pulling back to the manifold via an exponential map approximation (or Euclidean retraction).

Alternative approaches exist as well—for instance, constructing the Hessian of the objective function in the tangent space, solving a Newton step, and then pulling the result back to the manifold via the (approximate) exponential map. This is closely related to the Riemannian gradient, though introducing objects such as [Christoffel symbols](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=750598552&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=Christoffel%E7%AC%A6%E5%8F%B7&zhida_source=entity) makes the computations considerably more involved.

For the matrix Lie group [SO(3)](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=750598552&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=SO%283%29&zhida_source=entity)—the group commonly encountered in robotics for attitude optimization—there are convenient approximations. By using representations of infinitesimal rotations, we can write down the gradient and Hessian in closed form, enabling straightforward gradient-based methods.

There's also the interplay between SO(3), axis-angle, and quaternion representations. These involve certain topological group isomorphisms: the axis-angle vector is naturally isomorphic to the Lie algebra via the wedge and hat maps; the [Rodrigues formula](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=750598552&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=Rodrigues%E5%85%AC%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity) provides an isomorphism between axis-angle vectors and SO(3); and using the Gibbs vector derived from the axis-angle representation, one can obtain quaternions via the Cayley map. The rich structure here gives rise to many remarkable formulas.
