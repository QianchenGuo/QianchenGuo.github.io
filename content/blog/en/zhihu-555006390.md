This article is one of my answers that I think turned out quite well; it presents, in outline form, my personal understanding of the main thread and structure of advanced algebra, along with brief reviews of two books I have read (more detailed reviews may come later).

My first piece of advice is to make sure you nail down the concepts solidly. As a math major, you cannot confine yourself to computation—you must understand the concepts and the connections between them. Here are a few books.

——————————————————————

First, since you are a math major, you will inevitably encounter [abstract algebra](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8A%BD%E8%B1%A1%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) later on, so it is worth trying to read Kostrikin's *Introduction to Algebra* to get a feel for how seamlessly advanced algebra and abstract algebra interlock. It lets you survey advanced algebra from the lofty vantage point of abstract algebra, while also laying a solid foundation for your later study of abstract algebra. That said, Kostrikin does have a somewhat mischievous sense of humor in his writing, so I suggest getting used to that quirk before using the book strategically (laughs).

Second, if your ambitions are less lofty, or if for various reasons Kostrikin's book is too tough to chew through or you simply lack the time, I recommend Qiu Weisheng's *Advanced Algebra*. It is also a hefty tome, but there are video lectures on Bilibili, and the videos are organized by topic (so you can watch selectively), which can greatly boost your study efficiency. The book proceeds roughly from the concrete to the abstract: the first volume is close in content to engineering linear algebra, studying vector spaces, while the second volume introduces more abstract content such as linear spaces, and goes on to introduce metrics starting from linear spaces, bringing it closer to the central themes of advanced algebra as taught in math departments. Qiu also pays special attention to the background behind why a concept is introduced or studied, and he enjoys dissecting "sparrows" to explain motivation, which is very helpful for conceptual understanding.

In addition, Li Shangzhi and Yao Musheng also have advanced algebra video lectures on Bilibili; [Lan Yizhong](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%93%9D%E4%BB%A5%E4%B8%AD&zhida_source=entity)'s book is also highly regarded, and Godement's *Algebra* first covers abstract algebra and then surveys advanced algebra from above. These are all good books, but since I have not personally read them, I will not recommend them in detail here.

I skimmed Wang Efang's textbook out of curiosity, given its widespread use, but soon set it aside—the prose is simply too ugly, and the level is not much higher than engineering linear algebra. I really could not stand it.

As for the knowledge points themselves, here I will roughly give my own understanding of advanced algebra. The key is to grasp the main thread of the course and focus on the principal contradiction.

Advanced algebra—or linear algebra in the non-engineering sense—is mainly concerned with (finite-dimensional) linear spaces together with the linear maps on them and the linear transformations between linear spaces. From the perspective of abstract algebra, a linear space is a structure arising from the linear action of a field on an abelian group (there are other viewpoints, which I will omit). A truly elevated viewpoint embeds advanced algebra into module theory in abstract algebra, but for reasons of level I will not over-inflate here.

Historically, these theories all originate from the study of systems of linear equations. In the course of solving such systems, people introduced determinants and matrices—at the time regarded as mere notational devices—and established some of their properties and operations.

By studying determinants and matrices in the context of solving linear systems, people abstracted the vector space $K^n$, along with the concepts of basis and dimension, which are closely tied to maximal linearly independent sets of vectors and the rank of a matrix. At this point, matrices ceased to be mere notation and acquired the meaning of transformations on spaces; this gave us the conditions for solvability of linear systems and the structure of their solutions. Incidentally, in low dimensions this vector space has a geometric intuition, which is why linear algebra content applies to analytic geometry in space. For this part, I recommend 3Blue1Brown's *Essence of Linear Algebra* on Bilibili and a book called *The Geometric Meaning of Linear Algebra*.

But a vector space still has elements that are vectors in the traditional sense; by abstracting further, we arrive at the concept of a linear space, whose elements need no longer be traditional vectors. The experience we gained from studying vector spaces can be carried over to linear spaces. As is well known, in algebra there are generally two ways to study a space directly: study its subspaces or its [quotient spaces](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%95%86%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity). That is why we study subspaces and quotient spaces.

For matrices, the corresponding more abstract concept is the linear map between linear spaces; conversely, matrices serve as a tool for studying linear maps. Many concepts from our earlier study of linear systems and vector spaces have more abstract counterparts here, so we study operations on linear maps, kernels and images of linear maps, eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and [invariant subspaces](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%8D%E5%8F%98%E5%AD%90%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity). Moreover, the linear functionals on a linear space form its [dual space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AF%B9%E5%81%B6%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity). And all these abstract concepts (in the finite-dimensional case) can be reduced back to matrices.

Let me now touch on some concepts from abstract algebra. When analyzing an algebraic structure, we often study the relationships between different objects within the [algebraic structure](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0%E7%BB%93%E6%9E%84&zhida_source=entity), and we also require that such relational transformations preserve the algebraic operation structure (just as we study vector spaces and even linear maps on linear spaces, representing them with matrices). Such a relation or map is called a homomorphism. Furthermore, if there is a one-to-one homomorphism between two objects, then we say the two are algebraically indistinguishable—they are isomorphic. We group isomorphic objects into a class; such classes form an [equivalence class](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%AD%89%E4%BB%B7%E7%B1%BB&zhida_source=entity), and picking a representative from each equivalence class gives a canonical form. We can then focus our attention on canonical forms and the process of reducing to canonical form. This is why we introduce equivalence and similarity for matrices and even linear maps—and similarity alone is not enough, since it cannot account for nonzero [nilpotent transformations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B9%82%E9%9B%B6%E5%8F%98%E6%8D%A2&zhida_source=entity). Hence we introduce the Jordan canonical form, which gives a complete similarity classification over the complex numbers.

When studying such [equivalence relations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%AD%89%E4%BB%B7%E5%85%B3%E7%B3%BB&zhida_source=entity), we also pay special attention to invariants under the transformation, because invariants often carry deep meaning. For example, the [determinant](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9F%A9%E9%98%B5%E7%9A%84%E8%A1%8C%E5%88%97%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity), rank, and trace of a matrix are all invariants under similarity.

A plain linear space has somewhat too little structure, so in many problems its range of application is naturally too narrow. For instance, in mathematical analysis we study $\mathbb{R}^n$, i.e., analysis on $n$-dimensional Euclidean space. This involves introducing a metric, which brings with it familiar notions like length, angle, and distance. Starting from a linear space, we add structure on top; in order to introduce a metric on a linear space, we study [bilinear forms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%8C%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) on the space. In particular, a positive definite symmetric bilinear form on a real linear space $V$ is an inner product; once an inner product is introduced, the real linear space becomes a real [inner product space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%86%85%E7%A7%AF%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity), also called a [Euclidean space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AC%A7%E5%87%A0%E9%87%8C%E5%BE%97%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity). In physics, taking a certain nondegenerate [symmetric bilinear form](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AF%B9%E7%A7%B0%E5%8F%8C%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) as the inner product on $\mathbb{R}^4$ yields Minkowski space. Abstracting to linear spaces, with different types of inner products, we get the concepts of orthogonal spaces and [symplectic spaces](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BE%9B%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity).

To better bridge into the concepts of rings and fields in abstract algebra, every advanced algebra course includes a chapter on polynomials, giving an important example of a ring—the [polynomial ring](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%9A%E9%A1%B9%E5%BC%8F%E7%8E%AF&zhida_source=entity)—and studying its structure.

In Qiu Weisheng's *Advanced Algebra*, there is also some content on [multilinear algebra](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211467633&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%9A%E9%87%8D%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity), including tensor algebra and exterior algebra. Tensors will come up in other contexts of your studies, and one can regard this as a tool for applying the theory of linear spaces elsewhere.

One direction from advanced algebra leads forward to abstract algebra; another leads toward functional analysis. After all, functional analysis is colloquially known as infinite-dimensional linear algebra. In functional analysis, since the spaces are infinite-dimensional, matrices can no longer serve as a tool for studying linear transformations; instead we study linear maps and linear operators. For a taste of this functional-analytic way of thinking, you might look at *Linear Algebra Done Right* as an introduction.
