In mobile robot motion planning, the limited perception of the ego vehicle and other agents caused by finite sensor range and occlusions from surrounding objects is a key factor in balancing planning safety and conservatism. This was already reflected in my previous article [\[1\]](#ref_1). In essence, this is again a decision-making problem under limited information.

## I. Overview

### (i) Problem Description

If the overall problem is too unstructured—for instance, general motion planning in unknown environments—then we can only actively acquire additional information through the ego robot's own maneuvers. This is the core idea behind branches such as active SLAM, active perception, and active planning. However, in specific tasks like autonomous driving, once we incorporate road structure, right-of-way, certain traffic rules, and even the interactive nature of the task itself, we gain access to more information and assumptions.

Moreover, active planning in the robotics community rarely considers interaction; it typically handles only static and dynamic obstacle avoidance, and many works are quite crude (e.g., simply propagating a time-expanding distribution or set and using it as a soft constraint...). In autonomous driving, some occlusions are caused by static obstacles—such as road structure, terrain, and buildings blocking the view of potential hazards ahead. Human drivers typically slow down in such situations to gain more reaction time and stop when necessary. Other occlusions are dynamic, such as the ego sensor range or traffic participants in autonomous driving, which require more complex interaction for both safety and efficiency.

Therefore, the starting point of this article is—**to attempt to extract more information from the structure of these autonomous driving problems that can be used to handle occlusions, and to account for the characteristics of high dynamics and strong interaction, thereby providing a brief overview of occlusion-handling methods in PPnC**. It is worth noting that, for practical considerations, we **do not consider using V2X communication to solve this problem**, although there is related work [\[2\]](#ref_2) [\[3\]](#ref_3) [\[4\]](#ref_4) [\[5\]](#ref_5). In addition, we also **do not consider hardware-level issues**, such as sensor degradation due to adverse weather or faults. Finally, we **do not consider learning-based methods** [\[6\]](#ref_6).

### (ii) Core Tension

The trade-off between safety conservatism and traffic efficiency: assuming the worst case for absolute safety leads to excessive conservatism, sacrificing efficiency and smoothness. Local determinism versus global uncertainty: planning relies on local real-time perception (which is relatively certain), but must prepare for the unknown intentions of agents (highly uncertain) in globally unknown regions—that is, it must account for robustness.

### (iii) Takeaways

The broad conclusion of this article is that **no matter what methods we add to the framework (e.g., incorporating learning-based tricks), the framework itself can still be viewed as a generalization of problems in control theory. This, I think, is the power of control theory, or systems theory in general.** Unfortunately, many researchers in control theory are too absorbed in their formula games and neglect the new problems that emerge with development. We should boldly explore new problems and new structures, rather than endlessly dwelling on time delays, packet loss, event triggers, cyber-attacks, stacking buffs, and then flooding the field with LMIs. This is not to say that these lack theoretical value, but rather that control theory has veered off course and become self-contained. The difficulties that are widely recognized in practical problems remain unsolved. Furthermore, **occlusion handling does not fundamentally escape the paradigm of decision-making under limited resources, so many of the methods discussed in this article are also a set of common approaches for tackling this broader class of problems**.

Before formally beginning, let me add a disclaimer: my expertise is limited, so there are bound to be shortcomings or biases in my summarization. Feedback and discussion are welcome.

## II. Generalized Observer—Prediction

In essence, on the one hand, we need to resort to techniques to handle the "partial observability" here; on the other hand, we must endow the planner with corresponding robustness.

The former essentially involves constructing a **generalized observer** that makes reasonable inferences about the perceptually constrained regions caused by occlusion. The generalized observer here can be viewed as building a closed-loop update on the prediction-planning module. Stepping outside the ego-centric perspective, prediction can also be seen as a coarse **planner** for NPCs. The coarseness stems from the fact that our models of other vehicles are not as refined as that of the ego vehicle, and also from our inability to devote as much computational power to constructing closed-loop planners for surrounding key NPCs (of course, if colleagues working on learning-based modules would be willing to spare some GPU compute, that would be ideal).

Since we have an observer, we need to define what it observes—the state of some system. Here, we summarize in order from deterministic to stochastic to set-valued. **The source of information for the closed-loop update is crucial.**

### (i) Point-Based Analysis

This is almost the traditional observer domain, i.e., considering a state observer on Euclidean space $\mathbb R^n$ or a submanifold thereof. We must posit a virtual object in the occluded region, **assume the state is a deterministic but unknown point, and aim to converge via models and observations**. Observation information here can include: perception data, continuous tracking of visible parts of targets; road structure and traffic rules provided by the scene map; and intent prediction using historical interaction information via a sliding window to correct the current estimate.

This framework is not inherently problematic and can reasonably account for prediction error. However, for systems that are unobserved most of the time—as is the case with occlusions—traditional observers can only perform sparse updates, and uniform observability and persistence of excitation conditions cannot possibly be satisfied. Moreover, the uncertain multimodal scenarios in occluded regions cannot be characterized by a single convergent state. Modeling the "occluded region" as a disturbance is merely an intuition; a moment's reflection reveals that occlusion does not add a bounded disturbance to the state or the system model, but rather fundamentally changes the existence and evolution law of the state itself. Its utility is limited to short-term prediction for targets that have just entered occlusion but were previously stably tracked. It is computationally efficient but fails when occlusion lasts long and interactions are complex. Therefore, to handle safety in such cases, we introduce probabilistic and set-based frameworks.

PS: Of course, sparse observation issues also arise in SLAM (though not as severe as with occlusions), and there are corresponding techniques there as well [\[7\]](#ref_7).

### (ii) Probabilistic Inference Framework

Compared to point-based analysis, the probabilistic inference framework extends from the mean of a distribution to the distribution itself. **Uncertainty is described via probability distributions, and belief states are inferred through Bayesian updates or data-driven methods.** Its degree of conservatism lies between point-based and set-based analyses.

Many interaction-aware prediction frameworks also adopt this technique [\[8\]](#ref_8) [\[9\]](#ref_9). Dynamic Bayesian networks and Markov chains are used to infer the intentions of surrounding vehicles and predict motion. These methods account for interactions, road layout, and traffic rules. At each time step, they consider interactions among vehicles and the road layout to infer the probabilities of different behaviors, the probability of being in each possible driving corridor, and the probability of lane changes. These intentions are fused with motion predictions computed from kinematic models. Dynamics-based motion prediction more accurately analyzes the physical environment by considering multiple possible outcomes. Probabilistic methods, on the other hand, are better suited to characterize intentions across different scenarios, enhancing the ability to handle complex dynamic situations.

In the occlusion problem, we extend an existing multimodal interaction-aware perception and motion prediction framework. Virtual objects are constructed within the occluded region, and their states are treated as latent variables. Yu et al. [\[10\]](#ref_10) [\[11\]](#ref_11) employ sampling methods to represent the states of potential occluded objects as particles. Two-way reachability analysis is used to determine the distribution of the "virtual" objects. Using the scene, map, traffic flow, historical observations, and models, they predict the existence probability, intention, and multimodal future trajectories of hidden objects for risk assessment and decision-making. This framework is also common in information fusion and filtering communities, and it can flexibly incorporate (deep) Bayesian network components [\[12\]](#ref_12). The overall pipeline clearly resembles discrete-time Kalman filtering or other Markov observer steps, as follows:

1) **Create target entities and states to be estimated**: detect and classify occlusions caused by static objects, dynamic objects, or sensor range; generate "hidden vehicles" and their corresponding "virtual corridors" at the edges of occluded regions, followed by further classification and merging operations;

2) **Prediction step**: first perform intent prediction to determine which visible vehicles and/or scene elements influence the prediction of the hidden vehicle. To this end, pairwise intersections of corridor centerlines are computed, and the nearest and longest dependencies are selected to constrain the yield/pass relationship of the occlusion prediction. Then perform trajectory prediction using mocked acceleration information, based on the same method as normal prediction but applied to the occluded region (the difference being that the initial distribution uniformly covers the full possible interval);

Note that in the prediction step, depending on available computational power and the desired level of fidelity, one may introduce interaction games (ego vs. occluded vehicle, occluded vehicle vs. other vehicles) to better determine the yield/pass relationship.

3) **Update step**: based on the occlusion boundary and the continuous observation time, update the belief distribution of the hidden vehicle's initial state (position and velocity) in the form of a probability distribution. To simplify the number of occlusions and avoid unnecessary computation, any corridor that does not intersect the ego trajectory, or that is superseded by a closer dependency from another occlusion, is removed;

The hidden vehicle is incorporated into the framework's interaction-aware prediction pipeline as an ordinary traffic participant, ultimately generating a probabilistic motion grid that includes occlusion likelihood.

This approach integrates the occlusion problem into a broader **interaction-aware motion prediction** framework. Its core is to generate **probabilistic future scenarios that include the likelihood of occluded vehicles**. It does so by creating "virtual" traffic participants at the perception boundary and using traffic rules (e.g., right-of-way), scene structure (e.g., lane markings), and interaction relationships to estimate the probability distributions of their intentions and trajectories. The output is not a binary safe/unsafe judgment, but rather a **probability motion grid containing risk information**, which downstream planners use for trade-off decisions. This approach enables more natural, human-like driving behaviors (such as cautiously approaching an intersection), but it typically cannot provide strict safety guarantees; its safety depends on prediction accuracy and the planner's risk-handling logic.

### (III) Reachable Set Analysis

In earlier research, reachability analysis has been a classic tool in PPnC. It adopts a worst-case assumption, using sets rather than [point estimates](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%82%B9%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1&zhida_source=entity) to describe potential risk, introduces "virtual" objects beyond the field of view, and predicts their future states via forward reachability analysis to address risk assessment under occlusion while providing provable safety guarantees.

What we truly need in the occlusion problem is **the set of states that a potentially occluded object can reach from the current time, subject to all available perceptual information, [temporal logic](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%B6%E5%BA%8F%E9%80%BB%E8%BE%91&zhida_source=entity), and scenario knowledge constraints**. Constructing a refined boundary is essentially a process of progressively adding constraints.

**1. Open-Loop vs. Closed-Loop**

Basic reachable sets (open-loop) consider **[kinematic and dynamic](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%90%E5%8A%A8%E5%AD%A6%E4%B8%8E%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) modeling**. Some works model kinematics only. Orzechowski et al. [\[13\]](#ref_13) over-approximate the reachable set by assuming the object can emerge from occlusion at any time with maximum possible velocity. [\[14\]](#ref_14) assumes the object can dash out from any point on the boundary at maximum speed, forming a time-expanding "capsule" region. This is somewhat coarse for objects with dynamics models, but reasonable for objects such as pedestrians (pedestrian dynamics are harder to model, though some characteristics exist—e.g., as forward speed increases, turning capability decreases). Others model dynamics [\[15\]](#ref_15), pruning in both the (x, y) and (x, v) planes. A major issue is that computing reachable sets for high-dimensional and [complex dynamics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%8D%E6%9D%82%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) is computationally expensive, so the intersection of multiple [linear model](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%A8%A1%E5%9E%8B&zhida_source=entity) reachable sets or an over-approximation via a [polytope](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%9A%E8%83%9E%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity) is commonly employed.

In occlusion problems, refined boundaries introduce "innovation" in a closed-loop manner. For example, [\[15\]](#ref_15) uses **new observations** to take set differences and exclude certain states from the reachable set. In contrast, [\[16\]](#ref_16) employs a more sophisticated observation logic, introducing **temporal and tracking** elements, treating each sensor scan as a "pruning" operation on the reachable set. It excludes observable position intervals while continuously shrinking the velocity intervals in the [state space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity). The velocity upper bound is tightened based on occlusion duration (a long absence implies high-speed objects are unlikely), and boundary motion is tracked to dynamically update the possible region, **continuously contracting the state set** to reduce prediction conservatism.

**2. Road Structure and Right-of-Way**

Traffic flow and other NPCs are associated with lanes or a motion corridor, so **corridor constraints** can be reasonably incorporated into reachable set propagation. Meanwhile, an NPC inside the occluded region is also part of the overall traffic flow; what occludes the ego vehicle may not occlude other NPCs in the flow. Thus, **predictions of other NPCs** can further shrink or eliminate uncertainty about the occluded region.

**Traffic rules should also be translated into constraints on the state space.** The simplest example is speed limit restrictions on velocity intervals; additionally, during a red light phase, the reachable set of vehicles on the intersecting road should not intrude beyond the stop line. This requires incorporating traffic signal states as a time-varying parameter in reachable set computation.

Koschi and Althoff [\[17\]](#ref_17) also proposed a set-based method that approximates the reachable set of occluded objects by considering road structure and traffic rules. For those who wish to address both reasonable foreseeable risk response and the approximate allocation of responsibility and assessment of right-of-way, see [\[18\]](#ref_18), which embeds "virtual vehicles" into a Responsibility-Sensitive Safety (RSS) model to trigger standard "proper responses." The core idea is to generate virtual vehicles with **reasonably foreseeable velocity ranges** at the origins of occluded regions (behaviors that are "reasonably foreseeable" under traffic responsibility must be considered as constraints; extreme cases beyond this range are deemed force majeure). Longitudinal, lateral, and intersection conflict safety checks are then performed against the ego vehicle as defined in RSS. If RSS determines a potential hazard, the ego vehicle executes the corresponding "appropriate response" (e.g., braking, avoidance). In scenarios like curves where assuming a static occluder would cause excessive deceleration, two virtual objects with different behavioral assumptions are placed at the same location to represent both nominal and extreme cases.

Furthermore, the influence of virtual object velocity parameters and RSS model parameters themselves (e.g., reaction time, braking capability) on the balance between safety and traffic efficiency was analyzed in depth. It was found that in occluded scenarios, more conservative RSS parameters (such as a smaller minimum [braking deceleration](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%B6%E5%8A%A8%E5%87%8F%E9%80%9F%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity)) do not necessarily lead to lower efficiency. On the contrary, they may prompt the vehicle to decelerate earlier and more smoothly, potentially yielding shorter total traversal times than the last-moment hard braking caused by "aggressive" parameters.

**3. Behavioral Intent**

Introducing behavioral patterns and intent clearly makes the analysis more realistic. However, while behavior can be reasonably well defined in autonomous driving tasks (especially vehicle behavior), intent (which can be viewed as the action sequence planned by another vehicle's driver or a pedestrian) **requires significant computational resources** to plan for NPCs—though the current trend is to fit them with black-box models. Moreover, for NPCs in occluded regions, intent clearly depends on joint inference of the surrounding scene and traffic flow, which is an even more demanding problem, and the correctness of such inference is by no means guaranteed (though it can cope with some routine scenarios).

Another consideration is that intent exhibits **high uncertainty**, so a more thorough treatment essentially requires a probabilistic framework. Since NPCs cannot be presumed to have strong planning capabilities a priori, their motion characteristics and general level of aggressiveness must be introduced via priors or historical observations, which inevitably introduces severe **non-Markovianity**.

Even the **NPC type** may sometimes be unassigned. A somewhat hacky approach, as in [\[18\]](#ref_18), is to compute reachable sets for multiple types based on statistical inference, then weight different response strategies by probability, with the confidence shifting toward one strategy over time.

### (IV) Cross-Fusion

From the discussion above, it is evident that current research frontiers are not mutually exclusive but rather **fuse both approaches** to build hierarchical or hybrid estimation frameworks [\[19\]](#ref_19) [\[20\]](#ref_20). However, few works have applied this to occlusion problems so far. One relatively mature idea is to further fuse probabilistic or set-based formulations into a **risk field** [\[21\]](#ref_21) [\[22\]](#ref_22) [\[23\]](#ref_23), mapping the probability distributions or reachable sets of various obstacles and potentially occluded objects into spatially continuous [risk values](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%A3%8E%E9%99%A9%E5%80%BC&zhida_source=entity).

Some hacky approaches (not rigorous methods) include:

1.  Probability as guidance, sets as guarantees: two systems run in parallel; the planner moves toward the probabilistically optimal direction within the set-based safety boundary;
2.  Sets embedded in a probabilistic framework: set-based descriptions with bounded uncertainty serve as the support of the distribution;
3.  Probability used only as a prior: e.g., using historical behavior and scenarios as priors to reduce multimodality of the probability distribution, or using probabilistic envelopes to tighten the inflation of reachable sets;

In the near term, an ideal occlusion handling system is likely to be a **hierarchical architecture**: the **bottom layer** uses [formal methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BD%A2%E5%BC%8F%E5%8C%96%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) to provide provable safety strategies or boundaries; the **middle layer** employs probabilistic interaction to generate human-like behavior; and the **high-level decision** is responsible for probabilistic inference based on the scenario, other vehicles' intent, and driving style. The result is a more compact reachable set that fuses multiple sources of information and evolves with both deterministic and stochastic intent, serving as input to the planning module.

Of course, the price of such refinement is **increased computational complexity** and **dependence on information quality**. The multi-level analyses described above (whether probabilistic or set-based) are computationally demanding in dynamic high-dimensional settings, requiring subspace reduction in state spaces such as position, orientation, velocity, and acceleration, or [linear approximation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E8%BF%91%E4%BC%BC&zhida_source=entity); methods for further acceleration are currently lacking. Although introducing structure helps reduce conservatism, these methods still **ignore the ego vehicle's future response capability after acquiring new observations**. Consequently, forward invariant sets are a sufficient condition for safety, and the resulting behaviors tend to be overly conservative. Additionally, it is clear that **only bounded occlusions can yield useful information through temporal reasoning**, whereas unbounded occlusions (especially when considering potential objects beyond the field of view) cannot be updated similarly, and the reachable set rapidly expands as the planning horizon increases. One can only manually reduce the hazard level of unbounded occlusions to decrease conservatism, though doing so **also requires introducing an "appropriate response"** to avoid bearing responsibility for accidents under such information-limited conditions. However, RSS is not complete either—it is only self-contained. Therefore, while it provides sufficient conditions for achieving safety in the presence of occlusion, it may lead to suboptimal and conservative actions, negatively impacting driving performance as well as public trust and sense of safety. In engineering practice, additional redundancy is needed, such as maintaining multiple sets in parallel within the system.

## III. Extensions in Decision and Planning

Looking purely at the prediction part, several issues arise: one is that **parameter settings** depend on accurate calibration of other vehicles or heuristic configurations; additionally, the ego vehicle's future ability to **actively react** upon acquiring new information, and the influence of the ego vehicle's active reactions on other vehicles, are neglected. At the decision and planning layer, we must consider interactions among agents, thereby coupling prediction with planning, so that planning can also influence prediction outcomes.

Early work [\[24\]](#ref_24) [\[25\]](#ref_25) planned trajectories for mobile robots in free space over a horizon while considering objects that might emerge from occlusion. To formally analyze collision avoidance with occluded objects, [\[26\]](#ref_26) proposed the inevitable collision state with braking to achieve [passive safety](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%A2%AB%E5%8A%A8%E5%AE%89%E5%85%A8&zhida_source=entity) guarantees [\[27\]](#ref_27). [\[28\]](#ref_28) applied the above ideas to autonomous driving, predicting the future occupancy of different traffic participants in the current field of view and avoiding inevitable collision states. However, **passive safety** provides only a limited degree of guarantee, since other NPCs may still inevitably collide with a stopped ego vehicle. Next, we survey existing algorithms in order from interaction to planning, and from method to framework.

### (I) Game Theory and Interaction Modeling

Game theory models traffic participants as rational agents with competitive or cooperative relationships; the ego vehicle's decisions depend on predictions of NPC decisions, while NPC decisions also depend on predictions of the ego vehicle.

When solving equilibrium strategies (Nash equilibrium, Stackelberg equilibrium), attention must be paid to the (approximate) [convergence](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%94%B6%E6%95%9B%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) of the game; otherwise, introducing game theory can be counterproductive. Computational cost is another concern—solving global equilibria via Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs equations is nearly impossible in real time.

In occlusion problems, mutual intent guessing and strategic interaction among traffic participants are formalized. The ego vehicle and (possibly) hidden objects are modeled as game players, and hierarchical or hybrid game theory is used to solve for equilibrium strategies or safety sets. The advantage is strong modeling capability for highly interactive scenarios, providing non-conservative strict safety guarantees (optimal in the game-theoretic sense). However, the drawbacks are that solving is complex and [real-time computation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AE%9E%E6%97%B6%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97&zhida_source=entity) is extremely challenging; typically, simplifications of the dynamics and game structure are required.

**[Mixed strategy](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B7%B7%E5%90%88%E7%AD%96%E7%95%A5&zhida_source=entity) games** can solve for robust strategies. For example, [\[29\]](#ref_29) integrates a mixed zero-sum [dynamic game](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E6%80%81%E5%8D%9A%E5%BC%88&zhida_source=entity) into online planning. The problem is decomposed into a two-mode hybrid game: an open-loop mode (object not emerged) assumes no hidden objects for efficiency; a closed-loop mode (object emerged) switches to a pursuit-evasion game based on [perfect information](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AE%8C%E7%BE%8E%E4%BF%A1%E6%81%AF&zhida_source=entity), where the ego vehicle employs a [feedback strategy](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%8D%E9%A6%88%E7%AD%96%E7%95%A5&zhida_source=entity) to avoid. A key lemma guarantees that whenever a hidden object emerges at any point along the open-loop trajectory, the ego vehicle still has a closed-loop strategy to avoid collision. This elegantly combines open-loop efficiency with closed-loop safety.

Moreover, one must consider not only the ego vehicle's inability to observe certain objects ("I can't see him, he can't see me"), but also scenarios where the ego vehicle is in the blind spot of other traffic participants ("I can see him, he can't see me")—multi-level problems that essentially involve asymmetric information among game players. **Hypergame** theory can be used to analyze how cognitive divergence due to occlusion leads to conflicts. [\[30\]](#ref_30) uses hypergame theory for safety verification and [white-box testing](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%99%BD%E7%9B%92%E6%B5%8B%E8%AF%95&zhida_source=entity) of game-based decision planners. It models differences in players' perceptions of the game (who sees whom), quantifies risks induced by dynamic occlusion, and automatically generates extreme test cases.

### (II) Cost Functions for Active Planning

**Active perception** [\[31\]](#ref_31) [\[32\]](#ref_32) [\[33\]](#ref_33) attempts to close the loop between perception and motion planning by generating trajectories that explore the environment and acquire information to reduce uncertainty and improve future planning. In fact, using planning maneuverability to enhance other subtask capabilities can be broadly termed **active planning** (the most common areas are active perception and active SLAM). Some works [\[34\]](#ref_34) [\[35\]](#ref_35) also address occlusion via active perception by incorporating predictions of future fields of view into motion planning.

Most are based on optimization frameworks such as model predictive control (MPC), incorporating perception gains (e.g., field-of-view openness, [information gain](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BF%A1%E6%81%AF%E5%A2%9E%E7%9B%8A&zhida_source=entity)) or implicit [safety metrics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AE%89%E5%85%A8%E6%8C%87%E6%A0%87&zhida_source=entity) (e.g., risk fields) into the cost function or constraints.

On one hand, some consider the ego vehicle itself. Some maximize **the ego vehicle's own field of view** [\[36\]](#ref_36), defining a "blind spot angle" and minimizing it in the cost function to induce lateral movement that "peeks" into blind spots (e.g., a probing maneuver before overtaking). Wang et al. [\[35\]](#ref_35) proposed a **perception risk metric** based on predicted field of view, penalizing trajectories with low visibility and high speed when approaching occluded road segments; exploration behavior that increases visibility can improve trajectory efficiency. Others maximize cost derived from **probabilistic metrics**, where the MPC planner minimizes risk while progressing toward the goal, naturally producing avoidance of or cautious approach to occluded regions.

Additionally, some works account for **the blind spots of other vehicles** [\[37\]](#ref_37)—i.e., "he can't see me." This reflects the limited rear-view mirror visibility of others or the limited attention of other drivers. Especially for large freight vehicles, a common heuristic is "overtake cautiously, but be quick and decisive." Therefore, one can model the other vehicle's state estimation [covariance](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%8F%E6%96%B9%E5%B7%AE&zhida_source=entity) of the ego vehicle and minimize the ego vehicle's uncertainty in the other's perception. When the ego vehicle is about to enter another vehicle's blind spot, the trajectory is optimized to minimize the other's estimated covariance (average or terminal), achieving "active signaling" and guiding the ego vehicle to exit the blind spot faster or decelerate earlier to reduce uncertainty. However, assumptions about the other vehicle's perception model may be unrealistic.

The advantage is that active planning capabilities are integrated into existing MPC solvers. Yet in the current active perception or active planning literature, the vast majority of works heuristically construct corresponding costs, and the construction of MPC cost functions in particular should be well justified. Furthermore, the effects of such active planning are typically demonstrated only on an indirect task, rather than departing from the essence of occlusion handling (since that essence is the task of the entire PPnC pipeline, not planning alone). Strict safety guarantees for such heuristic [penalty functions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=269369613&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BD%9A%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) or costs are also difficult to satisfy rigorously.

### (3) A POMDP-Based Solution Framework

POMDP planning formulates the problem as a partially observable Markov decision process, solving online for an **optimal policy** that balances "exploration" (information gathering) and "exploitation" (efficient driving). This framework unifies the aforementioned "generalized observer" (belief state update) with planning, and is the standard framework for decision-making under partial observability. The core idea is that the agent maintains a belief distribution over the environment state (including other vehicles' states and intentions) and selects actions that maximize long-term expected return, where the return typically balances **safety risk** against **traffic efficiency**.

Vehicles that may be occluded are treated as part of the POMDP state. The ego vehicle's future sensing and reaction capabilities are explicitly accounted for, and the observation model in the belief state is carefully designed to incorporate occlusion factors. The optimal policy **proactively includes information-gathering exploratory behaviors** to reduce state uncertainty. One can go further by considering other vehicles' beliefs (nested beliefs)—i.e., other vehicles also have uncertainty about the ego vehicle. This gives rise to the ego vehicle actively reducing other vehicles' uncertainty about itself through specific behaviors.

Regarding occlusion specifically, earlier work [\[38\]](#ref_38) was the first to introduce **continuous-state POMDPs** into decision-making and explicitly highlighted the importance of handling occlusions. [\[39\]](#ref_39) focused on the **scalability** issue of POMDPs under occlusion, circumventing the computational bottleneck via a **decomposition method**. It cleverly exploits the structure of the optimal evasive policy to decompose the complex problem of simultaneously reasoning about multiple hidden road users into a series of sub-problems, each involving only a single user. [\[40\]](#ref_40) was the first to explicitly predict future field-of-view changes within the planning horizon in a POMDP framework. Rather than relying purely on sampling, it **incorporates reachable sets** before constructing a belief scenario tree via Monte Carlo methods. [\[41\]](#ref_41) focused on unsignalized occluded intersections, where the vehicle does not come to a complete stop but instead creeps forward to gain a better field of view, while still ensuring it can brake in time should a hidden vehicle or pedestrian emerge. [\[42\]](#ref_42) extended the virtual-vehicle concept proposed by Hubmann et al. to pedestrians. The emergence probability and future motion of virtual objects are no longer treated as random but are inferred using **map information and road structure**.

The advantages of this framework are its strong adaptability to uncertainty and its rich output (probabilities, multimodality). The framework is also loosely structured and highly flexible, making it easy to incorporate additional components (e.g., learning-based tricks). However, the biggest issue is that the DP framework itself suffers from the **curse of dimensionality** (non-scalability). Mainstream approaches resort to various approximations, pruning, and determinization assumptions to reduce the search space and computational complexity, but these simplifications are largely heuristic. Learning-based tricks suffer from **poor interpretability**, lack safety guarantees, and depend heavily on model assumptions or data quality. In **optimal control**, compared to the DP framework of POMDPs, existing MPC methods benefit from mature optimization frameworks and high computational efficiency. Moreover, fast computation does not require the kinds of assumptions or low-interpretability methods that POMDPs often rely on. On the other hand, the DP framework **cannot handle constraints well** (though safe RL is attempting to modify the DP framework...). In the MPC framework, behavior is guided by costs or constraints, and its safety depends on the uncertainty estimation of occluded regions and the degree of constraint violation.

### (4) A Risk-Aware Problem Framework

Risk-Aware is a paradigm that **exploits the tail information of uncertainty distributions for decision-making**. It aims to **explicitly trade off efficiency against tail risk**. It is a deep integration of **uncertainty quantification**, **risk measures**, and **real-time receding-horizon optimization**. In simple terms, it attempts to introduce safety guarantees within a probabilistic framework.

In classical **optimal control theory**, robust control assumes bounded disturbances and guarantees constraint satisfaction; stochastic control minimizes expected cost while ignoring distribution tails. Risk-Aware, by contrast, accepts that "absolute safety" does not exist and instead quantifies acceptable risk by incorporating tail losses. The core mathematical tool is the risk measure—a functional mapping a random **loss function** to a real number, $\rho: Z \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. Common examples include: the expectation $\mathbb{E}[Z]$ (risk-neutral), capturing average loss; the variance $\mathbb{E}[(Z-\mu)^2]$, capturing loss volatility; chance constraints defined as $P(Z > z) \le \epsilon$, bounding the probability of cost violation; **Value at Risk (VaR)**, which introduces the concept of **quantiles**, defined as $\inf\{z \vert P(Z \le z) \ge 1-\epsilon\}$; and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR), which captures the **expected tail loss**, defined as $\mathbb{E}[Z \vert Z \ge VaR_\epsilon(Z)]$. These measures are increasingly conservative in that order. CVaR has become the mainstream choice because [\[43\]](#ref_43): it satisfies **convexity**, monotonicity, translation invariance, and positive homogeneity, preserving problem convexity; it controls not only *whether* a violation occurs but also *how severe* the violation is; and unlike the discontinuous VaR, CVaR is differentiable.

This framework has given rise to several related subfields:

**1. Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO)**

In autonomous driving, we never know the true intention distribution of other vehicles. The predicted distribution always deviates from the true one. Therefore, instead of assuming a fixed distribution $P$, we consider an **ambiguity set** $\mathcal{P}$ of plausible distributions:

$$
\sup_{P \in \mathcal{P}} \mathbb{E}_P[J] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sup_{P \in \mathcal{P}} CVaR_{\epsilon}^P(h) \le 0
$$

Hakobyan et al. systematically developed the theory of CVaR-constrained control under the Wasserstein metric [\[44\]](#ref_44), reformulating the problem into a tractable SDP form. Results for partially observable linear systems can be found in [\[45\]](#ref_45).

**2. Risk-Aware CBF/MPC**

Risk measures must be embedded along two dimensions simultaneously: in the cost function to suppress tail risk, e.g., $\tilde J = CVaR_{\alpha}(J)$ or $\mathbb{E}[J] + \lambda Var(J)$; and in the constraints—compared to chance constraints such as $P_{collision} \le 0.05$, a risk-aware constraint takes the form $CVaR_{\beta}(d) \le d_{safe}$, ensuring that in the 5% tail scenarios where collision occurs, the severity (e.g., relative speed) remains controlled. [\[46\]](#ref_46) proves **risk-aware exponential stability** and **recursive feasibility**.

**3. Combination with Contingency Planning**

This addresses the **multimodality** of behavior. **RACP (Risk-Aware Contingency Planning)** [\[47\]](#ref_47) maintains a posterior belief $b(m)$ over other vehicles' strategies (intentions and trajectories). Each hypothesized intention $m$ corresponds to an optimal control sequence. The short-term executed trajectory is a probability-weighted average of the branches, but the long-term constraints require each branch to integrate risk awareness. **EraBMPC (Efficient Risk-Aware Branch MPC)** [\[48\]](#ref_48) further introduces min-max optimization to account for ambiguity in the predicted probabilities themselves, adding a regularization term to the probability update step to prevent over-trust in the prediction module.

Because this is a probabilistic framework, it naturally leverages the probabilistic observation framework discussed earlier to predict the future occupancy of occluded objects. **Risk quantification** becomes the central problem here. Additionally, the tail probability $\varepsilon$ in risk measures must be back-derived from OEM safety targets. Regarding occlusion handling specifically, [\[49\]](#ref_49) acknowledges the existence of unknown objects in occluded regions and performs reachable set analysis, but uses probabilities to modulate the reaction intensity rather than braking indiscriminately. [\[50\]](#ref_50) quantifies risk as a geometric inference of the "unknown" in occluded regions, without predicting the specific behavior of occluded objects, and dynamically adjusts speed to keep risk within a bounded range. [\[51\]](#ref_51) proposes a risk measure that captures both the probability of violating safety specifications and the average expected severity of such violations. [\[52\]](#ref_52) elevates risk quantification to an ethical level. A common critique, however, is that much of this work quantifies risk in a somewhat heuristic manner. I believe risk should be properly quantified on the basis of set-based and probabilistic predictions to construct well-founded risk-aware costs and constraints.

## **4. Integration Trends and Open Challenges**

Handling occlusions is not a "one-size-fits-all" problem—trade-offs and integration among the above techniques must be made according to the scenario (highway vs. urban), the degree of interaction, and the required **safety level**. An integration trend is already emerging, and future systems will likely adopt a hierarchical hybrid architecture. In summary, occlusion handling in autonomous driving has evolved from early single-object avoidance into a complex technical system that integrates perception and prediction, optimal control, and game-theoretic interaction.

The future breakthrough will lie in how to organically fuse these complementary approaches. Achieving this vision still faces numerous challenges: How to solve high-dimensional dynamic games or complex reachable sets in real time? How to organically combine probabilistic behavior prediction with formal safety guarantees—i.e., the rigorous definition and verification of "99.9% safe"? And how to ensure that the ego vehicle's "cautious" behavior under occlusion is correctly understood by human drivers (involving regulatory and psychological dimensions)?
