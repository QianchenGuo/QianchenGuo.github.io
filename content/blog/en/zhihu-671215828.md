## 1. Fundamental Problems in Control Theory

Mathematical control theory (which can be understood as control theory becoming a branch of [applied mathematics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BA%94%E7%94%A8%E6%95%B0%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity)) was first established by Pontryagin and others in the late 1950s. The classical results mainly concern deterministic finite-dimensional systems ([lumped-parameter systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9B%86%E4%B8%AD%E5%8F%82%E6%95%B0%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity), described by ODEs). In many practical problems, however, more accurate models are often described by PDEs; these are infinite-dimensional systems, also known as [distributed-parameter](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83%E5%8F%82%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) systems. Simply replacing them with finite-dimensional systems introduces errors. Moreover, randomness is omnipresent in the real world, making it necessary to study [stochastic systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9A%8F%E6%9C%BA%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) from a control-theoretic viewpoint; of course, randomness is only one form of uncertainty (others include [incompleteness](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AE%8C%E5%A4%87%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) and fuzziness).

The fundamental problems are:

1. System modeling and identification — to describe practical problems as accurately as possible; this is actually a branch within control theory.

2. [Structural analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BB%93%E6%9E%84%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90&zhida_source=entity) of control systems — controllability, observability, and stability, i.e., a [feasibility analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%AF%E8%A1%8C%E6%80%A7%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90&zhida_source=entity) of control implementation.

3. [Optimal control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%80%E4%BC%98%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity) and feedback control — (optimal) solutions to the problem.

4. System uncertainty — [adaptive control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%87%AA%E9%80%82%E5%BA%94%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity), robust control, using feedback to address stochastic issues.

5. Numerical methods and digital implementation of control problems.

It is worth pointing out that control problems are essentially different from problems concerning [parameter-dependent](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%90%AB%E5%8F%82&zhida_source=entity) ODEs. Although one may view them as [equation-solving](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%A7%A3%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) problems, control is concerned with an **ill-posed [inverse problem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%8D%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity)**: when both the state $x$ and the control input $u$ are unknown, one uses terminal conditions on the state (or other conditions) to recover the control input $u$. In contrast, the forward problem is to determine the state solution $x$ from the [driving equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%A9%B1%E5%8A%A8%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) given a known control input $u$. Consequently, existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence on initial conditions may all fail in control problems. This is the greatest essential difficulty, and it is also the biggest difference from the questions addressed by other "similar" branches of mathematics (such as [differential equations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity)).

## 2. Control Theory for Deterministic Finite-Dimensional Control Systems

### **2.1 Controllability**

We first consider the controllability of the following linear system on a finite-dimensional [Hilbert space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Hilbert%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity). The definition of exact controllability is as follows: For $t\in (0,T)$, consider

$\dot{x}(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t)\\ x(0)=x_{0}$

where $A \in \mathbb R^{n \times n}$, $B\in \mathbb R^{n \times m}$, $T >0$.

If

$$
\forall x_{0},x_{1}\in \mathbb R^{n} ,\ \ \exists u(\cdot)\in L^{2}(0,t;\mathbb R^{m}),\ \ s.t.\ \ x(T)=x_{1},
$$

then the system is said to be **exactly controllable on** $(0,T)$. One can then define the controllability operator $\mathcal L_{c}:L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb R^{m})\rightarrow \mathbb R^{n}$, $u \mapsto \int_0^Te^{A(T-s)}Bu(s)ds$; the definition above amounts to the controllability operator being [surjective](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%BB%A1%E5%B0%84&zhida_source=entity). It is difficult to directly determine whether an [operator](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=3&q=%E7%AE%97%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity) is surjective; however, one can instead study its adjoint operator. An operator is surjective if and only if its adjoint is [injective](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%95%E5%B0%84&zhida_source=entity) and has a lower bound (this always holds in [finite dimensions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=5&q=%E6%9C%89%E9%99%90%E7%BB%B4&zhida_source=entity); in infinite dimensions one needs the [open mapping](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BC%80%E6%98%A0%E5%B0%84&zhida_source=entity) and closed graph theorems). In fact, by analogy with [linear algebra](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity), this is like not wanting to check whether $A$ has full rank in $Ax=b$, but instead considering whether $A^Ty=0$ has only the zero solution. Introducing the [adjoint operator](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E4%BC%B4%E9%9A%8F%E7%AE%97%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity):

$$
\begin{aligned} <\mathcal L_{c}u,v>_{\mathbb R^{n}} &=\int_0^T<e^{A(t-s)}Bu(s),v>_{\mathbb R^{n}}ds\\ &=\int_0^T(e^{A(t-s)}Bu(s))^{*}vds\\ &=\int_0^T<u(s),B^{*}e^{A^{*}(t-s)}v>_{\mathbb R^{m}}ds\\ &=<u,\mathcal L_c^{*}v>_{L^{2}} \end{aligned}
$$

Setting $z(t)=e^{A^*t}v$, we obtain the [adjoint equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AF%B9%E5%81%B6%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) (in which there is no control term $u$):

$$
\dot z(t)=A^{*}z(t) \\ y(t)=B^*z(t)\\ z(0)=z_{0}=v
$$

The problem of solving equations is essentially a problem of comparing the [ranges](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%80%BC%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity) of two operators. In the linear case, we have the following **range comparison theorem**: Let $W,V,Z$ be Banach spaces with $W$ reflexive. If $F\in \mathcal L(V,Z)$ and $G\in \mathcal L(W,Z)$, then for every $z^{*}\in Z^{*}$, there exists $c>0$ such that $||F^{*}z^{*}||_{V^{*}}\leq c||G^{*}z^{*}||_{W^{*}}$ if and only if $R(F)\subseteq R(G)$, where $R$ denotes the range of an operator. Using this theorem, the problem of comparing ranges is again transformed into a kind of [a priori estimate](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%88%E9%AA%8C%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1&zhida_source=entity).

**Proposition**: We can obtain a controllability criterion: by the range comparison theorem, exact controllability of the [linear equation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%96%B9%E7%A8%8B&zhida_source=entity) is equivalent to the solution of the adjoint equation satisfying an observability a priori estimate:

$$
\forall z_{0}\in \mathbb R^{n}, \ \   \exists c>0,\ \ ||z_{0}||^{2}_{\mathbb R^{n}}\leq  c \int _0^T||B^{*}z(s)||^{2}_{\mathbb R^{m}}ds
$$

The total energy of the initial state is completely controlled by the observation information (the quantity inside the integral norm on the right is in fact the output/observation obtained by introducing the adjoint equation).

Proof: $\mathcal L_{c}$ is surjective if and only if there exists $c>0$ such that for all $v\in \mathbb R^n$, $||\mathcal L_c^*v||_{L^2}\geq c||v||_{\mathbb R^{n}}$; this follows from the range comparison theorem together with the Riesz representation theorem.

$$
\begin{align}  ||\mathcal L_c^*v||_{L^2} &=\int _0^T||B^*e^{A^*(T-s)}v||^2_{\mathbb R^m}ds\\  &=\int_0^T||B^*e^{A^*\tau}v||^2_{\mathbb R^m}d\tau\\  &=\int_0^T||B^*z(\tau)||^2_{\mathbb R^m}d\tau\\    \end{align}
$$

**Proposition**: From this we obtain the [Kalman rank condition](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Kalman%E7%A7%A9%E6%9D%A1%E4%BB%B6&zhida_source=entity): the system is controllable on $(0,T)$ if and only if $rank(B,AB,A^{2}B,\cdots,A^{n-1}B)=n$.

Proof: Using the compactness of the unit ball in $\mathbb R^{n}$ (finite-dimensional), the observability a priori estimate above is equivalent to:

"if $B^{*}z(t)\equiv 0, \ \ t\in(0,T)$, then $z_{0}=0$."

Then, examining the arbitrary [order derivatives](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%98%B6%E5%AF%BC%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) of $B^{*}z(t)$ being zero (and then, by the Hamilton–Cayley theorem, it suffices to consider derivatives of order $0$ through $n-1$), we obtain $B^{*}(A^*)^kv=0, \ \ k=0,\cdots,n-1$. That is, $v \perp span\{B, AB,\cdots,A^{n-1}B\}$. Then, since $v=0$, the necessary and sufficient condition follows.

Controllability for [nonlinear ODEs](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7ODE&zhida_source=entity) is much more complicated, and results on global controllability are scarce. For certain special problems in [nonlinear systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity), one can give a Lie-algebraic rank condition for controllability of nonlinear ODEs, analogous to the Kalman rank condition above. Controllability for PDE systems is also not simple, and the conclusions vary considerably; details are given later.

### 2. Observability

Consider a linear system whose structure is dual to the adjoint equation: $\dot{x}(t) = A x(t)$, $y(t) = C x(t)$, where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, $C \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n}$, and $x(0) = x_0$.

If any initial state can be uniquely determined by the observations on $(0,T)$, i.e.,

$$
C e^{A t} x_0^{(1)} = C e^{A t} x_0^{(2)} \ \forall t \in (0,T) \Rightarrow x_0^{(1)} = x_0^{(2)},
$$

then the system is observable on $[0,T]$. Equivalently,

$$
C e^{A t} x_0 = 0 \ \forall t \in (0,T) \Rightarrow x_0 = 0.
$$

Define the observability operator $L_o: \mathbb{R}^n \to L^2(0,T;\mathbb{R}^p)$ by $(L_o x_0)(t) = C e^{A t} x_0$, $t \in (0,T)$. Observability is equivalent to this operator being injective.

The adjoint operator $L_o^*: L^2(0,T;\mathbb{R}^p) \to \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies $\langle L_o x_0, y \rangle_{L^2} = \langle x_0, L_o^* y \rangle_{\mathbb{R}^n}$.

Computing the left-hand side:

$$
\langle L_o x_0, y \rangle_{L^2} = \int_0^T (C e^{A t} x_0)^* y(t) \, dt = x_0^* \int_0^T e^{A^* t} C^* y(t) \, dt.
$$

Therefore, $L_o^* y = \int_0^T e^{A^* t} C^* y(t) \, dt.$

The observability Gramian is defined as

$$
W_o(T) = L_o^* L_o = \int_0^T e^{A^* t} C^* C e^{A t} dt,
$$

an $n \times n$ symmetric positive semidefinite matrix. In controllability, we characterized surjectivity via a lower bound estimate of the adjoint operator. For injectivity, one can directly verify that injectivity holds if and only if there exists a constant $c > 0$ such that the observability inequality holds:

$$
\| L_o x_0 \|_{L^2}^2 \geq c \| x_0 \|^2, \quad \forall x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n.
$$

Using the compactness of the unit ball (a finite-dimensional property), the above is equivalent to:

$$
\| L_o x_0 \|_{L^2}^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x_0 = 0.
$$

Similarly, we have the Kalman rank condition:

$$
\text{rank} \begin{bmatrix} C \\ C A \\ \vdots \\ C A^{n-1} \end{bmatrix} = n.
$$

### 3. LQR Optimal Control

We now consider the LQR (linear quadratic regulator) problem for linear systems, with the performance index:

$$
J(u(\cdot)) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^T \left[ \langle Qx(t), x(t) \rangle + \langle Ru(t), u(t) \rangle \right] dt + \frac{1}{2}\langle Fx(T), x(T) \rangle,
$$

where $u(\cdot) \in L^2(0,T;\mathbb{R}^m)$, and $Q = Q^*$, $R = R^*$, $F = F^*$. If

$$
J(\bar{u}(\cdot)) = \inf_{u(\cdot) \in L^2(0,T;\mathbb{R}^m)} J(u(\cdot)),
$$

then $\bar{u}(\cdot) \in L^2(0,T;\mathbb{R}^m)$ is called the optimal control, and $\bar{x}(\cdot) = x(\cdot; x_0, \bar{u}(\cdot))$ is the optimal trajectory.

How do we characterize the optimal control? For the LQR problem above, this can be readily solved under suitable smoothness assumptions. By perturbing (taking variations of) the optimal control, we obtain a variational equation. For arbitrary $\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}$ and $u(\cdot) \in L^2(0,T;\mathbb{R}^m)$, write

$$
u^{\varepsilon}(\cdot) = \bar{u}(\cdot) + \varepsilon u(\cdot), \quad x^{\varepsilon}(\cdot) = x(\cdot; x_0, u^{\varepsilon}(\cdot)) = \bar{x}(\cdot) + \varepsilon r(\cdot),
$$

where $r$ satisfies $\dot{r}(t) = Ar(t) + Bu(t)$, $r(0) = r_0$.

From

$$
J(x^{\varepsilon}(\cdot)) = J(\bar{x}(\cdot)) + \varepsilon \left( \int_0^T \left[ \langle Q\bar{x}(t), r(t) \rangle + \langle R\bar{u}(t), u(t) \rangle \right] dt + \langle F\bar{x}(T), r(T) \rangle \right) + O(\varepsilon^2) \geq J(\bar{x}(\cdot)),
$$

we obtain:

$$
\int_0^T \left[ \langle Q\bar{x}(t), r(t) \rangle + \langle R\bar{u}(t), u(t) \rangle \right] dt + \langle F\bar{x}(T), r(T) \rangle = 0.
$$

Introducing a "backward" ODE:

$$
\dot{\psi}(t) = -A^* \psi(t) + Q\bar{x}(t), \quad \psi(T) = -F\bar{x}(T),
$$

we can derive the equation characterizing the optimal control. Computing $\frac{d}{dt}\langle r(t), \psi(t) \rangle$ and substituting the dynamics of $r$ and $\psi$ yields, for all $u(\cdot) \in L^1(0,T;\mathbb{R}^m)$,

$$
\int_0^T \left[ \langle Q\bar{x}(t), r(t) \rangle + \langle B^* \psi(t), u(t) \rangle \right] dt = -\langle F\bar{x}(T), r(T) \rangle.
$$

Comparing the two equations above, we obtain $R\bar{u}(t) = -B^* \psi(t)$. If $R$ is invertible, the optimal control input can be solved explicitly.

### 4. Optimal Control of Nonlinear ODEs

Consider the controlled nonlinear ODE system in the general case: for $a.e. \, t \in (0,T)$,

$$
\dot{x}(t) = f(t, x(t), u(t)), \quad x(0) = x_0,
$$

with the performance index

$$
J(u(\cdot)) = \int_0^T l(t, x(t), u(t)) dt + h(x(T)).
$$

First define a set of measurable functions as the admissible control set:

$$
\mathcal{U}_{ad} = \{ u(\cdot): [0,T] \to \mathcal{U} \mid u(\cdot) \text{ is measurable} \}, \quad \mathcal{U} \subset \mathbb{R}^m.
$$

Within the admissible control set, the control minimizing the performance index is defined as the optimal control.

For the optimal pair, there is a first-order necessary condition—the Pontryagin Maximum Principle.

If $(\bar{x}, \bar{u})$ is an optimal pair and the costate $p: [0,T] \to \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies, for $a.e. \, t \in [0,T]$,

$$
\dot{p}(t) = l_x(t, \bar{x}(t), \bar{u}(t)) - f_x^T(t, \bar{x}(t), \bar{u}(t)) p(t), \quad p(T) = -h_x(\bar{x}(T)),
$$

then for the Hamiltonian $H(t, x, u, p) = \langle p, f(t, x, u) \rangle - l(t, x, u)$, which acts as the conjugate function, we have

$$
H(t, \bar{x}(t), \bar{u}(t), p(t)) = \max_{u \in \mathcal{U}} H(t, \bar{x}(t), u, p(t)).
$$

Regarding this principle, the following points are worth noting:

1) The needle variation technique: this is the key to the proof. It perturbs the optimal control $\bar{u}$ on a Lebesgue null set.

![](/blog-assets/zhihu/671215828/image-02.jpg)

2) One may add terminal constraints $x(T) = x_1$. In this case, using Ekeland's variational principle, the problem can be transformed into a family of (approximately) unconstrained optimal control problems. In finite dimensions, taking the limit yields the corresponding Pontryagin Maximum Principle without difficulty.

However, without terminal constraints, Pontryagin's original proof is extremely complex and lengthy—about forty to fifty pages—and it took over 38 years before it was simplified. Modern expositions tend to add terminal constraints and then prove the principle in finite dimensions using Ekeland's variational principle.

3) For general state constraints, satisfactory results are not yet available. Since the 1970s, many researchers such as A.A. Agrachev, R.W. Brockett, and H.J. Sussman have studied the Pontryagin Maximum Principle when the state space is a general manifold, but most of the literature assumes these manifolds have no boundary.

### 5. Kalman–Bucy Filtering (Continuous Least Squares)

Consider a continuous-time linear system, which differs from the adjoint equation by an additional term $n(t)$:

$$
\dot{x}(t) = A(t)x(t), \quad y(t) = C(t)x(t) + n(t), \quad t \in [t_0, t_1].
$$

Define $\Phi(t, t_0) = e^{A(t-t_0)}$ as the state transition matrix. Given measurement data $y(\cdot)$, we estimate the initial state $x(t_0)$. Without the additional term, $y(t) = L_o x_0$. The observation uniquely determines $x_0$ if and only if $L_o$ is injective, i.e., the system is observable.

With the additional term, we seek $x_0$ that minimizes the error between the predicted output and the actual measurement:

$$
\min_{x_0} \| y - L_o x_0 \|_{L^2}^2.
$$

The solution is

$$
\hat{x}_0 = (L_o^* L_o)^{-1} L_o^* y = W_o^{-1}(t_0, t_1) \int_0^T e^{A^* t} C^* y(t) \, dt,
$$

with

$$
W_o(t_0, t_1) = L_o^* L_o = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \Phi^*(s, t_0) C^*(s) C(s) \Phi(s, t_0) ds.
$$

The key to the existence of this solution is the invertibility of the Gramian, which is equivalent to observability of the system. Therefore, observability is a prerequisite for the Kalman filter to uniquely determine the initial state.

$$
\hat{x}(t) = \Phi(t, t_0) \hat{x}_0 = \Phi(t, t_0) W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) \int_{t_0}^{t} \Phi^*(s, t_0) C^*(s) y(s) ds, \quad t \in [t_0, t_1].
$$

**(1) Filter Dynamics**

Differentiating $\hat{x}(t)$ yields the dynamics of the Kalman–Bucy filter, where the Kalman gain is computed via the continuous-time differential Riccati equation, and the existence of solutions to the Riccati equation again depends on observability.

Let $I(t) = \int_{t_0}^{t} \Phi^T(\tau, t_0) C^T(\tau) y(\tau) d\tau$. Then $\frac{dI(t)}{dt} = \Phi^T(t, t_0) C^T(t) y(t)$.

$$
\begin{aligned} \frac{d\hat{x}(t)}{dt} &= \underbrace{\frac{d\Phi(t, t_0)}{dt} W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) I(t)}_{\text{Term 1}} + \underbrace{\Phi(t, t_0) \frac{d}{dt}[W_o^{-1}(t_0, t)] I(t)}_{\text{Term 2}} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{\Phi(t, t_0) W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) \frac{dI(t)}{dt}}_{\text{Term 3}} \end{aligned}
$$

Computing each term separately:

$$
\text{Term 1} = A(t)\Phi(t, t_0) W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) I(t) = A(t) \hat{x}(t),
$$

$$
\begin{aligned} \text{Term 2} &= \Phi(t, t_0) \left[ -W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) \Phi^T(t, t_0) C^T(t) C(t) \Phi(t, t_0) W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) \right] I(t) \\ &= -\Phi(t, t_0) W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) \Phi^T(t, t_0) C^T(t) C(t) \Phi(t, t_0) W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) I(t) \\ &= -\Phi(t, t_0) W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) \Phi^T(t, t_0) C^T(t) C(t) \hat{x}(t), \end{aligned}
$$

$$
\text{Term 3} = \Phi(t, t_0) W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) \Phi^T(t, t_0) C^T(t) y(t).
$$

Define

$$
P(t) = \Phi(t, t_0) W_o^{-1}(t_0, t) \Phi^T(t, t_0).
$$

Then

$$
\text{Term 2} = -P(t) C^T(t) C(t) \hat{x}(t), \quad \text{Term 3} = P(t) C^T(t) y(t).
$$

Putting it together:

$$
\dot{\hat{x}}(t) = A(t) \hat{x}(t) + P(t) C^T(t) \left[ y(t) - C(t) \hat{x}(t) \right].
$$

Defining the Kalman gain $K(t) = P(t) C^T(t)$, we obtain:

$$
\dot{\hat{x}}(t) = A(t) \hat{x}(t) + K(t) [y(t) - C(t) \hat{x}(t)].
$$

This is precisely the form of a Luenberger observer.

**(2) Kalman Gain Computation**

$$
\dot{P}(t) = \dot{\Phi}(t, t_0) W_o^{-1} \Phi^T + \Phi \frac{d}{dt} W_o^{-1} \Phi^T + \Phi W_o^{-1} \dot{\Phi}^T.
$$

Substituting the individual terms:

$$
\dot{\Phi}(t, t_0) = A(t)\Phi(t, t_0), \quad \dot{\Phi}^T(t, t_0) = \Phi^T(t, t_0) A^T(t), \quad \frac{d}{dt} W_o^{-1} = -W_o^{-1} \Phi^T C^T C \Phi W_o^{-1},
$$

we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned} \dot{P}(t) &= A(t)\Phi W_o^{-1} \Phi^T - \Phi W_o^{-1} \Phi^T C^T C \Phi W_o^{-1} \Phi^T + \Phi W_o^{-1} \Phi^T A^T(t) \\ &= A(t) P(t) - P(t) C^T(t) C(t) P(t) + P(t) A^T(t). \end{aligned}
$$

This yields the Riccati equation

$$
\dot{P}(t) = A(t) P(t) + P(t) A^T(t) - P(t) C^T(t) C(t) P(t).
$$

The initial value satisfies $W_o(t_0, t_0) = 0$, so $P(t_0)$ is undefined.

Define

$$
Q(t) = P^{-1}(t) = \Phi^{-T}(t, t_0) W_o(t_0, t) \Phi^{-1}(t, t_0).
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned} \dot{Q} &= -Q \dot{P} Q = -Q ( A P + P A^T - P C^T C P ) Q \\ &= -Q A P Q - Q P A^T Q + Q P C^T C P Q \\ &= -Q A - A^T Q + C^T C. \end{aligned}
$$

With the initial value

$$
Q(t_0) = \lim_{t \to t_0^+} P^{-1}(t) = 0,
$$

this is a differential Lyapunov equation. Finally:

$$
\begin{cases} \dot{\hat{x}}(t) = A(t) \hat{x}(t) + K(t) [y(t) - C(t) \hat{x}(t)], \\ K(t) = P(t) C^T(t) = Q^{-1}(t) C^T(t), \\ \dot{Q}(t) = -Q(t) A(t) - A^T(t) Q(t) + C^T(t) C(t), \quad Q(t_0) = 0. \end{cases}
$$

## 3. Deterministic Distributed Parameter System Control Theory

Originating in the 1960s, early work was primarily carried out by H. O. Fattorini, J. L. Lions, and D. L. Russell, among others.

Over the past five decades (from the 1960s to the 2010s), this area of theory developed rapidly, with dozens of monographs and numerous papers published.

To date, the most successful approach in distributed parameter control theory has been the functional analysis method, though its drawback is that the conditions it yields are often difficult to verify concretely.

In the last one to two decades (early 21st century), researchers have increasingly turned to modern PDE techniques to study distributed parameter control systems.

### **1. Controllability Problems**

Consider the controllability of the following linear system on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. The distinction from lumped parameter control systems lies in the fact that $A$ is an [unbounded linear operator](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%97%A0%E7%95%8C%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%AE%97%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity). The underlying spaces $X, U$ are corresponding Hilbert spaces, and $A$ generates a $C_0$-semigroup on $X$. For $t \in (0, T)$, consider:

$\dot{x}(t) = Ax(t) + Bu(t)$
$x(0) = x_0$

where $B \in \mathcal{L}(U, X)$.

Although exact controllability can be defined by analogy with the finite-dimensional case, when

$$
\dim Y = \infty
$$

exact controllability often fails to hold, necessitating a relaxation of this requirement. Concepts such as approximate controllability and null controllability are then introduced. The above concerns the linear case; analogous concepts exist for nonlinear distributed parameter systems.

A substantial body of work exists on the controllability of PDEs. Classical references include:

D. L. Russell (SIAM Rev., 1978);
J. L. Lions (SIAM Rev., 1988);

More recent references (as of the lecture) include:

J. M. Coron (Control and nonlinearity, 2007);
T. T. Li (Controllability and observability for quasilinear hyperbolic systems, 2010);
E. Zuazua (Controllability and observability of partial differential equations: some results and open problems, 2006).

Similarly, by the range comparison theorem, the null controllability of the above linear control system is equivalent to the following adjoint equation:

$$
\dot{z}(t) = A^{*}z(t) \\
y(t) = B^{*}z(t) \\
z(0) = z_0 = v
$$

whose solution must satisfy the following observability estimate:

$$
\forall z_0 \in X, \quad \exists c > 0, \quad \|z_0\|_{X}^{2} \leq c \int_0^T \|B^{*}z(s)\|_{U}^{2} ds
$$

Similar to the adjoint equations discussed earlier, there is again no control term $u$ here. However, unlike the finite-dimensional case, even linear problems in infinite dimensions are highly complex. Except in very special cases, establishing observability estimates for specific PDEs is far from straightforward. Consider the heat equation, a simple PDE, as an example:

$$
x_t - \Delta x = 0 \quad \text{in } (0,T) \times G \\
x = 0 \quad \text{on } [0,T] \times \partial G \\
x(0) = x_0 \quad \text{in } G
$$

where $G$ is a bounded domain in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. Its internal observability estimate is as follows (with $G_0$ being a [nonempty open subset](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=237289113&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%A9%BA%E5%BC%80%E5%AD%90%E9%9B%86&zhida_source=entity) of $G$):

$$
\forall x_0 \in L^2(G), \quad \exists c > 0, \quad \|x(T)\|_{L^2(G)} \leq c\|x\|_{L^2([0,T] \times G_0)}
$$

That is, the energy at time $T$ must be controlled by the energy of the solution on the small subdomain $G_0$. This estimate was proved for the case $n = 1$ by H. O. Fattorini and D. L. Russell in 1971, while the result for $n \geq 1$ was independently obtained by G. Lebeau and L. Robbiano in 1995, and by A. V. Fursikov and U. Yu. Imanuvilov in 1996. The version where $G_0$ is merely a Lebesgue-measurable subset of $G$ was only established recently (relative to the lecture) by G. Wang et al.

Common methods for establishing observability estimates include:

① Wave equation: spectral methods, Rellich-type multiplier techniques, microlocal analysis, global Carleman estimates;
② Heat equation: spectral methods, global Carleman estimates, etc.

### **2. Optimal Control Problems**

Classical reference: X. Li and J. Yong, Optimal control theory for infinite-dimensional systems, 1995.

The essential difference between optimal control of infinite-dimensional systems and the finite-dimensional case is that, even when terminal constraints are present, counterexamples show that the Pontryagin-type maximum principle generally fails to hold. This was already recognized in the late 1960s and early 1970s. To address this, **finite codimensionality conditions** were introduced; however, these conditions are highly abstract, and there is currently no general method for verifying them in concrete problems.

The finite codimensionality condition can be understood as follows: the solution to the PDE may not be unique, but the solution space is at most finite-dimensional. In PDE theory, uniqueness is relatively easy to achieve, but proving the structure of the solution space requires much finer tools and is very difficult. In the more than two decades since the aforementioned classical reference appeared (now nearly 30 years as of this lecture), progress on this issue has been limited.

For time optimal control theory of the heat equation, G. Wang and others have produced a series of refined studies in recent years.
