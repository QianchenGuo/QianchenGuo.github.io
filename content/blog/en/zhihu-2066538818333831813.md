## 1. Sub-Riemann Structures and Horizontal Paths

A Sub-Riemann structure is a manifold equipped with a distribution; contact structures can be seen as a special case (where the distribution is of codimension 1). Consequently, it connects with nonholonomic systems subject to velocity band constraints (e.g., a billiard ball rolling on a plane, whose velocity is constrained by a nonholonomic constraint represented by a rank-3 subbundle), adiabatic processes in thermodynamics, optimal control theory, and optimal transport problems. The metric in Sub-Riemannian geometry is typically called the Carnot–Carathéodory metric (C-C metric). The term "Carnot–Carathéodory metric" originates with Gromov, as it appears (in a more general form) in Carathéodory's 1909 paper on the formalization of classical thermodynamics, where horizontal paths roughly correspond to adiabatic processes. Here, we recall that adiabatic, quasi-static changes of state of a system can be represented by curves satisfying the Pfaffian equation $d\varepsilon+dA=0$.

### 1. Definitions

**Definition.** A Sub-Riemannian structure on $M$ is given by a pair $(\Delta,g)$, where $\Delta$ is a completely nonholonomic distribution and $g$ is a smooth Riemannian metric on $\Delta$.

One way to obtain a Sub-Riemannian structure is to restrict the metric of a Riemannian manifold to a completely nonholonomic distribution. A completely nonholonomic distribution is a non-integrable distribution; Montgomery calls it bracket-generating, Bellaïche calls it nonholonomic, and Agrachev and Sachkov call it a completely nonholonomic family. Specifically, for any family of smooth vector fields $\mathscr{F}$ on an open set, let $Lie^1(\mathscr{F})$ be the space spanned by $\mathscr{F}$ in $\mathscr{X}^\infty(M)$, and define

$$
Lie^{k+1}(\mathscr{F})=\mathrm{Span}\big(Lie^k(\mathscr{F})\cup\{[X,Y],\ X\in\mathscr{F},\ Y\in Lie^K(\mathscr{F})\}\big).
$$

This defines an increasing sequence of vector spaces in $\mathscr{X}^\infty(M)$ satisfying $Lie(\mathscr{F})=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}Lie^k(\mathscr{F}).$ We say that smooth vector fields $X^1,\dots ,X^m$ satisfy the Hörmander condition on an open set $\mathscr{O}\subset M$ if and only if

$$
Lie \{X^1,\dots,X^m\}(x)=T_xM,\quad x\in\mathscr{O}.
$$

The distribution $\Delta$ is called completely nonholonomic if, for each point $x\in M$, there exists an open neighborhood $\mathscr{V}_x$ and a local frame $X^1,\dots ,X^m$ on it satisfying the Hörmander condition. We call the (nonholonomic) degree of $\Delta$ at $x$ the smallest integer

$$
r=r(x)\geq 1
$$

such that

$$
Lie ^r\{X^1,\dots,X^m\}(x)=T_xM
$$

. These definitions do not depend on the choice of local frame.

For a Sub-Riemannian structure, we are naturally concerned with curves that satisfy the constraint, i.e., curves whose velocities always lie in the given distribution. A continuous path $\gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow M$ is called a horizontal path with respect to $\Delta$ if it is absolutely continuous, its derivative is square-integrable, and it satisfies

$$
\dot{\gamma}(t)\in\Delta(\gamma(t)),\quad  t\in[0,T]a.e.
$$

Let $\Omega_\Delta^{x,T}$ denote the set of horizontal paths starting at $x$. One observation is that if $\Delta$ admits a global frame, then there is a one-to-one correspondence between $\Omega_\Delta^{x,T}$ and an open subset $U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}$ of $L^2([0,T],\mathbb{R}^m)$. Specifically, the map $u\in U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}\mapsto \gamma_u\in\Omega_\Delta^{x,T}$ is bijective, where $\gamma_u$ is the solution of the following Cauchy problem:

$$
\dot{\gamma}_u(t)=\sum_{i=1}^m u_i(t)X^i(\gamma_u(t)),\quad t\in [0,T] a.e.,\quad \gamma_u(0)=x.
$$

In fact, any horizontal path can be viewed as a trajectory associated with some control

$$
u\in U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}
$$

. Our focus is on two aspects: the singularity of horizontal paths, and the existence of horizontal paths between any two points.

### 2. Regular and Singular Horizontal Paths

Let $\Delta$ be a distribution that can be represented by a generating family $\mathscr{F}=\{X^1,\dots,X^k\}$ of smooth vector fields (not necessarily linearly independent). The singularity of a horizontal path is distinguished by whether the tangent map of the endpoint map degenerates. Let $\Phi_{\mathscr{F}}^u(t,x)$ denote the flow of the time-dependent vector field $X_{\mathscr{F}}^u(t,x):=\sum_{i=1}^k u_i(t)X^i(x)$. The endpoint map is defined as $E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}:U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}\rightarrow M,\quad u\mapsto\gamma_u(T).$ This map is of class $C^1$ on $U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}$, and its differential $D_uE_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}:L^2([0,T],\mathbb{R}^k)\rightarrow T_{E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}(u)}M$ is given by:

$$
D_uE_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}(v)=S_u(T)\int_0^T S_u(t)^{-1}B_u(t)v(t)dt.
$$

Here, $S_u(t)$ is the Jacobian matrix of the flow $\Phi_{\mathscr{F}}^u(t,x)$ with respect to $x$, and $B_u$ is the matrix whose columns are $X^i(\gamma_u(t))$. Given $u\in U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}$, we set

$$
\mathrm{Im}_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}(u):=D_uE_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}\big(L^2([0,T],\mathbb{R}^k)\big)\subset T_{E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}(u)}M.
$$

Let $\mathrm{rank}_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}(u)$ denote the dimension of $\mathrm{Im}_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}(u)$. If $\mathrm{rank}_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}(u)=n$, then the control $u$ is regular; otherwise, $u$ is singular. When $u$ is singular, it is a critical point of the endpoint map $E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}$. Correspondingly, viewing a horizontal path as a trajectory associated with the control $u$, the singularity of the horizontal path $\gamma_u$ is defined by the singularity of the control $u$. For a regular horizontal path, the linear map $D_uE_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}(\cdot)$ is surjective.

An important proposition for determining the singularity of horizontal paths is the following:

**Proposition.** Define the Hamiltonians $h^1,\dots,h^k$ as

$$
h^i(\psi)=p\cdot X^i(x),\quad \forall \psi=(x,p)\in T^*M.
$$

Let $\vec{h}_i$ denote their Hamiltonian vector fields. The control $u\in U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}$ is singular if and only if there exists an absolutely continuous arc (called an abnormal extremal lift) $\psi:[0,T]\rightarrow T^*M$, which never intersects the zero section (i.e., $p(t)\neq 0$), such that

$$
\begin{aligned} &\text{(S1)}\quad \dot{\psi}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_i(t)\vec{h}^i(\psi(t)),\quad t\in [0,T]a.e.\\ &\text{(S2)}\quad h^i(\psi(t))=0,\quad \forall t\in [0,T]. \end{aligned}
$$

Clearly, in local coordinates, the above proposition states that there exists an absolutely continuous arc $p:[0,T]\rightarrow (\mathbb{R}^n)^*\setminus\{0\}$ satisfying

$$
\begin{aligned} &\text{(S1')}\quad \dot{p}(t)=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_i(t)\,p(t)\cdot D_{\gamma_u(t)}X^i,\quad  t\in [0,T]a.e.\\ &\text{(S2')}\quad p(t)\cdot X^i(\gamma_u(t))=0,\quad \forall t\in [0,T]. \end{aligned}
$$

Applying the above proposition directly yields the following conclusion: Consider a two-dimensional completely nonholonomic distribution $\Delta$ on a three-dimensional manifold $M$. Using (S1) and (S2), a direct computation shows that the singular paths are exactly the horizontal paths contained in $\Sigma_\Delta$. Here, $\Sigma_\Delta$ is called the Martinet surface, defined as

$$
\Sigma_\Delta:=\{x\in M,\ \Delta(x)+[\Delta,\Delta](x)\neq T_xM\}.
$$

In other words, $x\in\Sigma_\Delta$ if and only if $\Delta$ is not a contact distribution at $x$. The distribution $\Delta$ is called fat if, for every point $x$ and every section $X$ of $\Delta$ with $X(x)\neq 0$, we have $T_xM=\Delta(x)+[X,\Delta](x).$ By definition, in this case $\Sigma_\Delta=\emptyset$, so singular horizontal paths are just constant curves. Fat distributions contain no nontrivial singular horizontal paths.

Consider a two-dimensional completely nonholonomic distribution $\Delta$ on a four-dimensional manifold $M$. If the dimension of $\Delta(x)+[\Delta,\Delta](x)$ is 3, and

$$
T_xM=\Delta(x)+[\Delta,\Delta](x)+[\Delta,[\Delta,\Delta]](x),\quad \forall x\in M,
$$

then there exists a smooth line field (a one-dimensional distribution) $L\subset\Delta$ such that the singular paths are exactly the integral curves of $L$. For example, consider $X^1=\partial_{x_1},\ X^2=\partial_{x_2}+x_1\partial_{x_3}+x_3\partial_{x_4}$ on $\mathbb{R}^4$; the singular paths are of the form $x(t)=(x_1(t),x_2(0),x_3(0),x_4(0))$.

### **3\. [Chow–Rashevsky Theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%91%A8%E2%80%93Rashevsky%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity)**

The Chow–Rashevsky theorem, proved independently by [Wei-Liang Chow](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%91%A8%E7%82%9C%E8%89%AF&zhida_source=entity) and Rashevsky in the 1930s, can be regarded as the first major theorem in sub-Riemannian geometry. This theorem guarantees the existence of a horizontal path between any two points on a sub-Riemannian manifold, which allows us to introduce a notion of distance as in Riemannian geometry, and thereby discuss minimizing curves and [geodesics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B5%8B%E5%9C%B0%E7%BA%BF&zhida_source=entity), and subsequently consider the existence of minimizing geodesics between any two points, analogous to the Riemannian case (the [Hopf–Rinow theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Hopf-Rinow%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity)). The proof we present here follows Bellaïche's proof of the so-called [orbit theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BD%A8%E9%81%93%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity). To prove the existence of a horizontal path between any two points, it suffices to show that for $x\in M$, its attainable set is the entire manifold $M$. Specifically, let $\mathscr{A}_{\mathscr{F}}(x,T)$ denote the set of points in $M$ reachable from $x$ via some control $u$, i.e., $\mathscr{A}_{\mathscr{F}}(x,T)=E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}(U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T})$. Our strategy here is to prove that this [attainable set](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E7%9B%AE%E6%A0%87%E9%9B%86&zhida_source=entity) is both open and closed, and nonempty.

To prove that $\mathscr{A}_{\mathscr{F}}(x,T)$ is open, we need the following lemma: **Lemma.** The endpoint map $E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}:U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}\rightarrow M$ is an [open map](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BC%80%E6%98%A0%E5%B0%84&zhida_source=entity).

The proof proceeds as follows: **Step 1.** For each $\varepsilon>0$, let

$$
d(\varepsilon)=\max\{\mathrm{rank}_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}(u),\ u\in U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}\ \text{satisfying}\ \|u\|_2<\varepsilon\}.
$$

Note that this is integer-valued and nondecreasing; hence there exists $\varepsilon_0$ such that $d(\varepsilon)=d_0\geq 1,\ \forall \varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_0)$. Fix $\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_0)$ and $u^\varepsilon\in U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}$ satisfying $\|u^\varepsilon\|_2<\varepsilon$. Since the rank of $D_{u^\varepsilon}E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}$ is $d_0$, there exist an open neighborhood $\mathscr{V}$ of $0\in\mathbb{R}^{d_0}$ and $d_0$ controls $v^j$ such that the map

$$
\mathscr{E}:\mathscr{V}\rightarrow M,\quad \lambda\mapsto E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}\Big(u^\varepsilon+\sum_{j=1}^{d_0}\lambda^j v^j\Big)
$$

is an embedding, whose image is a $d_0$-dimensional $C^1$ [submanifold](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AD%90%E6%B5%81%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity) $N$ of $M$. Using the fact that $X^i(E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}(u))\in\mathrm{Im}_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}(u)$ together with the Hörmander condition, we obtain $d(\varepsilon)=n,\ \forall \varepsilon>0$. Therefore $N$ is indeed an [open submanifold](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BC%80%E5%AD%90%E6%B5%81%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity).

**Step 2.** There then exists $u\in U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}$ such that $\|u\|_2<\varepsilon$ and $\mathrm{rank}_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}(u)=n$ (regular). Let $\mathscr{O}$ be an open subset of $U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}$ and take $v\in\mathscr{O}$. Thus the path concatenation $\tilde{v}=u*\breve{u}*v_{T/(T-2\varepsilon)}$ is also regular, its rank being the maximum of the ranks of the individual paths. Then the map $w\mapsto E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}(\tilde{v}+w)$ sends a small ball around the origin in $L^2([0,T],\mathbb{R}^k)$ into a [small neighborhood](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B0%8F%E9%82%BB%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity) of $E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}(\tilde{v})=E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}(v)$.

**Step 3.** Verify that $\|\tilde{v}-v\|_2$ is sufficiently small when $\varepsilon$ is small enough, so that $\tilde{v}\in\mathscr{O}$. Hence $E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,T}(\mathscr{O})$ contains a [neighborhood](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=4&q=%E9%82%BB%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity) of $E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}(v)$, and $E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,\varepsilon}$ is an open map.

**Proof of the Chow–Rashevsky Theorem.** We have shown that $\mathscr{A}_{\mathscr{F}}(x,T)$ is open; we now show it is closed. Take a sequence $z_k\in \mathscr{A}_{\mathscr{F}}(x,T)\rightarrow z$ for some $z\in M$; we need to show that $z\in \mathscr{A}_{\mathscr{F}}(x,T)$. Note that $E_{\mathscr{F}}^{z,1}(0)=z$, and the set $E_{\mathscr{F}}^{z,1}(U_{\mathscr{F}}^{z,1})$ is an open neighborhood of $z$. For $k$ sufficiently large, $z_k$ lies in this neighborhood, so there exists a control $u$ such that $\gamma_u$ connects $z$ and $z_k$. Taking the path concatenation $u_k*\breve{u}$ yields a control from $x$ to $z$, so that $z\in \mathscr{A}_{\mathscr{F}}(x,T)$.

The set $\mathscr{A}_{\mathscr{F}}(x,T)$ contains $x$, hence is nonempty, and is both open and closed; since $M$ is a connected manifold, we conclude that $\mathscr{A}_{\mathscr{F}}(x,T)=M$. $\Box$

### **4\. Sub-Riemannian Distance**

We denote by $|\cdot|_x^g$ the sub-Riemannian norm on $\Delta(x)$, i.e., $|v|_x^g=\sqrt{g_x(v,v)},\ \forall v\in\Delta(x)$. The length of a horizontal path $\gamma\in\Omega_\Delta^{x,T}$ is defined as $\mathrm{length}^g(\gamma):=\int_0^T |\dot{\gamma}(t)|_{\gamma(t)}^g\,dt.$ The Chow–Rashevsky theorem guarantees that the following definition of a [distance function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B7%9D%E7%A6%BB%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) is well-posed: for any $x,y\in M$, the sub-Riemannian distance is defined as

$$
d_{SR}(x,y)=\inf\{\mathrm{length}^g(\gamma),\ \gamma\in\Omega_\Delta^{x,T}\ \text{such that}\ \gamma(1)=y\}.
$$

This distance function is also called the C–C metric, and it makes the sub-Riemannian manifold $M$ into a [metric space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BA%A6%E9%87%8F%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) $(M,d_{SR})$, known as a Carnot–Carathéodory metric space. We call the sub-Riemannian ball (C–C ball) centered at $x$ with radius $r$ the set

$$
B_{SR}(x,r)=\{y\in M,\ d_{SR}(x,y)<r\}.
$$

The openness of the endpoint map shows that the topology induced by $d_{SR}$ coincides with the original topology, since $E_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,1}(\cdot)$ yields the original topology of $M$ from the topology of $U_{\mathscr{F}}^{x,1}$ defined by $\|\cdot\|_2$. Moreover, as in the Riemannian case, if $d_{SR}(x,y)=\mathrm{length}^g(\gamma)$, we call a horizontal path $\gamma\in\Omega_\Delta^{x,T}$ connecting $x$ and $y$ a minimizing horizontal path.

## II. Minimizing Horizontal Paths and Minimizing Geodesics

The previous articles have introduced the sub-Riemannian structure; by the [Chow–Rashevsky theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Chow-Rashevsky%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity), one can define the Carnot–Carathéodory metric space $(M, d_{SR})$. Naturally, we are now concerned with minimizing horizontal paths and minimizing geodesics therein.

Our discussion focuses mainly on the following aspects:

1.  Whether a minimizing geodesic exists between two points, corresponding to the Hopf–Rinow theorem in classical Riemannian geometry.
2.  The normality of minimizing geodesics. For normal minimizing geodesics, their properties are analogous to those in the classical Riemannian case; for instance, their lifts satisfy the Hamiltonian geodesic equations, and short normal geodesics are minimizing. Indeed, this issue has been of great importance in the development of sub-Riemannian geometry: for a long time it was believed that every sub-Riemannian minimizing geodesic is normal, i.e., it admits a normal extremal lift, even if it is singular. In 1991, Montgomery (the author of the classical sub-Riemannian geometry textbook *A Tour of Subriemannian Geometries*) discovered the first counterexample (known as the [Montgomery counterexample](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Montgomery%E5%8F%8D%E4%BE%8B&zhida_source=entity)).
3.  The existence and uniqueness of normal minimizing geodesics between two points, i.e., defining the [exponential map](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8C%87%E6%95%B0%E6%98%A0%E5%B0%84&zhida_source=entity) and discussing the size of its image. On one hand, we will see that its image is open and dense, i.e., the exponential map is almost surjective; on the other hand, an open problem asks whether its image is of full measure.
4.  Conditions under which a given horizontal path is a minimizing geodesic.

### 1\. Minimizing Horizontal Paths and Minimizing Geodesics

For the metric space $(M, d_{SR})$, we can define minimizing horizontal paths. As in Riemannian geometry, constant-speed minimizing paths minimize energy; we define the SR energy

$$
e_{SR}(x, y) = \inf\{\text{energy}^g(\gamma), \gamma \in \Omega_{\Delta}^{x,1} \text{ s.t. } \gamma(1) = y\},
$$

where

$$
\text{energy}^g(\gamma) := \int_0^1 (|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^g_{\gamma(t)})^2 dt.
$$

By the Cauchy–Schwarz [inequality](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%8D%E7%AD%89%E5%BC%8F&zhida_source=entity), we have $e_{SR}(x, y) = d_{SR}(x, y)^2.$ We thereby define minimizing geodesics as those satisfying

$$
e_{SR}(x, y) = \text{energy}^g(\gamma),
$$

i.e., constant-speed minimizing horizontal paths.

As a precursor to the SR version of the Hopf–Rinow theorem, we have the following local statement, which asserts the existence of a minimizing geodesic connecting sufficiently close points.

**Proposition.** Let $x \in M$. Then there exists $\rho > 0$ such that for any points $y, z \in B_{SR}(x, \rho)$ and any sequence of constant-speed horizontal paths $\gamma^k$ satisfying $\gamma^k(0) \to y, \gamma^k(1) \to z, \text{ length}^g(\gamma^k) \to d_{SR}(y, z)$, up to passing to a subsequence, $\gamma^k$ [converges uniformly](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%80%E8%87%B4%E6%94%B6%E6%95%9B&zhida_source=entity) to some minimizing geodesic.

**Proof.** Note that locally there exists an orthonormal frame; using the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, one can show that $\gamma^k$ converges to some $\gamma$.  
Furthermore, the controls $u^k$ are bounded in $L^2$; by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, they [converge weakly](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BC%B1%E6%94%B6%E6%95%9B&zhida_source=entity) to some $\bar{v}$.  
By the lower [semicontinuity](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%8A%E8%BF%9E%E7%BB%AD&zhida_source=entity) of the $L^2$ norm under weak convergence, together with $\text{length}^g(\gamma^k) \to d_{SR}(y, z),$

we deduce

$$
\| \bar{v} \|_2 \leq \lim_{k \to \infty} \| u^k \|_2 = d_{SR}(y, z).
$$

Clearly,

$$
d_{SR}(y, z) \leq \text{length}^g(\gamma),
$$

and moreover, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

$$
\text{length}^g(\gamma) \leq \| \bar{v} \|_2 \leq d_{SR}(x, y),
$$

so we obtain

$$
\text{energy}^g(\gamma) = \| \bar{v} \|_2^2 = d_{SR}(y, z)^2 = e_{SR}(y, z),
$$

Thus the limit $\gamma$ is a minimizing geodesic connecting $y$ and $z$. □

**Theorem (Hopf–Rinow).** Assume $(M, d_{SR})$ is a complete metric space. Then for any two points on $M$, there exists a minimizing geodesic.

**Proof.** The proof relies on the following compactness property: $B_{SR}(x, r)$

is compact for every $r \geq 0$. Note that the continuity of $d_{SR}$ implies that $(M, d_{SR})$ is locally compact. Let $I_x$ be the set of $r \geq 0$ for which $B_{SR}(x, r)$ is compact. By local compactness, the supremum $R_x$ of $I_x$ satisfies $\geq 0$. We now show that $I$ is both open and closed on $[0, +\infty)$. The key to the proof is to find a Cauchy subsequence of any sequence in $B_{SR}(x, R_x)$ (which converges by [completeness](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AE%8C%E5%A4%87%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity)). The proof shows that $I$ admits a $\delta$-small cover for some $\delta > 0$, i.e., we take a finite [open cover](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BC%80%E8%A6%86%E7%9B%96&zhida_source=entity) of it. Using finite covers of $B(x, R_x)$ and its boundary $\partial B(x, R_x)$, we can choose $\delta$ small enough so as to cover all of $B(x, R_x + \delta)$.

Now we prove the theorem. Note that, unlike the local proposition, a global orthonormal frame need not exist. Let $R := \max \{ 2d_{SR}(x, y), 1 \},$ choose $\gamma^k$ such that $d_{SR}(x, y) = \lim_{k \to \infty} \text{length}(\gamma^k),$ and assume without loss of generality that $\text{length}(\gamma^k) < R,$ so that all curves $\gamma^k$ lie in the compact set $B_{SR}(x, R)$. Now, by means of an open cover, we can partition $\gamma^k$ into small segments and apply the previous proposition locally.

### 2\. Hamiltonian geodesic equations

As the second major theorem of sub-Riemannian geometry, the sub-Riemannian version of the Hopf–Rinow theorem guarantees the existence of a minimizing geodesic between any two points on a complete SR structure. We now turn to the normality of minimizing geodesics. For a normal minimizing geodesic, its normal extremal lift is a smooth Hamiltonian flow, exactly as in Riemannian geometry. Regular minimizing geodesics are necessarily normal (one can think of it this way: if the path is regular, its behavior resembles the unconstrained Riemannian case with rank

$$
k = n
$$

). For abnormal paths, they may also admit a normal lift, which is why it was once believed that all minimizing geodesics are normal.

Since $\gamma$ minimizes distance, it cannot self-intersect. Hence $(\Delta, g)$ admits a unitary frame along $\gamma$. Now consider the energy of the path. By unitarity, $e_{SR}(\gamma) = \int_0^1 \sum_{i=1}^m u_i(t)^2 dt := C(u)$ is precisely $\| u \|_2^2,$

At this point we say that the minimizing geodesic $\gamma$ with control $u$ minimizes $C(u)$ among all $u \in L^2([0, 1], \mathbb{R}^m)$ satisfying $E_{\mathcal{F}}^{\gamma,1}(u) = 1$.

By the Lagrange multiplier theorem, there exist $p \in T^*_y M \simeq (\mathbb{R}^n)^*$ and $\lambda_0 = 0$ or $1$, with $(\lambda_0, p) \neq (0, 0)$, such that

$$
p \cdot D_{u\gamma} E_{\mathcal{F}}^{\gamma,1}(v) = \lambda_0 D_{u\gamma} C(v), \quad \forall v \in L^2.
$$

(1) $\lambda_0 = 0$.  
Here $p \cdot D_{u\gamma} E_{\mathcal{F}}^{\gamma,1}(v) = 0$,  
so $D_{u\gamma} E_{\mathcal{F}}^{\gamma,1}(\cdot)$ is not of full rank, and $\gamma$ is abnormal.

(2) $\lambda_0 = 1$.  
Here the situation is the same as in Riemannian geometry.  
Since the minimizing geodesic minimizes energy, $\gamma$ admits a normal extremal lift $\psi(t) = (\gamma(t), p(t))$ in $T^* M$ satisfying $\psi(1) = (y, p/2).$ We call $\gamma$ a normal minimizing geodesic with constant speed $|\dot{\gamma}(t)|_{\gamma(t)} = \sqrt{2H(\psi(t))}.$ The normal extremal $\psi(t)$ is the integral curve of the Hamiltonian vector field generated by the Hamiltonian

$$
H(x, p) = \frac{1}{2} \max \left\{ \frac{p(v)^2}{g_x(v, v)}, v \in \Delta(x) \setminus \{0\} \right\}
$$

In local coordinates,

$$
H(x, p) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^m (p \cdot X^i(x))^2 = \max_{u \in \mathbb{R}^m} \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^m u_i p \cdot X^i(x) - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^m u_i^2 \right\},
$$

where the $\max$ is attained at $u_i = p \cdot X^i(x)$.  
Note that $v = \sum_{i=1}^m u_i X^i(x)$ (for some $u \in \mathbb{R}^m$), and locally $X^i(x)$ forms a unitary frame; using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality one readily verifies that the two definitions above coincide.

The classification above provides a first-order condition for a given horizontal path to be a minimizing geodesic. It can be viewed as a weak form of Pontryagin's maximum principle (a key component of [modern control theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8E%B0%E4%BB%A3%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity)). Again consistent with Riemannian geometry, the projection of a normal extremal is minimizing over short times. This conclusion rests on the method of characteristics for the Hamilton–Jacobi equation.

**Proposition.**  
Let $\bar{x} \in M$ and $\bar{p} \in T^*_x M$ satisfy $H(\bar{x}, \bar{p}) \neq 0$.  
Then there exist a neighborhood $\mathcal{W}$ of $\bar{p}$ in $T^*_x M$ and $\varepsilon > 0$ such that every normal extremal $\psi(t)$ with $\psi(0) = (\bar{x}, p)$ minimizes the SR energy on $[0, \varepsilon]$. Writing $\gamma = \pi \circ \psi$, we have $e_{SR}(\gamma(0), \gamma(\varepsilon)) = 2H(\bar{x}, p) \varepsilon^2.$ In particular, $\gamma$ minimizes length between $\bar{x}$ and $\gamma(\varepsilon)$.

**Proof.**  
It suffices to prove the statement in local coordinates, i.e., in $\mathbb{R}^n$.  
For the HJE $H(x, \nabla S(x)) = H(\bar{x}, p)$ with given Hamiltonian $H(x, p) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^m (p \cdot X^i(x))^2$, we can obtain a local solution via the method of characteristics.

Formally, a solution is given by $S(x) = 2H(\bar{x}, p) \cdot \tau(x)$, where $\tau(x)$ is the time required to travel from $\pi(x) \in x + \Pi$ (with $\Pi$ a linear [hyperplane](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%B6%85%E5%B9%B3%E9%9D%A2&zhida_source=entity) satisfying $\langle p, v \rangle = 0, \forall v \in \Pi$) to $x$.

$S(x_s(s)) = 2H(\bar{x}, p) \cdot s$, where $x_s(0) = x$.

Locally, the Hamilton equations generated by

$$
H
$$

have a solution

$$
(x(t), p(t)) : [0, \varepsilon] \to \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n ,\ \  x(0) = \bar{x}, \ \ p(0) = p \in \mathcal{W}.
$$

Let $y(t)$ be a horizontal path connecting $\bar{x}$ and $x(\varepsilon)$. We have

$$
S(x(\varepsilon)) - S(\bar{x}) = S(y(\varepsilon)) - S(y(0)) = \int_0^\varepsilon \frac{d}{dt} S(y(t)) dt
$$

$$
= \int_0^\varepsilon \langle \nabla S(y(t)), \dot{y}(t) \rangle dt = \int_0^\varepsilon \langle \nabla S(y(t)), \sum_{i=1}^m u_i(t) X^i(y(t)) \rangle dt,
$$

Noting that in local coordinates

$$
H(x, p) = \max_{u \in \mathbb{R}^m} \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^m u_i p \cdot X^i(x) - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^m u_i^2 \right\}
$$

the expression above satisfies

$$
\leq \int_0^\varepsilon [H(y(t), \nabla S(y(t)))] + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^m u_i(t)^2] dt = \varepsilon H(\bar{x}, p) + \int_0^\varepsilon \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^m u_i(t)^2 dt.
$$

By the formal solution of the HJE above,

$$
S(x(\varepsilon)) - S(\bar{x}) = 2H(\bar{x}, p) \varepsilon
$$

therefore

$$
2\varepsilon H(\bar{x}, p) = \int_0^\varepsilon \sum_{i=1}^m \langle p(t), X^i(x(t)) \rangle^2 \leq \int_0^\varepsilon \sum_{i=1}^m u_i(t)^2 dt = e_{SR} .
$$

### 3\. The sub-Riemannian exponential map

We first define the [domain](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AE%9A%E4%B9%89%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity) of the exponential map $\mathcal{E}_x := \{ p \in T_x^* M, \psi_{x,p} \text{ is defined on } [0, 1] \},$ and then define $\pi(\psi_{x,p}(1))$. The sub-Riemannian exponential map from $x$ is defined as

$$
\exp_x : \mathcal{E}_x \to M, p \mapsto \pi(\psi_{x,p}(1)).
$$

Note that for an abnormal path admitting an abnormal extremal lift from $(x, q)$, if it also admits a normal extremal lift from $(x, p)$, then $(p + \lambda q) \cdot D_u E_{x,p}^{\alpha,1} = p \cdot D_u E_{x,p}^{\alpha,1} = D_u C$, i.e., $\exp_x(p + \lambda q) = \exp_x(p),$ so the exponential map may fail to be injective.

For any $p \in T_x^* M$, the curve $\gamma_p(t) := \exp_x(tp) = \pi(\psi_{x,p}(t))$ is a constant-speed horizontal path satisfying

$$
\text{energy}^g(\pi(\psi_{x,p})) = (\text{length}^g(\pi(\psi_{x,p})))^2 = 2H(x, p).
$$

By an ODE argument (completeness of the phase flow for an [autonomous system](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%87%AA%E6%B2%BB%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity)), we have

**Proposition.** If $(\Delta, g)$ is complete, then $\mathcal{E} = T_x^* M$.

Naturally, we ask how large the image of the exponential map is; in fact it is an open dense set.

**Theorem.** Assume $(\Delta, g)$ is complete and fix $x \in M$. There exists an open dense set $\mathcal{D} \subset M$ such that for every $y \in \mathcal{D}$ there exists $p_y$ with $\exp_x(p_y) = y$ and $d_{SR}(x, y) = \sqrt{2H(x, p_y)} .$

**Proof.** The proof of the theorem relies on the following lemma:

**Lemma.** Let $y \neq x$ and suppose there exists a function $\phi : M \to \mathbb{R}$ differentiable at $y$, satisfying $\phi(y) = d_{SR}^2(x, y)$ and $d_{SR}^2(x, z) \geq \phi(z)$ for all $z \in M$. Then there exists a unique minimizing geodesic between $x$ and $y$. It is the projection of a normal extremal $\psi$ satisfying $\psi(1) = (y, D_y \phi / 2)$. In particular, $x = \exp_y(-D_y \phi / 2)$.

The proof of this lemma consists in noting that the control

$$
u^\gamma
$$

of the minimizing geodesic minimizes the functional

$$
D(u) := C(u) - \phi(E_{x, y}^{\gamma, 1}(u)).
$$

Setting $\lambda = D_y \phi$, we obtain $\lambda \cdot D_{u^\gamma} E_{x, y}^{\gamma, 1} - D_{u^\gamma} C = 0$, which implies that $\gamma$ admits a normal extremal lift. By the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem (uniqueness of ODEs), such an extremal is unique.

Let $\mathcal{P}_x$ denote the set of points $y$ for which there exists a unique normal minimizing geodesic from $x$ to $y$. By construction, for any fixed $r > 0$, there exists $y \in B_{SR}(x, r)$ satisfying the conditions of the lemma, so $\mathcal{P}_x$ is dense.

Furthermore, we can show that points of rank $n$ in $\mathcal{P}_x$ are also dense. If not, there would exist an open set $\mathcal{O}$ such that all points in $\mathcal{P}_x \cap \mathcal{O}$ have rank less than $n$. Let $\hat{y}$ be a point of maximal rank therein, $\text{rank}(\hat{y}) = \hat{r}$, and let $\Pi_y$ be the affine subspace such that for $p \in \Pi_y$, $\exp_x(tp) = \gamma_y(t)$, with $\gamma_y(1) = y$. By continuity of the map $y \in \mathcal{P}_x \mapsto \Pi_y$, there exists an open set $\mathcal{O}' \subset \mathcal{O}$ containing $\hat{y}$ such that a submanifold $\mathcal{S}$ of dimension $\hat{r}$ transverse to $\Pi_y$ is transverse to $\Pi_y$ for all $y \in \mathcal{O}' \cap \mathcal{P}_x$. Hence $\{y\} = \exp_x(\Pi_y) = \exp_x(\Pi_y \cap \mathcal{S}) \subset \exp_x(\mathcal{S})$, but $\dim \mathcal{S} < n$, so $\mathcal{P}_x \cap \mathcal{O}'$ is not dense in $\mathcal{O}'$, contradicting the density of $\mathcal{P}_x$.

We fix $\bar{y} \in \mathcal{P}_x$ with $\text{rank}(\bar{y}) = n$. We also note that there exists an open set containing $\bar{y}$ on which $d_{SR}(x, \cdot)$ is locally Lipschitz on $\mathcal{O}_{\bar{y}}$. Hence it is differentiable almost everywhere on $\mathcal{O}_{\bar{y}}$. By the lemma, for every $y \in \mathcal{O}_{\bar{y}}$ at which $d_{SR}(x, \cdot)$ is differentiable, there exists a unique constant-speed minimizing geodesic between $x$ and $y$, with speed given by $\sqrt{2H(x, p_y)}$. For each $y \in \mathcal{O}_{\bar{y}}$, take a sequence $y_k \to y$ of such points; by the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, up to a subsequence, the normal extremals from $(x, p_k)$ converge to a normal extremal whose projection is a geodesic connecting $x$ and $y \in \mathcal{O}_{\bar{y}}$, and $\bar{y}$ constitutes a [dense set](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A8%A0%E5%AF%86%E9%9B%86&zhida_source=entity). □

-   **The Goh condition**

The earlier discussion of the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_0 = 0$ or $1$ gave a first-order condition for a given horizontal path to be a minimizing geodesic. We now present a second-order necessary condition, known as the Goh condition. To state it, we need a series of preliminary propositions.

**Theorem (Openness).** Let $F : \mathcal{U} \to \mathbb{R}^N$ be of class $C^2$ on an open set $\mathcal{U} \subset L^2$, and let $\bar{u} \in \mathcal{U}$ be a critical point of $F$ with corank $r$. If the negative index

$$
\text{ind}_- \left( \lambda \cdot (D_{\bar{u}}^2 F) \big|_{\ker(D_{\bar{u}} F)} \right) \geq r, \quad \forall \lambda \in \text{Im}(D_{\bar{u}} F)^\perp \setminus \{0\}.
$$

then $F$ is locally open at $\bar{u}$. Here $D_{\bar{u}}^2 F : L^2 \to \mathbb{R}^N$, $\text{Im}(D_{\bar{u}} F) \subset \mathbb{R}^N$, $\lambda \in (\mathbb{R}^N)^*$, and the quadratic form

$$
\lambda \cdot (D_{\bar{u}}^2 F) \big|_{\ker(D_{\bar{u}} F)} : L^2 \to \mathbb{R}.
$$

**Theorem (Index).** Let $\bar{u}$ and $\bar{p} \in (\mathbb{R}^n)^*$ satisfy $\bar{p} \cdot D_{\bar{u}} E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1} = 0$. Assume

$$
\text{ind}_- \left( \bar{p} \cdot (D_{\bar{u}}^2 E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1}) \big|_{\ker(D_{\bar{u}} E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1})} \right) < +\infty.
$$

 Then $\bar{p}(t) := \bar{p} \cdot \bar{S}(1) \bar{S}(t)^{-1}$ satisfies $\bar{p}(t) \cdot [X^i, X^j](\gamma_{\bar{u}}(t)) = 0,$ known as the Goh condition.

**Proof Step 1.** By Taylor expansion and Gronwall's inequality, one verifies that

$$
F(u) := (E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1}(u), C(u))
$$

is of class $C^2$. Moreover, writing $\delta_x(t) := \gamma_{u+\epsilon v}(t) - \gamma_u(t)$ as $\delta_x^1(t) + \delta_x^2(t) + o(\epsilon^2),$ we obtain

$$
D_{\bar{u}}^2 E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1}(v, v) = 2 \int_0^1 \bar{S}(1) \bar{S}(t)^{-1} [\bar{C}(t) + \bar{D}(t)] dt,
$$

where

$$
C(t) := \sum_{i=1}^m v_i(t) D_{\gamma_u(t)} X^i \cdot \delta_x^1(t) \text{ and } D(t) := \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^m u_i(t) D_{\gamma_u(t)}^2 X^i (\delta_x^1(t), \delta_x^1(t)).
$$

**Step 2.** For any $v \in \ker(D_{\bar{u}} E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1})$ with $\text{supp}(v) \in [\bar{t}, \bar{t} + \delta]$, define

$$
\bar{Q}_{\bar{t}, \delta}(v) := \int_{\bar{t}}^{\bar{t} + \delta} \int_{\bar{t}}^{\bar{t}} \left[ \sum_{i,j=1}^m v_i(t) v_j(s) (\bar{p}(\bar{t}) \cdot D_{\bar{\gamma}(\bar{t})} X^i \cdot X^j (\bar{\gamma}(\bar{t}))) \right] ds dt.
$$

Using $\int_{\bar{t}}^{\bar{t} + \delta} \bar{p}(t) \cdot \bar{C}(t) dt - \bar{Q}_{\bar{t}, \delta}(v)$ and $\int_0^1 \bar{p}(t) \cdot \bar{D}(t) dt$ respectively, we obtain

$$
\bar{p} \cdot D_{\bar{u}} E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1} = 0 \text{ and } \bar{p} \cdot D_{\bar{u}}^2 E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1}(v, v) = 2 \int_0^1 \bar{p}(t) \cdot [\bar{C}(t) + \bar{D}(t)] dt
$$

as well as

$$
|D_{\bar{u}}^2 E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1}(v, v) - \bar{Q}_{\bar{t}, \delta}(v)| \leq K \|v\|_2^2 \delta^2.
$$

**Step 3.** Proceed by [contradiction](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%8D%E8%AF%81%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity). Suppose the Goh condition fails; then there exist $\bar{t} \in (0, 1)$ and $\bar{i} \neq \bar{j}$ such that $\bar{p}(\bar{t}) \cdot [X^{\bar{i}}, X^{\bar{j}}](\bar{\gamma}(\bar{t})) = 0 > 0$.

By constructing

$$
L_{\bar{t}, \delta, N} := \{ v \in L^2([0, 1], \mathbb{R}^m), v(t) = (0, \dots, v_{\bar{i}}(t), 0, \dots, v_{\bar{j}}(t), \dots, 0) \in \mathbb{R}^m \},
$$

for any integer $N > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ and a subspace $L_{\delta} \subset L^2$ of dimension greater than $N$ such that

$$
\bar{Q}_{\bar{t}, \delta}|_{L^1 \setminus \{0\}}(-K \|v\|_2^2 \delta^2, \forall v \in L^1 \setminus \{0\}).
$$

Indeed, given $N > n$, the dimension of the kernel of the linear operator $(D_{\bar{u}} E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1}) \big|_{L}$ is $\geq N - n$, implying $\dim \ker(D_{\bar{u}} E_{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}^{\omega,1}) \geq N - n$. Combining $Q_{\bar{t}, \delta} |_{L^1(0)}(v) < -K \|v\|_2^2 \delta^2$ with $|D_u^2 E_{\bar{t}, \delta}^{x,1}(v, v) - Q_{\bar{t}, \delta}(v)| \leq K \|v\|_2^2 \delta^2$, and since $N$ can be arbitrarily large, we have

$$
\text{ind}_- \left( \bar{p} \cdot (D_u^2 E_{\bar{t}, \delta}^{x,1}) \right)_{\text{ker}(D_u E_{\bar{t}, \delta}^{x,1})}
$$

unbounded, a contradiction. □

A minimizing geodesic is called strictly abnormal if it is abnormal and admits no normal extremal lift.

**Theorem (Goh).** Let $\bar{\gamma}$ be strictly abnormal. Then there exists an abnormal lift $\bar{\psi} = (\bar{\gamma}, \bar{p})$ such that $\bar{p}(t) \cdot [X^i, X^j](\gamma_{\bar{u}}(t)) = 0$. Such a $\bar{\gamma}$ is called a Goh path.

**Proof.** The map $F(u) := (E_{\bar{t}, \delta}^{x,1}(u), C(u))$ cannot be open at $\bar{u}$. Indeed, if the image of a neighborhood of $\bar{u}$ contained a neighborhood of $F(\bar{u})$, it would contain a control with $\|u\|_2^2 \leq \|\bar{u}\|_2^2$, contradicting the minimality of $\bar{u}$.

By the Openness Theorem, there exists

$$
\lambda \in \text{Im}(D_u F)^\perp \setminus 0
$$

such that

$$
\text{ind}_- \left( \lambda \cdot (D_u^2 F) \right)_{\text{ker}(D_u F)} < r := n - \text{rank}(\bar{u}).
$$

Note that there exists $v \in L^2$ such that $D_u C(u) = 2\langle \bar{u}, v \rangle L^2 \neq 0$, so the last component of $\lambda$ is zero; hence

$$
\text{ind}_- \left( \bar{p} \cdot (D_u^2 E_{\bar{t}, \delta}^{x,1}) \right)_{\text{ker}(D_u E_{\bar{t}, \delta}^{x,1})} < +\infty.
$$

Denoting the preceding components by $\bar{p}$, we have $\bar{p} \cdot D_u E_{\bar{t}, \delta}^{x,1} = 0$, and the Index Theorem yields the desired conclusion. □

As an application, medium-fat distributions admit no nontrivial Goh paths. Recall that a distribution is called medium-fat if for every section $X$ of $\Delta$, one has $T_x M = \Delta(x) + [\Delta, \Delta](x) + [X, [\Delta, \Delta]](x)$. Any 2-generating distribution is medium-fat. Indeed, by the Goh condition combined with $p \cdot X^i = 0$, one obtains the existence of $p \in T_x^* M \setminus 0$ such that

$$
p \cdot (\Delta(x) + [\Delta, \Delta](x) + [X, [\Delta, \Delta]](x)) = 0,
$$

contradicting the definition.

### 4\. Examples of SR geodesics

Here we present two examples of great importance in sub-Riemannian geometry.

1.  **Geodesics in the Heisenberg group.** The Heisenberg group $\mathbb{H}^1$ is the sub-Riemannian structure $(\Delta, g)$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$, spanned by $X = \partial_x - \frac{y}{2} \partial_z$ and $Y = \partial_y + \frac{x}{2} \partial_z$, with $g = dx^2 + dy^2$ making $\{X, Y\}$ an orthonormal frame. In Heisenberg's original work on [quantum mechanics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%87%8F%E5%AD%90%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity), $X$ and $Y$ are [self-adjoint operators](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%87%AA%E4%BC%B4%E7%AE%97%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity) on a Hilbert space, where $X$ corresponds to measuring position and $Y$ to measuring momentum. This structure is left-invariant under the group law  
    $(x, y, z) * (x', y', z') = (x + x', y + y', z + z' + \frac{xy' - x'y}{2})$. This additional structure makes $\mathbb{H}^1$ a **Carnot group**, i.e., a simply connected Lie group. Carnot groups are to sub-Riemannian geometry what Euclidean spaces are to Riemannian geometry (recall that the [tangent space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%87%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) $T_x M$ is generated by the brackets of the totally nonholonomic distribution and carries a Lie [algebra structure](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0%E7%BB%93%E6%9E%84&zhida_source=entity)). Here $\Delta$ is fat, so there are no nontrivial singular horizontal paths. According to the Hamiltonian geodesic equations, the projections of minimizing geodesics onto the $x$-$y$ plane are circles.
2.  **Montgomery's counterexample.** Consider a Martinet-type distribution on $\mathbb{R}^3$ generated by $X = \partial_{x_1}$ and $Y = (1 + x_1 \phi(x)) \partial_{x_2} + x_1^2 \partial_{x_3}$, where $\phi$ is a [smooth function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%89%E6%BB%91%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity), equipped with a metric $g$ making $\{X, Y\}$ an orthonormal family. The singular curves are the horizontal paths contained in the Martinet set  
    $\Sigma_\Delta = \{x_1 = 0\}$. Note that if $\phi \equiv 0$ (i.e., $g = dx_1^2 + dx_2^2$), then the horizontal path $\gamma(t) = (0, t, 0)$ (for any $t \in [0, \epsilon]$) is a singular normal minimizing geodesic. Different choices of the metric can provide examples of strictly abnormal minimizing geodesics. If $\phi(0) \neq 0$, then no re[parameterization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%82%E6%95%B0%E5%8C%96&zhida_source=entity) of $\gamma$ is the projection of a normal extremal.

## III. Optimal Transport on Sub-Riemannian Manifolds

Work in this direction originated with the study of optimal transport problems on the Heisenberg group by Ambrosio and Rigot in 2004 in J. Func. Anal. The main result, a Brenier–McCann-type theorem in the SR setting, comes from the 2010 article by Figalli and Rifford in Geom. Funct. Anal.

In optimal transport theory, by the classical Brenier–McCann theorem, for the Brenier cost $c(x, y) = \|x - y\|^2 / 2$ on Euclidean space and, more generally, the McCann cost $c(x, y) = d_g(x, y)^2 / 2$ on Riemannian manifolds, the optimal transport map exists and is unique in the measure-theoretic sense. (It is worth mentioning here that, according to the 2007 article by Bernard and Buffoni, this cost can be generalized to locally Lipschitz costs associated with [variational problems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%98%E5%88%86%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98&zhida_source=entity) involving Tonelli Lagrangians, which connects the theory to Mather theory.) We now ask whether a similar conclusion holds for the quadratic cost $c(x, y) = d_{SR}(x, y)^2 / 2$ on SR manifolds. In fact, as we shall see below, the optimal transport map in the SR setting transports along unique normal minimizing geodesics, defined via the SR exponential map; this amounts to imposing a nonholonomic constraint on the transport process.

By analogy with classical optimal transport theory, one naturally considers the [regularity](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AD%A3%E5%88%99%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) of the cost, which imposes conditions on the SR structure. By the Mitchell ball-box theorem, SR distances are locally Hölder; for complete SR distances, the distance is Lipschitz on a dense set. However, this set need not be of full measure (the Sard conjecture), so one cannot obtain an optimal transport map in the measure-theoretic sense, and hence one cannot expect the desired result for general SR structures. As we will see, we need to require the SR distance to be locally Lipschitz, or, more strongly, locally semiconcave (ideal). The semiconcavity condition is sometimes necessary: by Alexandrov's famous result, semiconcave [functions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%87%B9%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) are twice differentiable almost everywhere, which ensures that the sub-Riemannian Monge–Ampère equation holds almost everywhere. In fact, the distance of an ideal SR structure behaves like a Riemannian distance (at least away from the diagonal $D = \{(x, x)\} \subset M \times M$, i.e., on a set of full measure).

### 1\. The Monge Problem and the Kantorovich Problem

Optimal transport theory originated with Monge's 1781 work, in which he considered how to minimize the cost of transporting mass from one continuous distribution to another (e.g., the movement of soil), namely, finding the map $T$ minimizing $\int_{\mathbb{R}^3} \|T(x) - x\| d\mu(x)$. The more general optimal transport problem, the Monge problem, was formulated by Kantorovich in 1942. Since the transport condition satisfied by the map is equivalent to a nonlinear Monge–Ampère equation, which is difficult to solve, Kantorovich proposed a relaxed version of the problem, equivalent to considering the graph of the optimal transport map, namely, the Kantorovich problem.

The Monge problem asks how to minimize $\int_M c(x, T(x)) d\mu(x)$, where the [measurable map](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%AF%E6%B5%8B%E6%98%A0%E5%B0%84&zhida_source=entity) $T$ pushes the [probability measure](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=280266163&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A6%82%E7%8E%87%E6%B5%8B%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity) $\mu$ forward to $\nu$; this requirement is equivalent to

$$
\int_M \varphi(T(x)) d\mu(x) = \int_M \varphi(y) d\nu(y) \quad \text{for all } \nu \text{-integrable functions } \varphi.
$$

Letting $f$ and $g$ denote the R–N derivatives of $\mu$ and $\nu$ with respect to Lebesgue measure, by the change of variables formula we obtain the Monge–Ampère equation

$$
\det(D_x T) = f(x) / g(T(x)) \quad \text{a.e. in } M.
$$

$$
c(x, y) = \|y - x\|
$$
