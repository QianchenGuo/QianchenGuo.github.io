Personally, I think it's feasible, though it requires certain conditions. Given a certain level of insight, having a good mentor can greatly accelerate one's growth in mathematics.

In my vision, if someone can guide you in learning mathematics, the approach shouldn't be rigidly tied to the textbook's progression. Instead, it should involve asking more questions, building frameworks, and clarifying motivations. When this mentor answers your questions in that context, your mathematical thinking and maturity can improve very quickly.

Of course, this largely depends on your own perceptiveness as well as the mentor's level and even their mathematical taste, because they need a clear grasp of both the framework and most details of a given branch to address what might be all sorts of curious questions you throw at them. It also depends on whether the mentor has the energy to cultivate you. In reality, mentors in China today rarely reach that standard—often not because of insufficient expertise, but mainly because they lack the time and energy.

Consider, for instance, the example of [Shiing-Shen Chern](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=650705804&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=%E9%99%88%E7%9C%81%E8%BA%AB&zhida_source=entity). He often said that to do mathematics, you should work with masters—that's the essence. Anyone familiar with his life knows that [Cartan](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=650705804&content_type=Answer&match_order=1&q=Cartan&zhida_source=entity), as his mentor, played an enormous role in advancing Chern's mathematical development.
