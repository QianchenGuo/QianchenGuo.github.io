## I. What Is Control

In general, control concerns **the behavior of solutions to a [dynamical system](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) and how it can be manipulated by us**. Compared to [vibration theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8C%AF%E5%8A%A8%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity), which focuses more on the specific form of solutions, i.e., the mode shapes, control theory places greater emphasis on **qualitative analysis of solutions, especially their asymptotic behavior**.

More specifically, the method of control is **to alter the vector field on the underlying manifold, i.e., the state space, via input terms or update terms, thereby changing the nature of the solutions**.

However, in reality, we should establish a "broad view of control," not confining our perspective to the narrow qualitative theory of dynamical systems mentioned above, but rather considering the task of having the dynamical system move one or more points in the [state space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E7%8A%B6%E6%80%81%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) to another location. This encompasses what is traditionally referred to as planning.

[https://www.zhihu.com/question/497640941/answer/2229258201?utm_psn=1793661460892753921](https://www.zhihu.com/question/497640941/answer/2229258201?utm_psn=1793661460892753921)

As mentioned above, beyond the tasks of tracking, stabilization, and [disturbance rejection](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%89%B0%E5%8A%A8%E6%8A%91%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity) in the narrow sense of control, the broad sense of control can also generate motions, compensating for some shortcomings of traditional planning. A so-called motion is a [submanifold](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AD%90%E6%B5%81%E5%BD%A2&zhida_source=entity) of the state space, together with its corresponding control law.

I believe this entails a shift from control of [causal systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9B%A0%E6%9E%9C%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) to control of [non-causal systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E5%9B%A0%E6%9E%9C%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity). Control in the narrow sense is causal control: tracking a reference signal requires only a few [derivatives](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%98%B6%E5%AF%BC%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity), with no need for future reference signals. For non-causal control, however, one must integrate with traditional planning and the predictive theory of traditional estimation, incorporating future reference signals into the control law, rather than merely treating planning and control as upstream and downstream modules as is currently done.

Correspondingly, rigorous definitions of many concepts and problems themselves constitute a new frontier, potentially giving rise to fundamental properties beyond [controllability and observability](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%83%BD%E6%8E%A7%E8%83%BD%E8%A7%82&zhida_source=entity). If we set aside foundational concepts for now and proceed from a downstream application perspective, we can quickly derive some valuable questions.

Predictions of the future are inevitably imprecise, and tracking errors are entirely normal. Unlike ordinary control, which only needs the current reference signal and its derivatives to guarantee tracking convergence through certain designs, future reference signals depend on predictions of the future world. In complex environments, such as predicting the behavior of other agents, perfect accuracy is impossible. We must consider **how to incorporate this future uncertainty into our [theoretical framework](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA%E6%9E%B6%E6%9E%84&zhida_source=entity)**. That is, the question posed earlier: "How to possess both the predictive capability of non-causal control (predictive control or planning) and the [robustness](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%B2%81%E6%A3%92%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) of causal feedback control."

In traditional control, this amounts to the two paradigms of "responding to all changes with constancy," i.e., [robust control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%B2%81%E6%A3%92%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity), and "responding to changes with changes," i.e., [adaptive control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%87%AA%E9%80%82%E5%BA%94%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity).

Even if uncertainty is incorporated into the theoretical framework, in practical applications **we cannot handle uncertainty conservatively without bound—so how do we then guarantee safety**? Safety in a predicted environment does not imply actual safety. Thus, we should endow control with the ability to adapt on the fly. Does this mean we must fuse control with game theory, or consider multiple possible future evolutions of the dynamical system? How should the array of problems in traditional control be generalized?

In particular, when simplified, the above issues essentially correspond to some of the problems currently faced in motion planning. I have also provided some summary and analysis of the fundamental problems in planning.

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/708362997?utm_psn=1852666217569071104](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/708362997?utm_psn=1852666217569071104)

## II. How to Control

First, we must analyze the behavioral properties of solutions to the dynamical system to determine what qualitative behavior we desire, and then study the feasibility and specific methods for achieving those preset goals. The **feasibility study, in turn, often feeds back into the analysis of properties**, yielding further insight into the system's characteristics. Below, I discuss problems in control from several angles, proceeding from parent problems to subproblems.

### (1) Topological constraints

At minimum, the effectiveness of control is constrained by the topological properties of the [phase space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%B8%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) (state space) of the dynamical system. We have results from [algebraic topology](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0%E6%8B%93%E6%89%91&zhida_source=entity) and differential topology in this regard. A large part of topology studies some kind of "obstruction," and apart from obstructions, the rest is the eternal mathematical problem of classification—though studying obstructions is itself a form of classification. The most famous result in this vein is probably the Poincaré–Hopf theorem.

These topological obstructions mean that sometimes the effectiveness of control has a ceiling, as with the inverted pendulum:

[https://www.zhihu.com/question/31552490/answer/408647826?utm_psn=1775469268399960064](https://www.zhihu.com/question/31552490/answer/408647826?utm_psn=1775469268399960064)

(Although, due to my limited knowledge, I still don't understand why there are three [unstable equilibrium points](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%8D%E7%A8%B3%E5%AE%9A%E5%B9%B3%E8%A1%A1%E7%82%B9&zhida_source=entity) rather than four or five.)

However, topology is not always an obstacle; at times, it can be leveraged to achieve better, more globally oriented results that are not always attainable in flat spaces. For example, the results in the article below.

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/695337197?utm_psn=1834768417363738624](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/695337197?utm_psn=1834768417363738624)

It is worth noting that we usually fail to notice the influence of phase space topology because, in most cases, the phase space is ℝⁿ—this flat, well-behaved space—rather than other manifolds.

Problems such as pose estimation, which involves SO(3), and the [configuration space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%8D%E5%BD%A2%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) in planning become far more complex due to obstacles, even [dynamic obstacles](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E6%80%81%E9%9A%9C%E7%A2%8D&zhida_source=entity). For such complex configuration spaces in planning, we are often forced to resort to sampling-based methods in path planning to avoid explicit representation of the complex space. At the lower optimization level, we can hardly avoid the issue of agent behavior interacting with [complex topology](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%8D%E6%9D%82%E6%8B%93%E6%89%91&zhida_source=entity). In fact, I often wonder whether this points toward a new kind of dynamic [topology](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8B%93%E6%89%91%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity) with uncertainty.

### (2) [Integrability](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%AF%E7%A7%AF%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity)

From the perspective of [integrable systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%AF%E7%A7%AF%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity), we can discuss the integrability of ODE systems—that is, whether the behavior of [integral curves](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86%E6%9B%B2%E7%BA%BF&zhida_source=entity) is complex and whether the foliation is sufficiently nice. The root of all this lies in codimension.

Two-dimensional systems are governed by the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem, which states that their integral curves cannot be too complicated: the limit set is either a closed orbit or an equilibrium point. Existence and uniqueness of solutions ensure that integral curves are simple and are immersed submanifolds of the state space. However, [three-dimensional systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%89%E7%BB%B4%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) quickly exhibit chaos sensitive to initial conditions and parameters, such as the famous [Lorenz attractor](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Lorenz%E5%90%B8%E5%BC%95%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity), where integral curves are highly complex.

Introducing foliations—a concept applied in algebraic topology, algebraic geometry, and [low-dimensional topology](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%8E%E7%BB%B4%E6%8B%93%E6%89%91&zhida_source=entity)—we can roughly define it as an [equivalence relation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%AD%89%E4%BB%B7%E5%85%B3%E7%B3%BB&zhida_source=entity) on an n-dimensional manifold (the state space), where each [equivalence class](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%AD%89%E4%BB%B7%E7%B1%BB&zhida_source=entity) is a d-dimensional immersed submanifold.

Example: Integral curves form a 1-dimensional foliation. Given an n-dimensional manifold M and a vector field on it, this induces an ODE on M; two points p, q in M are equivalent if and only if they lie on the same integral curve. A 2-dimensional foliation corresponds to the common solutions of two vector fields whose Lie bracket is zero, yielding an "integral surface." Again, with existence and uniqueness, any two integral surfaces cannot self-intersect.

The [codimension](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%99%E7%BB%B4%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) c = n − d: the smaller c, the simpler the topology of the foliation. When c = 1, there is a [classification theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%86%E7%B1%BB%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity) for foliations; when c ≥ 2, the situation becomes more complicated. Of course, this naive idea ultimately culminates in [Frobenius's theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Frobenius%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity): a vector field has sufficiently many conserved quantities—i.e., sufficiently many other vector fields with which its Lie bracket vanishes—forming a foliation with codimension 1.

In my view, the significance of integrability for control and nonlinear studies lies in its ability to decompose the well-behaved portion of general [nonlinear systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity), thereby enabling progress in nonlinear research. If a system is non-integrable, it falls into the domain of [chaos theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B7%B7%E6%B2%8C%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity), where, at present, we can only describe chaotic behavior through vaguer, more holistic concepts such as attractors, measures, and entropy. Yet the existence of chaos has its own value: it shows that the physical world is not [deterministic](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%86%B3%E5%AE%9A%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity). This may well be a source of complex intelligence.

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/607670311?utm_psn=1848533590217990144](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/607670311?utm_psn=1848533590217990144)

### (3) The Whole and the Part

In my previous post, I mentioned the concepts of the whole and the part: control is concerned with global, large-scale properties, or rather, the asymptotic behavior of solution curves. Therefore, even though from the perspective of integrability, every ODE is locally integrable away from singular points (local rectification of vector fields), this is far from sufficient for control. Control necessarily requires finding the extent of the [domain of attraction](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%90%B8%E5%BC%95%E5%9F%9F&zhida_source=entity), rather than settling for results of the form "as long as the region is small enough, the properties are good enough." In other words, even if control cannot achieve global results, it should operate within a region that can be estimated. Obtaining such results is no easy feat. In Section (1), I also mentioned that sometimes topology can yield results of a more global nature.

Unfortunately, mathematicians already consider the integrability of ODEs to be a largely settled matter. Current research focuses more on specific systems (e.g., [Hamiltonian systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Hamilton%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity)) or problems on specific manifolds, many of which originate from other branches, or else researchers move directly into integrable PDE systems. The former benefits from concrete equations and additional structural constraints, enriching the problem; the latter is a relatively new branch that is flourishing.

The global integrability problem for general ODEs is simply too difficult. This is why the development of control theory has effectively been anchored within existing systems that possess sufficient integrability. The fundamental difficulty of nonlinearity cannot be tackled overnight, which is why people say "control is dead." But we must understand that the existing [nonlinear control](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity) theory is already capable of handling a great many application-driven problems.

Moreover, the relationship between the whole and the part reflects a dialectical shift in scientific paradigms:

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/567256063?utm_psn=1848532641030205441](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/567256063?utm_psn=1848532641030205441)

### (4) Controllability and Observability

These concepts, creatively introduced by Kalman and others, carry profound [philosophical significance](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%93%B2%E5%AD%A6%E6%84%8F%E4%B9%89&zhida_source=entity) and are universal in their applicability to systems—indeed, this pair of properties transcends the [linear-nonlinear](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) distinction. The introduction of controllability and observability precisely reflects a feasibility study feeding back into the analysis of system properties, as hinted at the beginning of this section.

Controllability is about the extent to which the outside can influence the inside; observability is about the extent to which the outside can reflect the inside. Why do we care so much about the inside? Because we need feedback. What is feedback? Feedback is the transformation of a non-[autonomous system](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%87%AA%E6%B2%BB%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity) into an autonomous one. Only autonomous systems admit Lyapunov stability analysis. And tasks such as tracking, regulation, disturbance rejection, and observer design ultimately all reduce to proving some form of stability. (I have not yet studied controllability and observability for nonlinear systems; that requires learning geometric control theory.)

### (5) Linear vs. Nonlinear

As I mentioned in the previous post, linear and nonlinear systems behave very differently. In control theory and in most branches of mathematics—optimization, algebraic equations, [functional analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%B3%9B%E5%87%BD%E5%88%86%E6%9E%90&zhida_source=entity) as the general framework of analysis, differential equations as its proving ground—the linear case is generally simpler and yields a systematic body of results. Nonlinear research, by contrast, tends to be scattered and lacks a unified methodology.

In my view, the most fundamental difference between linear and nonlinear lies in decoupling: the linear constraint prevents the system from exhibiting extreme coupling. Behind decoupling lies the nice structure of [linear spaces](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity)—a linear space can be decomposed into a [low-dimensional subspace](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BD%8E%E7%BB%B4%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) and its [orthogonal complement](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AD%A3%E4%BA%A4%E8%A1%A5&zhida_source=entity). Similarly, the most central concept in linear systems is the controllability-observability decomposition, which dissects the system in a fully transparent manner.

For [nonlinear control systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity), on the other hand, we focus on affine systems of the form f + gu. Affine systems retain a linear-like decoupling property with respect to the control input. After discretization, they typically also exhibit a Markovian property, which weakens the coupling between quantities at different time instants.

Other prominent nonlinear phenomena include chaos, solitons, and bifurcations. [Soliton theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%AD%A4%E6%B3%A2%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity) is the main object of study in integrable PDE systems—those interested can check out Teacher 647's lectures on Bilibili. [Bifurcation theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%86%E5%B2%94%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity) currently belongs to dynamical systems in mathematics, or more specifically, to [nonlinear dynamics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E5%AD%A6). It studies how phase trajectories change as parameters vary. The difference in focus between bifurcation theory and control theory is that the former is concerned with equivalence, or topological changes, of system trajectories, i.e., equivalence of dynamical systems under parameter variation, whereas control theory remains concerned with the stability of singular points.

### (6) First-Order ODE Systems (State-Space Models)

Finally, let me discuss why our models look the way they do.

Recently I learned about Professor [Duan Guangren](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%AE%B5%E5%B9%BF%E4%BB%81&zhida_source=entity)'s high-order fully actuated theory, and he seems to believe that the state-space model for differential equations has its limitations. I would like to understand this at a surface level, which boils down to why we introduced state-space models in the first place, and further back, why mathematics reduces all ODEs to first-order systems.

I am not clear on the historical development here (**if anyone knows the history, I would appreciate being informed**). However, I believe the greatest advantage of first-order ODE systems is their connection to vector fields on manifolds. On a manifold, first derivatives can be handled naturally via the tangent bundle structure, whereas higher derivatives are more cumbersome. The language of vector fields greatly facilitates qualitative analysis of dynamical systems, and decades of development have also produced a wealth of mature numerical algorithms for first-order systems. The theory of first-order systems should be complete, so why does Professor Duan's concern arise? This is something I still need to investigate.

That said, one obvious point is that the second-order models in high-order fully actuated theory are very common in practical applications and clearly carry more physical meaning. Those who have worked in robot control should readily appreciate this.

## 3. How to Study Control Theory

First, I quite agree with

[@Flavius Julianus](//www.zhihu.com/people/2e7521a62564ecf1a09b1bfe6286e840)

that to properly understand the fundamental concepts in control, one should approach them from the most general perspective, namely from nonlinear systems.

### (1) How to View [Classical Control Theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BB%8F%E5%85%B8%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity)

The so-called classical control theory taught in automatic control principles courses is filled with a **plethora of mechanic-like tricks, vague criteria, and "conclusions" based on experimental observation rather than proven theory**. To be sure, the "payoff" from these mechanic-like tricks is substantial: they carry a certain physical meaning and are highly convenient for application and dissemination, which is precisely why classical control is so widely used. Anyone familiar with mathematics or its history will readily recognize that, before the explosive development of mathematics following World War II under the influence of Bourbaki's structuralism, mathematics in its unstructured or pre-axiomatic period also exhibited a strong, more or less "artistic" or technical character, with [intuitionism](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%B4%E8%A7%89%E4%B8%BB%E4%B9%89&zhida_source=entity) lurking behind many results. Nevertheless, we cannot deny that these LTI tricks are simple and practical; in many contexts, they work just fine—after all, feedback control is inherently endowed with a certain degree of robustness.

When Qian Xuesen wrote [*Engineering Cybernetics*](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E3%80%8A%E5%B7%A5%E7%A8%8B%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E8%AE%BA%E3%80%8B&zhida_source=entity), it served as a synthesis of the various results from the classical control era (including some stochastic and nonlinear results). One can also see that from its inception, "cybernetics" already envisioned far-reaching future developments. Cybernetics, or [systems science](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F%E7%A7%91%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity), has its own potential. The influence of Qian's book is so great that even today's undergraduate "Automatic Control Principles" courses in automation programs are all derived from it. One could say that the current undergraduate training in control has historical reasons behind it.

However, we should note that **the underlying frequency-domain theory of classical control is actually more abstract than its time-domain counterpart**. Its foundation rests on the following:

1. Linearity and time-invariance decouple the [differential operator](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BE%AE%E5%88%86%E7%AE%97%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity) from the function, allowing one to construct an explicit input-output mapping—the key point being the explicitness;

2. Once the explicit input-output operator is obtained, its spectral decomposition and the time-domain behavior of the eigenfunctions—exponential functions (and their [linear combinations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E7%BB%84%E5%90%88&zhida_source=entity))—establish the connection between the spectrum and time-domain properties.

These rely on functional analysis, operator spectral theory, and similar foundations, which we have actually developed less thoroughly than the direct time-domain theory. This is also why, whenever we discuss classical control theory, we tend to remain confined to [linear time-invariant systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E6%97%B6%E4%B8%8D%E5%8F%98%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity): outside this class, the above theory rarely yields satisfactory results. From this perspective, we must admit that for control majors, learning classical control before modern control follows the historical order of development—but the pedagogical logic is inverted relative to the theory itself. It feels like "one must run before learning to walk, because in the LTI world, running is actually easier than walking in the general world."

I have seen large numbers of graduates from control-related programs who don't even know what their own field is about or what it can do, trapped instead by the label imposed by non-specialists, by those who never truly understood control, and by society at large: "Automation is a jack-of-all-trades—you learn a bit of everything but master nothing." We must therefore have the courage to break free from historical constraints. Instead of dividing control into classical and modern camps and learning them separately, we should **get to the root of the matter**. Clarifying what control is and how to control—this, I believe, is what will truly benefit the development of the discipline.

### (2) Is Control Theory Too Abstract?

Some might say that for most undergraduates, being introduced directly to nonlinear systems is hard to accept, and that it would effectively abandon the dominant position of classical control theory, which remains the most widely applied framework in practice.

But what I actually want to convey is the understanding of fundamental concepts in control, not the practical design at the application level. The former is necessarily more abstract, but abstraction always exerts a powerful guiding influence on the concrete. I have already discussed this dialectical relationship in a previously published article (and abstract thinking is, after all, a hallmark of mathematics enthusiasts).

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/639200474?utm_psn=1774522069252141056](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/639200474?utm_psn=1774522069252141056)

I believe the difficulty in acceptance stems from certain unreasonable aspects of the undergraduate control curriculum, where the gap between mathematical foundations and control is far too wide. For the standard mathematics courses, many universities place too much emphasis on exam-oriented learning for graduate entrance exams, while giving little thought to what these courses actually mean for the students.

### (3) The Mathematical Foundation One Should Have

I have imagined that different majors or schools should have different series of engineering mathematics courses. Take automation, for example:

In calculus, one could simply drop the differential equations portion. In fact, those competition-style tricks and techniques shouldn't be given a platform either—there is already plenty of classical material in calculus (for those who doubt this, I suggest flipping through the three volumes of Fikhtengolts' [*Course of Differential and Integral Calculus*](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E3%80%8A%E5%BE%AE%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86%E5%AD%A6%E6%95%99%E7%A8%8B%E3%80%8B&zhida_source=entity)). Wouldn't it be better to expose students to these classical, natural techniques rather than artificial or exam-targeted tricks?

In [linear algebra](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E4%BB%A3%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity), we should discard the peripheral computational tricks. Raise the status of linear spaces, build an intuition for [linear transformations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7%E5%8F%98%E6%8D%A2&zhida_source=entity), and make clear the abstractness and generality of linear spaces, thereby preparing students for [integral transforms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86%E5%8F%98%E6%8D%A2&zhida_source=entity). I think some answers and articles on Zhihu already discuss this quite well. (In other words, this means bringing more [matrix theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9F%A9%E9%98%B5%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity) content into the undergraduate curriculum.)

[Complex analysis](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A4%8D%E5%8F%98%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) could also be compressed further (this is entirely feasible, as I have tried it myself), focusing on the argument principle. Integral transforms could be taught in conjunction with signals and systems. The key is to convey the *idea* behind integral transforms, rather than mechanically applying transformations without understanding them. With the groundwork laid above, students can begin to grasp [function spaces](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity), and then understand that performing transformations in function space means projecting onto a new basis.

The ODE course should convey the style of differential equations as the core of control research. Let me elaborate on what it should contain (note: none of the following involves stochastic systems):

1. Most of the differential equations content from standard engineering calculus, i.e., the elementary integration methods from a mathematics department's ODE course. Together with [linear ODEs](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%BA%BF%E6%80%A7ODE&zhida_source=entity), this essentially covers the differential equations portion of graduate entrance exam material.

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/656182185?utm_psn=1774528577809342464](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/656182185?utm_psn=1774528577809342464)

2. Linear ODEs

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/657345545?utm_psn=1774528742582738944](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/657345545?utm_psn=1774528742582738944)

This chapter can directly introduce much of the content from undergraduate [modern control theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%8E%B0%E4%BB%A3%E6%8E%A7%E5%88%B6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity). It should include not only the motion analysis of state equations but also input-output descriptions. For high-order linear time-invariant ODEs, one can introduce [transfer functions](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%A0%E9%80%92%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity), including system block diagrams and time-domain analysis of transfer functions. For undergraduates, time-varying content can be optional.

3. An introduction to qualitative theory of nonlinear ODEs

Clarify the essence of differential equations and control, and introduce nonlinear systems. Understand phase space, vector fields, and integral curves.

This content should be combined with the vector calculus portion of calculus, giving students a deeper understanding of fields, rather than merely computing gradients, divergences, and curls, or distinguishing type-I (scalar) and type-II (vector) surface integrals. For example, Liouville's formula reveals the relationship between the trace of a matrix and the divergence of a vector field—a truly beautiful result.

Here we can introduce [phase plane](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%B8%E5%B9%B3%E9%9D%A2&zhida_source=entity) analysis, including the Poincaré-Bendixson criterion, and so on. Naturally, we can also easily extract a series of time-domain performance indices from phase portraits or outputs.

5. Stability

Undergraduate modern control theory typically covers linear systems. But stability can certainly be discussed for general nonlinear systems; for undergraduates, the focus should be on LTI systems, with time-varying and nonlinear cases as supplementary material. Stability analysis should clarify the distinction between internal stability and external (input-output) stability; for the latter, BIBO stability (L∞) suffices.

Criteria should include the general Lyapunov first and second methods in the time domain. For SISO systems in particular, one can mention the Routh-Hurwitz criterion, present frequency-domain [stability criteria](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A8%B3%E5%AE%9A%E5%88%A4%E6%8D%AE&zhida_source=entity), and introduce the core tools of classical control theory—the Nyquist plot and [Bode plot](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Bode%E5%9B%BE&zhida_source=entity). These classical results are, after all, nothing more than tricks for characterizing eigenvalues.

The structure of this content can follow [Khalil](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=243280589&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Khalil&zhida_source=entity)'s *Nonlinear Systems*, Chapters 4 and 5, but the content should be substantially simplified to the undergraduate level: proofs should be reduced to essentials or omitted, with the occasional conceptually insightful proof retained.

### (IV) How to Teach a New Control Theory Course

I believe that after the discussion in Section 3(I), we now have a correct understanding of classical control theory, and it is clear that the teaching of control courses has been deeply influenced by historical reasons. So how should we teach a new control theory course?

We need a new course on control theory and design. This course should serve as a core professional course and needs to cover:

1. Introducing the basic tasks and fundamental ideas of control

Present precise definitions of the basic tasks in control, such as stabilization, tracking, and disturbance rejection. Emphasize the central role of feedback in control. Point out that feedback is divided into state feedback and output feedback, and that its essence is to transform a non-autonomous equation into an autonomous one for stability analysis.

2. Simple analysis and linearization of nonlinear systems

This is divided into local linearization via Taylor expansion and feedback linearization. At this point, the theory of feedback linearization can be extended, introducing some standard forms of nonlinear feedback linearization. This naturally leads to concepts such as zero dynamics, relative degree, and minimum phase.

3. Controllability and observability of linear systems

The controllability and observability portion can basically follow the undergraduate modern control theory course. The most important point is to clarify, in the context of controllability/observability decomposition and state-space realization, the relationship between transfer function poles and system matrix eigenvalues. This is arguably the most core knowledge in undergraduate modern control theory, and it is precisely what classical control theory overlooks—hidden in the pole-zero cancellation of transfer functions. If conditions permit, controllability and observability decomposition should be viewed from the perspective of linear spaces, as this is also an important component of the geometric theory of linear systems.

2. SISO frequency-domain design and compensation

Here we return to classical control, but we should explain, based on the theory developed earlier, why these classical methods work, thereby unifying the two. I believe there are few books on the market that achieve this.

For the external description of systems, we need to propose performance indices for SISO systems. Building directly on root locus analysis and frequency-domain analysis centered on transfer functions, we obtain a series of relationships between time-domain performance indices and complex-domain/frequency-domain indices. At the same time, the concept of minimum phase systems must be unified with its time-domain definition introduced earlier.

Then comes the standard compensation content. But this should not be limited to classical control methods like PID—we can fully incorporate modern control perspectives. For example, PID control with saturation limits is actually a special case of sliding mode control with relative degree 2 plus integral control.

3. MIMO design and compensation

Explain the basic methods of pole placement and introduce the concept of observers. Establish the duality relationship between controllability and observability.
