In [Autonomous Driving PPnC Occlusion Handling](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1997333141497590142), I conducted a literature review of the main approaches to occlusion handling. However, the survey reveals that some methods are heuristic, others are more theoretically grounded, and each has its own strengths and weaknesses. So within the existing framework, what might a reasonable benchmark and an effective solution to the series of problems caused by occluded blind zones look like? This article starts from several typical scenarios (each highly representative of a class of problems) to attempt solutions, borrowing from a few papers in the previous survey that prove useful.

## 1. Brief Commentary

### 1.1 Prediction

A core contradiction lies in **how to avoid merely removing the visible portions of the occluded region from the previous frame based on observations at each frame, while generating no information for regions that remain occluded in the current frame** — that would be equivalent to producing no useful predictive information for planning at all [\[1\]](#ref_1). This tells us that we must introduce **dynamics** and **temporal** constraints to bound the velocity within the occluded region. Thus, as time progresses and no object emerges from the occluded region, even if an object could exist in the current occluded position set, its velocity must be low. Specifically, rather than making strong assumptions about the state (position, velocity, etc.) of objects in the occluded region, we **maintain a set along with a velocity field within it** [\[2\]](#ref_2). This also avoids having to maintain a virtual object (since where to place such a virtual object is itself a problem).

In [Autonomous Driving PPnC Occlusion Handling](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1997333141497590142), it is first clear that introducing **reachable sets** in prediction is necessary for safety; the corresponding core issue is balancing computational speed with conservativeness. Fast computation has already been demonstrated in the literature: by adopting some **linear model approximations**, one can then take intersections [\[2\]](#ref_2); the reachable set of a **nonlinear model** is a subset of this. Such computation can be performed by decomposing the occluded region into **polygons** and combining them with set-operation libraries [\[3\]](#ref_3). Other approaches directly **simplify away the lateral behavior** of the occluded object [\[4\]](#ref_4), retaining only the lane boundary and front/rear occlusion boundaries. **Probabilistic** frameworks can do something similar: initialize a uniform distribution covering the blind zone, then perform inference, which again can only reduce conservativeness through velocity inference.

### 1.2 Decision and Planning

**1. A Reasonable Solution Framework**

First, a major framework is **contingency planning**, which is surprisingly rare in the occlusion-related literature survey (only one paper from 2026). This corresponds to the "proactive" safety discussed in [Solving 99% of Autonomous Driving, Why Stuck on the Last 1%? — "Contingency Planning"](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1998410424610210766), and is the key to avoiding the excessive conservativeness of "reactive" approaches. This behavior is also human-like: when facing unpredictable extreme risks, people do not crawl or stop completely while driving; instead, they ensure they can handle a certain level of risk during normal driving.

[\[5\]](#ref_5) proposes an **occlusion-aware contingency planning framework**. It uses a simplified reachability quantification (SRQ). Compared to sampling-based **Monte Carlo methods** or full reachable-set computation, SRQ provides a risk distribution quickly via **closed-form formulas**, offering a real-time basis for subsequent velocity-bound generation. Contingency planning is parameterized by **Bezier curves**, with two trajectories sharing an initial segment to ensure control continuity during execution. **Terminal constraints** require the final lateral position to return to the lane centerline with the **heading angle** tending to zero, aiming to ensure trajectory **convergence**. The original problem contains nonlinear **kinematic** constraints and **obstacle-avoidance** constraints; by introducing **auxiliary variables** and linearizing, each ADMM subproblem becomes a convex **quadratic program**. This allows **dual variables** to be updated in parallel, and with small subproblem sizes (fixed Bezier order), the computational complexity scales linearly with the number of obstacles. Experiments show average solve times of 23–28 ms, supporting replanning frequencies above 20 Hz.

A similar idea appears in [\[6\]](#ref_6), where the planning horizon is split into two modes: **closed-loop mode** assumes the occluded object becomes observable, leading to a pursuit-evasion game, where one approximately solves an HJI equation whose terminal condition is given by a *forward safe invariant set* (predefined, e.g., some relaxation based on RSS), yielding a value function whose **level sets** can be used for **invariant set** estimation, thus yielding a *capture set* (the set of joint states from which, regardless of the ego vehicle's response, the pursuer is guaranteed to win, i.e., cause a collision) [\[7\]](#ref_7). Correspondingly, the *danger zone* is the set of pursuer states that, given the ego state, would drive the trajectory into the capture basin. **Open-loop mode** refers to a planner (such as MPC) that accounts for occluded objects indirectly through conditions.

Based on the above theory, we need to compute a pursuer-winning state set (danger zone) from our own state, the dynamics of the ego and hidden vehicles, and some basic parameters (reaction time, **maximum deceleration**, etc.). Then, we ensure that at the next step, the **planner maintains the hidden set** (the occluded position set from Section 1.1) **crossed with a reasonable velocity interval** (which could be the tighter interval inferred in [\[2\]](#ref_2)) **having no intersection with this danger zone**. When the condition is not satisfied at the next step, the current step also switches to closed-loop mode to guarantee safety. This ensures the planned trajectory has an active-perception effect and can guarantee safety (based on the forward safe invariant set).

**2. Other Approaches**

Regarding the body of work on **active perception**, I have not found a sound cost design; most papers heuristically construct costs related to "field of view" or "observation" to induce active behavior. For **POMDP**, the biggest issue is **dimensionality reduction**; admittedly, having seen many claims that it does not work, I have not read it in detail, but [\[8\]](#ref_8) looks relatively reliable — more recent work seems to be dominated by RL and similar methods. Then there is **risk-aware** planning: the framework itself is fine, but how to define the **risk field** remains heuristic and rule-based. In particular, some papers claim to construct risk fields from reachable-set analysis of positions, but in reality, unless an NPC enters the blind zone from outside, one cannot truly exclude positions in the blind zone; one can only infer velocity and eliminate some positions through new observations.

## 2. Benchmark

### Scenario 1: Driving Blind Zone

![](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-edb036c8fc254d0f8c26b8b8bfc37cd3_r.jpg)

As shown in the figure, during driving it is common for the following distance to the vehicle ahead or behind in the current lane to become too small, creating blind zones in the front, rear, and adjacent lanes. Without any blind-zone handling, the decision and planning process is prone to erroneous lane changes and detours; the blind zone can lead to misjudgments about traffic efficiency, and even safety issues due to a slow vehicle (or fast vehicle from behind) in the blind zone of the adjacent lane.

This scenario is the most structured, commonly occurring on highways or urban roads with normal traffic. The **structured** nature means we have more conditions to exploit. For example:

- Vehicles in other lanes follow the correct direction of travel and do not drive against traffic;
- Other vehicles have no motive to actively create risk or accidents with the ego vehicle; for instance, they do not follow a **zero-sum game** and deliberately drive (at an aggressive speed) away from the blind-zone boundary to make it as hard as possible for the ego vehicle to discover them;
- Other vehicles have no motive to actively create risk or accidents with other vehicles; they also obey speed limits and follow the traffic flow, meaning their speed is bounded by the traffic-flow speed (otherwise they would collide within the flow);

However, there are corresponding challenges, and we will address them with the above framework:

- The occlusion is unbounded, which is actually common in autonomous driving;
- Observations are sparse — a vehicle may pass through the visible region, but often no vehicle appears at all, leaving almost no information about the corresponding lane;
- How to handle the lateral behavior of occluded objects?

For unbounded occlusion, we should use **speed limits and RSS** for (responsibility-aware) bounding, similar to the idea in [\[9\]](#ref_9). By assuming the other vehicle reacts safely after detecting the ego vehicle, we obtain a reasonable speed range; on the other hand, we can perform **traffic-flow speed estimation**, which is a standard feature in efficiency-oriented lane changes. Together, the two yield a speed interval, which in turn gives a position interval over the horizon. Then, as mentioned in the framework above, we maintain a **velocity field based on a reachable set** to infer the blind-zone information.

For sparse observations, we still introduce **contingency planning**: eventually, as active-perception behavior (which I believe should not be generated by heuristic costs) progressively expands the visible region, and once the visible region covers the above position interval, we can determine that a lane change is safe. Thus, the final human-like behavior should be: slightly increase the gap to the lead vehicle, approach the target lane to shrink the blind zone, and finally decide whether to detour or change lanes. At the same time, we must ensure we can return to the safe invariant set at any time (possibly with some relaxation).

For lateral behavior, it depends on the modeling fidelity of longitudinal and lateral dynamics in the forward model of the reachable set. If the lateral/turning dynamics are modeled sufficiently completely, the lateral behavior can be forward-simulated.

### Scenario 2: Unprotected Left Turn at Intersection with Occluded Oncoming Traffic

![](https://pica.zhimg.com/v2-d8fafd2fd0032845bb780f23fa10e0b6_r.jpg)

As shown in the figure, the vehicle ahead proceeding straight through the intersection creates a blind spot for our vehicle (which intends to turn left) regarding the opposing through-lane. Although this scenario is relatively structured, the core challenge lies in the high level of interaction, which becomes even harder when combined with the occlusion. The structured nature manifests in the consistency between behavior and lane geometry: the occluded vehicle's intent is limited to either going straight or turning left, and the closer the occluded vehicle is to the straight or right-turn zone, the sooner it will emerge from the blind spot. Beyond contingency planning, some noteworthy approaches from the literature include:

[\[10\]](#ref_10) exploits the structure of strong interaction, **inferring possible behaviors within the blind spot via [game theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=270849690&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%9A%E5%BC%88%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity) based on the behavior of visible NPCs (e.g., the preceding vehicle)**. This is a theoretically insightful and potentially practical idea, although a reasonable implementation remains elusive and much work is still needed.

This paper assumes a fixed maximum number of occluded agents and formulates a bilevel optimization problem. The outer level performs parameter estimation (an inverse game problem), while the inner level solves a receding-horizon multi-agent contingency game whose equilibrium KKT conditions serve as constraints for the outer level. Clearly, this is only theoretically feasible:

- The contingency branches use fixed beliefs and manually fixed branching times, which is unreliable;
- In practice, if the inner problem were formulated exactly as described, the entire [optimization](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=270849690&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%97%AE%E9%A2%98%E6%B1%82%E8%A7%A3&zhida_source=entity) would be prohibitively cumbersome and slow;
- However, if the inner problem were drastically simplified to reduce the interaction between visible and occluded NPCs, the observability of the estimation would drop significantly, leading to severe ill-posedness with multiple solutions. Establishing precise [observability](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=270849690&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%AF%E8%A7%82%E6%B5%8B%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) conditions for the inverse game remains an open problem. Moreover, using an inverse-square distance term to model NPC interactions is clearly too crude.

Other work [\[8\]](#ref_8) **exploits the structure of the occluded vehicle's intent (as mentioned above, the occluded vehicle can only go straight or turn left, providing a strong prior via reference paths) to effectively prune the POMDP belief tree**. It also employs a warm start using an A* search combined with a constant-velocity rollout, guided by an "inevitable collision state" (ICS) heuristic. Additionally, it uses [Monte Carlo sampling](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=270849690&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%92%99%E7%89%B9%E5%8D%A1%E6%B4%9B%E9%87%87%E6%A0%B7&zhida_source=entity) with a [belief tree](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=270849690&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BF%A1%E5%BF%B5%E6%A0%91&zhida_source=entity), where possible actions at each node are pruned using Upper Confidence Bound (UCB), which is fairly standard in POMDP methods and need not be elaborated here. Of course, many other simplifications in this paper are rather unjustified [\[11\]](#ref_11).

### Scenario 3: Occlusion "Blind Spot Surprise" (Pedestrian Pop-out)

![](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-d1c7a4535bde5e1ef010043261487cdd_r.jpg)

This is the least structured scenario. Here, we do not assume that pedestrians must use crosswalks; pedestrians, children, and non-motorized vehicles can appear from any direction with bounded but arbitrary speeds. Their behavior is highly stochastic, with large speed variations; the only discernible characteristic might be that higher speeds imply lower steering capability.

In fact, reachable set velocity analysis is largely ineffective here, because the lateral motion characteristics of the occluded NPC are too strong. Predicting requires models, such as reachable set analysis, with powerful lateral extrapolation capabilities. However, even with the previously mentioned velocity fields, lateral extrapolation tends to make planning overly conservative.

What we can possibly do is construct "capsule"-shaped reachable sets along the occlusion boundary and roughly estimate velocities to obtain some information about the blind spot. Effective solutions likely all boil down to **contingency planning**—which is what human drivers naturally do in such scenarios: ensure that, when passing, one can brake to a full stop if forced to brake harder. Unfortunately, existing literature offers no effective methods, and it remains unclear how industry experts tackle this issue.

## III. Summary

Overall, among the vast body of literature, perhaps less than one in ten methods are effective on these benchmarks; the better ones merely offer insightful ideas tailored to the problem structure. Moreover, there is a notable scarcity of research on contingency planning in the occlusion domain, despite this being a promising direction.
