To document a hobby that is quite unique among those around me, and to summarize a methodology for learning mathematics that suits me—or, at the very least, one I am currently exploring—I have written this article. Readers are welcome to treat it as a story, and those so inclined may also glean some of my personal experience from it. As for the more substantive, technical aspects of my mathematical studies, I may continue to write about them in future articles; I also plan to write a book review–style piece, in part to prevent this article from becoming overly long and losing its focus.

During elementary school, middle school, and even the first two years of high school, I had no particular interest in mathematics beyond the ordinary. As a hallmark of the era, I even dabbled in what was called "[Olympiad Mathematics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211828161&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%A5%A5%E6%9E%97%E5%8C%B9%E5%85%8B%E6%95%B0%E5%AD%A6%E7%AB%9E%E8%B5%9B&zhida_source=entity)" in elementary school—it was something of a trend back then, much like the piano studios and taekwondo dojos that once dotted every street. At that time, I had no real understanding of mathematics; I only knew that university had a subject called "advanced mathematics." But everything began to shift in my third year of high school.

At that point, I was reviewing for the college entrance exam (gaokao). As my study of high school mathematics deepened, the mathematical materials I could access—or was capable of accessing—kept growing. Through Zhihu, I came across books like *The Secrets of Derivatives* and *The Secrets of Conic Sections* from Zhejiang University's Youxue series, as well as the *Do You Really Master the New Gaokao Mathematics?* series. These books revealed to me what in-depth study of high school mathematics could look like. From there, I embarked on my mathematical journey via Bilibili and Zhihu, lifting a corner of the veil over mathematics. Thanks to the workings of recommendation algorithms, as my engagement with math-related content grew, I inevitably got pushed more advanced mathematical material, including anecdotes about famous mathematicians and even popular science. This broadened my horizons considerably, and I began to understand what math majors—and mathematicians—actually do. It felt like I was gradually discovering the beauty of mathematics, yet at the same time, I wondered why that beauty never seemed to surface in high school math. Looking back, I realize that view was somewhat unfair, though there were underlying reasons for it.

I knew the immediate priority was the gaokao, so I didn't foolishly throw myself into mathematical fervor. Back then, my quiet goal was to get into an ordinary 985 university like [Shan Chuan Ji Lan](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211828161&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B1%B1%E5%B7%9D%E5%90%89%E5%85%B0&zhida_source=entity), or, failing that, a school like Harbin Institute of Technology at Weihai or Shandong University at Weihai. Little did I expect that I would struggle so much with the new gaokao math, especially after the Eight-Province Joint Exam, and my normally strong geography subject also inexplicably underperformed. In the end, I missed Shandong University at Weihai by a narrow margin and landed at a decent 211 university in a decent (engineering) major to carry on. Still, I never lost my love for mathematics. After quickly moving past the disappointment of the gaokao, I launched my own plan.

During the summer after the gaokao, I roughly drafted a plan for the future, including book selections and video courses. I started with Chen Jixiu's *Mathematical Analysis* from Fudan, and for algebra, I chose Qiu Weisheng's *Advanced Algebra* (the "white book"). My original idea was to pick between [Chen Jixiu](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211828161&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E9%99%88%E7%BA%AA%E4%BF%AE&zhida_source=entity) and Shi Jihuai based on accessibility, leaving more advanced options like Zorich or Rudin for later. I settled on Chen Jixiu, since Teacher 647, in his book-selection video, was rather unenthusiastic about the latter, while Zhihu opinions on both were fairly neutral. As I progressed, I came to understand Shi Jihuai's perspective and realized that Chen Jixiu was indeed simpler. For advanced algebra, I chose Qiu Weisheng over Yao Musheng, mostly without much rationale.

That summer, I slowly worked through the real numbers and limits sections of Chen Jixiu. Regrettably, despite planning, I still took a detour: I spent too long on the theory of real numbers. For beginners, this part is best skimmed to form a rough impression, then revisited later for deeper study. At the time, I was perhaps simply captivated by the elegance of the real number theory (wry smile).

Then the first semester of freshman year began, and here I would stumble into another detour—though perhaps not really one. As an engineering student, I had to take engineering mathematical analysis (which at our school was just a rebranding of advanced calculus, with the "more advanced" content neither taught nor tested) and linear algebra with analytic geometry. The school's self-compiled textbooks were, frankly, hard to describe. They could have simply used the standard Tongji calculus and linear algebra books, but instead insisted on this "engineering mathematical analysis," resulting in a mishmash of content. Several supplementary analysis topics, such as the Cauchy convergence principle and [uniform convergence](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211828161&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%B8%80%E8%87%B4%E6%94%B6%E6%95%9B&zhida_source=entity), were left isolated and unsupported, and the quality of the self-compiled material was itself unreliable. The linear algebra was merely the standard domestic fare—unremarkable and rather frustrating.

For me, this was the moment I dove into 3Blue1Brown's "Essence of Linear Algebra" on Bilibili, while also buying Strang's *Introduction to Linear Algebra*.

Unfortunately, I badly misjudged my efficiency. I ended up taking extremely detailed notes on the 3Blue1Brown videos—so detailed that I was recording nearly every sentence, and entirely in English. This revealed a major flaw of mine: I unconsciously let my notes become ever more granular instead of concise, and since I don't embrace paperless note-taking, I ended up doing all this work on A4 paper. In short, after the first semester of freshman year, I was deeply dissatisfied with my progress: neither fast enough nor deep enough. Many problems remained unresolved. For instance, I still hadn't fully completed the real number theory for single-variable calculus; the definition of the differential was still murky to me (a common ailment, I've heard); the invariance in the invariance of the differential form made me feel there must be deeper support behind it; my understanding of the relationships among convergence, continuity, differentiability, and continuity of the derivative for functions of one variable was not yet well constructed; and I was also digging into the [mean value theorem for integrals](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211828161&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%A7%AF%E5%88%86%E4%B8%AD%E5%80%BC%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity). As for algebra in the second semester, I fell into the rabbit hole of [Kostrikin](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=211828161&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9F%AF%E6%96%AF%E7%89%B9%E5%88%A9%E9%87%91&zhida_source=entity)'s *Introduction to Algebra* at the start of the term. I gradually came to understand why domestic linear algebra courses are arranged the way they are. Permutations, which form an important example—permutation groups—in group theory, are completely excised from engineering linear algebra, yet we cling to the permutation definition of determinants. This leaves the determinant's definition baffling to beginners, especially those in engineering.

Before the final exam week of the second semester, I managed to work through the early chapters of the first volume of *Introduction to Algebra* in my spare time, up to the ring and field sections, which serve as a preliminary to abstract algebra. Along the way, I gained a rather basic understanding of groups.

Of course, as an engineering student, I also took probability and statistics that semester, at the level required for the engineering graduate entrance exam. Since I don't have much interest in probability, I simply followed the school course earnestly, at most looking up Zhihu answers or articles to help with concepts—nothing more than standard.

Additionally, I took an elective course in numerical methods. The professor was excellent (huge praise for Professor Ling Huanzhang) and the course was very good, giving me a broad introduction to many topics in computational methods. However, since it was an elective with generally lower student levels, low requirements, and few contact hours, the course fell far short of a genuine numerical analysis course. Still, I learned a great deal. Here's the menu so you can judge the level: interpolation (Lagrange and Newton), function approximation and curve fitting (best square approximation and least squares), numerical integration (trapezoidal rule, Simpson's rule, Cotes formula, and their composite versions, plus Romberg integration), iterative methods for nonlinear equations (bisection, fixed-point iteration, Newton's method and variants), direct methods for linear systems ((column-pivoted) Gaussian elimination, LU decomposition, Thomas method, Cholesky decomposition, and ill-conditioned systems), iterative methods for linear systems (Jacobi, Gauss-Seidel, successive over-relaxation), and numerical solutions of ordinary differential equations (though by the end I'd forgotten what was covered).

During my studies, I also amassed a large collection of PDF resources, which proved immensely helpful—after all, it's well known that one shouldn't stare at a single book when learning math. The right approach is to use one primary text and others as references. Having so many PDFs saved me a lot of money and gave me a handy reference library I could print from when needed.

I want to mention two books in particular: Yu Pin's *Lecture Notes on Mathematical Analysis* and Li Wenwei's *Methods of Algebra*. These two made me see hope in the new generation of domestic math textbooks. Interestingly, both Yu Pin and Li Wenwei have excellent command of Chinese prose; their writing is not only a world apart from the older Soviet-style textbooks, but can even be called elegant. Both books are also very modern. The former is the lecture notes for Tsinghua's Qiuban (Qiu Chengtong) program, offering a glimpse of what top-tier contemporary textbooks should look like. Unfortunately, my own level is limited, so I've only read the real-number part of Yu Pin and a small portion of Li Wenwei's group theory. Still, I believe even that has broadened my horizons.

Here I'll quote the epigraph from the beginning of Yu Pin's book:

> But I would advise young scholars to read each book several times over. Books are as vast as the sea, containing all manner of treasures, and human energy cannot master them all at once—only what one seeks. Therefore, the learner should pursue a single aim each time. If one seeks the rise and fall of dynasties, or the works of sages, let that be the sole aim, with no other thoughts. On another pass, seek out historical facts, institutions and cultural relics, and so forth, likewise. All else follows this pattern. Though this method may seem slow and clumsy, when one's studies are complete, one will be able to withstand attacks from all sides, and cannot be compared to the mere dilettante.

> — Su Shi, *Second Letter in Reply to Wang Xiang*

By this point, my plans for future mathematical study were gradually becoming clearer, shifting from the initial "catch up with and surpass England and America" bravado to a more measured approach. In my first year, I intended to spend a year and a half finishing mathematical analysis (Chen Jixiu—certainly not at the level of Zorich) and advanced algebra. After that, I planned to move to abstract algebra on the algebra side, and on the analysis side, progress from real and complex analysis to functional analysis—or at least reach the level of applied functional analysis. I even had ambitions in other branches, such as touching algebraic topology after point-set topology, self-studying ODEs, and perhaps even dabbling in PDEs. I also planned to align mathematics with my major by studying graph theory, convex optimization, stochastic processes, and so on. Later, as I studied, I realized these ideas were somewhat divorced from reality—the plan was far too ambitious. After all, I have my own major to attend to, and studying in spare time couldn't achieve such high efficiency. So I adjusted my plans: no longer chasing quantity or setting overly specific goals. If I can lay a solid foundation in mathematical analysis and advanced algebra during college, that will be a success.

In the summer before my sophomore year, I signed up for the National College Mathematics Competition, partly hoping it would help with graduate school applications. Of course, I kept a level head, prepared earnestly, and didn't fixate on results. For this, I used Pu Heping's *Tutorial for the College Mathematics Competition*. Preparing for the competition was mostly about sharpening my exam-solving techniques. To be honest, I don't enjoy problem-solving—which is actually a dangerous mindset for learning mathematics—and I like even less drilling problems, which is equally dangerous for exam prep. Fortunately, I'm not a math major, but I will still face the math component of the graduate entrance exam. Also, some classmates asked me to form a team for mathematical modeling competitions, signing up for whatever came up. I agreed, though I felt somewhat helpless (wry smile)—I'll need to study up on algorithms.

While preparing for the math competition over the summer, knowing time was tight (the preliminary round was expected on November 12, 2022), I focused on key topics. Whether I picked the right ones, I still don't know, but over the break I reviewed higher-order derivatives, mean value theorems, integral inequalities, and a bit of infinite series, leaving the behemoth of limits for the start of the semester.

During this period of mathematical study, I gradually came to appreciate a key principle: subjects like these foundational math courses should be approached through a "first course" and a "second course." In plainer terms, I prefer to use a lower-level, shorter course to quickly cover the big picture, then follow up with a more detailed treatment to fill in the gaps. A major taboo in learning mathematics, I believe, is "seeing only the trees, not the forest." So my current approach is to use a preliminary first course plus survey-style articles on Zhihu to grasp the overall structure and key concepts, and only then dig into the finer properties and proofs (after all, some things can be understood even without proving them). Along the way, I've pondered the tension between mathematics as a hobby and my own major's coursework. Many people have asked me why I like mathematics; more often, they say this hobby is impressive and that I must be very smart.

But what I want to say is this: I'd rather go learn and do meaningful things I choose for myself than get caught up in the endless rat race within the academy. I'd already resolved this firmly at the start of my second semester. I will, of course, take my major coursework seriously and treat exams with due respect, but I won't spend enormous amounts of time on repetitive practice or resort to underhanded means just to squeeze out a few more points, as some classmates do. As for graduate school recommendations or the entrance exam, there's no reason for those to torment me now. I've even noticed that once I calmed down, I stopped being nervous during exams, my mind no longer went blank, I stopped underperforming, and my grades actually improved. This isn't "lying flat," nor is it "involution"—but I've found a space of my own. And mathematics is what keeps me company in that space.

To the curiosity or admiration of those around me, I'd say this: mathematics is quite forgiving. Perhaps researching it, or making a living as a mathematician, is not easy. But simply loving it, admiring it, and appreciating it is entirely within reach—this is also one reason I didn't transfer into a math major. I prefer the freedom of this hobby. As von Neumann once said: "If people do not believe that mathematics is simple, it is only because they do not realize how complicated life is." Indeed, compared to the true-and-false, real-and-illusory messiness of life, mathematics is on the whole beautiful and pure. This hobby isn't all that impressive, and liking mathematics doesn't require special intelligence.

In any case, I love mathematics. I admit that at first, there may have been a sense of superiority from knowing more than others. But gradually, that feeling was replaced by something purer—the joy of acquiring knowledge and perceiving beauty. When I'm feeling low, I sometimes choose to study math; it's a way for me to cope. That total immersion lets me temporarily step away from my emotions and also brings me happiness. Some people might say that mathematical ability sets the ceiling for one's development, or that math gives engineering a crushing advantage—I don't really care about any of that. I don't place mathematics in a utilitarian position; what I study now, for instance, is of no direct help to my major courses. My only hope is not to become a "terminology dabbler" and to have some real substance (my biggest issue right now is probably insufficient depth, especially in proofs).

Some say the beauty of mathematics felt by ordinary people differs from that felt by great mathematicians: the former is like touring the foothills of a great mountain, while the latter is the sublime vista after a grueling climb to the summit. I disagree with that. I'd simply say that beauty has no hierarchy, no distinction of high or low—everyone possesses this most basic, human emotion. But if I had to adopt that framing, I'd gladly be a humble sightseer at the foot of the lofty mountain of mathematics.
