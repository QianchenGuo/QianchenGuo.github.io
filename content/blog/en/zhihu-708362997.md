Recently, I had a conversation with a friend on my team who is gifted in research. He considers his main research direction to be information fusion. However, he doesn't know much about planning. The other day, the topic drifted somewhere, and he asked me to explain roughly what planning is about. Naturally, I didn't cater to trendy topics like autonomous driving or try to give an overly formal definition. Instead, I tried to convey that planning is "moving a point from one place to another in a space," and that the fundamental difficulty lies in it being an NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem. But perhaps he didn't pick up on my serious tone, as he replied, "So, what deep intellectual innovations does your branch have? It feels like your innovations aren't in your field, but in optimization." This led me to reflect further, and I realized this touches on some very big questions, which ultimately gave rise to this article.

The purpose of this article is to **deeply analyze my understanding of planning from a planner's perspective, discuss the inherent challenges in planning itself, and explore the underlying logic of current classical frameworks in addressing these challenges, ultimately pointing to the enormous difficulties in achieving robot intelligence**.

This article primarily elucidates the foundational logic of control and even planning, but with a greater focus on control. Its bottom-up exposition leads me to call it "clarifying the fundamentals."

## I. What is Planning[\[1\]](#ref_1)

### (I) The Generality of Planning—State Space[\[2\]](#ref_2) [\[3\]](#ref_3)

First, it should be pointed out that planning algorithms are not only applicable to motion planning for mobile robots. As I said, planning is merely "**moving a point from one place to another in a space**." This may sound easy, but we require the process of moving to satisfy a series of our constraints[\[4\]](#ref_4). So, roughly described in natural language, the problem becomes "how to find a way to move a point from one place to another while satisfying the requirements."

The first question is the definition of "a space," which actually corresponds to the **state space** induced by the existence of an entity. This was already discussed in my previous article[\[5\]](#ref_5), i.e., the underlying manifold of a dynamical system (or one of its bundle manifolds). As long as a substance exists, we can choose several of its attributes to quantify, thereby obtaining a state space. Then, moving a point from one place to another in the state space is simply transforming one state of a thing into another. Constructing a state space involves only two issues: the issue of state selection and the issue of constraints. Constraints are easy to understand—they impose restrictions on the space, shrinking its scope, and potentially reducing degrees of freedom and connectivity.

For robot motion planning, we call the state space the configuration space. Below, I use specific examples unrelated to robotics to illustrate the generality of planning:

Example 1: Any game (e.g., board games, puzzles, Rubik's cubes, or jigsaw puzzles)

A while ago, I came across an article on Zhihu about a highly intelligent person from a prestigious university who, at a class reunion, was asked whether he played games. He showed off by saying he only played three types of games: "forming certain combinations to score higher than others... (details forgotten)." But if you really dig into games, they essentially involve reaching a certain state of the game while satisfying the constraints of the rules. All games are a form of planning. If a game has too many constraints, it's hard to find a good solution (corresponding to a game that's no fun and almost unplayable). If a game has too few constraints, there may well be a winning strategy (like Gomoku, which is in such an awkward position).

For example, board games like Go are about making the game state reach a certain configuration under rule constraints—i.e., reaching a feasible region in the state space—to win. Worth noting, for computers and human brains, the perceived complexity differs. Humans might think becoming a Go master is hard, but for a computer, it's just planning within a finite and not very large state set (space) to find "the path to victory." Moreover, many games have symmetries in their rules that reduce complexity. For instance, the Rubik's cube's state space is a finite group. Mathematically, finite group theory can be used effectively to find solutions to the Rubik's cube—there exists a "theory of everything" for it.

Example 2: Protein folding and drug design

Interactions between macromolecules can, to some extent, explain certain biological phenomena. This is crucial for healthcare and drug development. Planning algorithms play a significant role here: how to get macromolecules to fold in the desired way? Can a molecule insert itself, and how, into the space surrounded by larger molecules? How to make molecules react in the desired manner to increase the yield of a product? You'll find that examining these problems naturally requires considering the state space of molecular poses, as well as constraints like flexible molecular dynamics and reaction kinetics—i.e., the requirements during "moving." These problems share commonalities with robot path/trajectory planning and the games discussed above.

From these examples, we can glimpse that **the generality of planning roughly aligns with the generality of state spaces**. As I mentioned earlier, from a philosophical standpoint, any "existing" object can be abstracted into states, hence the concept of state space. This raises two questions:

1. How to abstract states?

We can generally sense the existence of state abstraction, but can we go beyond existence and provide a construction method?

(1) First, **is the abstracted state meaningful?** This depends on the scope of the problem. For instance, if you're studying chess strategy, you only need to abstract the positions of different pieces on the board, not their heights or masses; for mobile robot motion planning, you focus on states related to robot kinematics and dynamics. This criterion applies to any applied mathematics or physics problem—it's a basic principle of mathematical modeling, so much so that it seems like a tautology.

(2) Second, **is the abstracted state complete?** This also depends on how much we simplify the problem. For example, in low-speed macroscopic mechanics, Newtonian mechanics is a complete framework. Within this framework, Newton's principle of determinacy states that the initial positions and velocities of all points in a mechanical system uniquely determine its motion[\[6\]](#ref_6). Through differential calculus, initial positions and velocities yield accelerations. Thus, the foundation of Newtonian mechanics—Newton's equations—is established. For such a system of particles, position and velocity form a complete set of states.

But we must understand that, in many cases, a complete set of states is not easy to find. Newton's greatness lies in that even setting aside his contribution to calculus, he keenly abstracted the key states of macroscopic low-speed objects and used complete states to produce such an elegant theoretical framework. The situations we face are mostly—complex problems before us, where we can only try to simplify them, but without knowing whether our simplifications are too aggressive or too conservative, to the point where we can't tell if the candidate states at hand are deficient, complete, or redundant. Redundancy is rare; completeness is hard to come by (you can't expect a Newton to appear often); deficiency is common and most vexing. Many issues in planning implementation and even in science stem from state deficiency, leaving us confined to a subspace.

Here's an example of expanding the state space to broaden perspectives: [Flavius Julianus: Trajectory Planning, Behavior Decision-making, and Latent Variables](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/688372564)

(3) Finally, **how to abstract states**,

For an actual system, this is an experimental question. Or, further, a data-based question. But before our data scientists and the general public get overly excited, thinking of AI and big data, let's return to rational thinking.

Recall the work humanity has done: simply put, much of the work in fields like system identification in control theory, machine learning in AI, and statistics in mathematics (some argue statistics is parallel to mathematics, but that's not important) is about (non)parametric estimation (here, "estimation" is broad, not just from random disturbances). This essentially helps us determine states and unknown parameters. In other words, the problem of abstracting states (including determining parameters) may go by different names in different contexts: filtering, estimation, pattern recognition, feature extraction, (un)supervised learning, (non)parametric estimation... Humanity has indeed done considerable work here. Mathematically, these methods each have mature solutions, and some directions are even merging. But are these states truly useful? When facing an actual complex problem involving behavior decision-making and task planning, are position and velocity still complete? If not, do we have good methods to find those "seemingly from the void" deficient states?

From Newton's example, we see that for states to be practical, they must—putting completeness aside—have a certain intuitiveness. This intuitiveness might be physical (like velocity and acceleration) or theoretical (like reactance and impedance). In other words, it's either tied to human perception or closely integrated with other parts of the theoretical framework; otherwise, arbitrary linear combinations from state sets wouldn't be conceived by humanity even over millennia. This implies that states obtained via statistics or machine learning are unlikely to meet intuitiveness requirements—a rather depressing problem. And even someone as great as Newton had to seek patterns from predecessors' mechanics experiments to propose the principle of determinacy. For today's problems, far more elusive than mechanical frameworks, what experiments do we have, and what have we gleaned? Unless a great mind proposes a set of states that's not only intuitive but also complete, we can only make supplementary patches on deficient states.

2. Static Planning and Generalized Dynamic Planning[\[7\]](#ref_7) [\[8\]](#ref_8)

Planning can be divided into static and dynamic types. **Static planning's** most classic subsets include traditional optimization theory, roughly divided into gradient-based optimization, heuristic optimization, zero-order and black-box optimization; it also includes static estimation in statistics, such as least squares and minimum variance estimation. The reason these are just subsets is that static planning only indicates time-invariant constraints, without requiring optimality. Without optimality, there's no clear direction, which is why optimization theory, an applied mathematics branch, pervades so many mathematically involved fields.

The **generalized dynamic planning** discussed here is not the dynamic programming proposed by Bellman, but rather planning problems with time-varying constraints. The most classic subset of dynamic planning is control theory, especially optimal control, which is optimal planning with dynamic constraints; MPC is receding-horizon optimal planning with both dynamic constraints and additional static/dynamic constraints[\[9\]](#ref_9) [\[10\]](#ref_10) [\[11\]](#ref_11). Dynamic programming, on the other hand, is just one method for solving optimal control problems.

First, the fruits of the "grand view of control"[\[12\]](#ref_12) I proposed earlier are, in a sense, the dynamic planning discussed here; second, as Professor Gao Fei from Zhejiang University put it—**planning is open-loop control, and control is closed-loop planning**—this also reflects the "grand view of control." Specifically, **control theory also concerns the stability and robustness of the state-point moving process**, which corresponds to the so-called "closed loop," leading to the concept of the region of attraction in state space. In comparison, control considers more aspects of the same problem, making it harder; conversely, given the many constraints, it's difficult to consider as much as control does, so we sometimes compromise, reducing focus on robustness. Thus, **simply viewing control theory as an optimization problem is biased, neglecting the significance of robustness to the system, and one shouldn't forget that planning, even with compromises, ultimately needs robustness.** However, theory keeps developing; roughly speaking, MPC aims to solve constrained optimal control problems, attempting to simultaneously address optimality and robustness under constraints.

3. Is "Moving" the Ultimate Answer to "Problem Solving"?

Back to planning itself: can planning solve all problems? This question seems overblown. Looking back at history, when artificial intelligence was first proposed, when symbolism, connectionism, and behaviorism flourished on this planet, people expected AI to be a "problem solver" (query solver). So, can planning, as a "state-point mover," be a universal "problem solver"?

Unfortunately, I can't directly answer this difficult question; I can only offer some reflections. Perhaps after reading more AI-related books, the answer will become clearer.

Moving a state point is itself an act of exploring the state space. This exploration, via moving, probes the path connectivity of the state space. We typically think that if we can traverse the state space, the answer to the problem is naturally in sight. But can we be more efficient (as later sections show, efficiency is crucial) and obtain the solution with only limited exploration? Planning is essentially doing exactly this. That is, **planning isn't just about moving; it's also about thinking how to explore more efficiently.**

### (II) Elements of Planning

1. State and State Space: The state space can be discrete, corresponding to finite or countably infinite states; or continuous, corresponding to uncountably infinite states. It can be very concrete, closely tied to the physical world (e.g., the configuration space in robot motion planning is constructed from the physical world); or highly abstract, unable to be explicitly represented.

2. Time: All dynamic planning problems involve a sequence of actions over time. Time can be explicit (model parameters and states are time-varying) or implicit, embedded in the order of actions. Of course, continuous time can also be discretized.

3. Actions and Action Space: Planning generates actions that operate on states, called control inputs in control theory. Additionally, the environment acts on the system under study, which we typically treat as non-omniscient disturbances.

4. Initial and Goal States: Planning involves starting from an initial state and reaching a specific goal state or a set of goal states.

## II. The Fundamental Challenges of Planning

In the previous section, we established the state space and basic elements of planning. Below, we address the intrinsic problems within this defined domain.

### (I) Essence—NP-hard Nonconvex Combinatorial Optimization

Motion planning within planning is NP-hard; some proofs of this have been discussed in a previous article[\[13\]](#ref_13). This indicates that no method can, in any general case, find a solution quickly (in polynomial time). Piecewise continuous, non-algebraic collision-free constraints naturally give rise to combinatorial optimization problems. In fact, NP-hardness and "combinatorial" are closely linked, as many combinatorial problems exhibit combinatorial explosion. Below, we elaborate from the combinatorial and nonconvex perspectives.

### (II) Combinatorial Explosion and the Curse of Dimensionality[\[14\]](#ref_14) [\[15\]](#ref_15)

For combinatorial optimization, the main exact methods include branch-and-bound and dynamic programming. Both essentially aim to find special structures in combinatorial problems to reduce search space and improve search efficiency. Dynamic programming, in particular, exploits recursive optimal substructures. However, for some problems, combinatorial explosion prevents real-time solutions via dynamic programming. Bellman proposed dynamic programming and the concept of the **"curse of dimensionality."** Powell argues that the curse of dimensionality has three main causes: large action spaces, large state spaces, and random disturbances. Moreover, dimensionality not only limits computational efficiency but also affects solution quality. High-dimensional states have far more degrees of freedom than low-dimensional ones, and due to significant computational errors, it's hard to generalize low-dimensional results to high-dimensional cases, causing issues for existing methods.

Many problems have extremely high search dimensions; many people may not realize that tasks often considered complex, like playing Go, have state spaces far smaller in dimensionality than some everyday tasks. An example of this can be found in Part IV of this article.

### (III) Nonconvexity

It's easy to see that the real world is full of nonconvex, dynamic, complex environments, with complex and time-varying path homotopy classes. If we don't simplify problems by exploiting their structure and crudely convert them into optimization problems, we get extremely complex nonconvex problems, and the search space may even be disconnected. This not only leads to getting stuck in local minima but also, with missing connectivity, general numerical optimization algorithms can't guarantee finding feasible solutions. I've heard that many people work on such directions, but few consider the fundamental difficulties of nonconvexity and combinatorial nature.

## III. Classical Frameworks for the Fundamental Challenges[\[16\]](#ref_16) [\[17\]](#ref_17) [\[18\]](#ref_18) [\[19\]](#ref_19)

For these fundamental challenges, our current frameworks use various means to reduce their difficulty, thereby solving problems to some extent. In [Gongqingcheng Gemini: Random Thoughts on Mathematics and Control (Continued) Clarifying the Fundamentals](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/698176373?utm_psn=1843026715376492545), I proposed the grand view of control and a series of underlying logics. For planning, these logics are the same, though with differences in details. Below, I use this framework to interpret the essence of **existing motion planning frameworks**.

### (I) Topological Constraints and Integrability

First, planning problems, compared to general control problems, involve much more complex state-space topology—high dimensionality, various nonconvexities, and even poor connectivity. The aforementioned fundamental challenges reflect these well, indicating the significant impact of topology on planning problems.

Given a state space, its topological connectivity can be characterized via decomposition, partitioning the state space into a finite number of cells. Clearly, in dynamic planning, this topology is time-varying and uncertain, which led to my earlier question "[Does there exist a mathematical branch of random topology?](https://www.zhihu.com/question/668234983)". Later, I found that current motion planning research doesn't explicitly consider this but instead directly considers Markov chains on multi-agent state spaces, forming trajectory trees for multi-body systems, where branches represent different real-world scenarios—i.e., different topologies—and Bayesian inference is used on the tree. Some works also address topological jumps in decomposition due to perception uncertainty, using filtering or temporal Bayesian inference to make topological relations robust—a nontrivial problem.

Integrability is more mundane in planning, since, without considering robustness, planning focuses only on a single trajectory. We seek the optimal state trajectory. Even with robustness, we should require dynamic integrability, because without integrability, planning search is impossible.

### (II) Hierarchical Decomposition[\[20\]](#ref_20)

Hierarchical decomposition is an effective way to reduce combinatorial explosion complexity. Earlier, I said: "Planning isn't just about moving; it's also about thinking how to explore more efficiently." Exploration can be divided into global and local aspects. Global refers to the overall topological properties of the state space; local corresponds to analytical properties, or more specifically, calculus properties (though not exclusively—it could be discrete differences and sums after discretization). In hierarchical decomposition, we separate problems by scale and use approximation methods suited to each scale, yielding better results than mixing all problems together with a single approximation method.

### (III) Global Planning[\[21\]](#ref_21)

At large scales, our primary task is exploring topological properties, essentially partitioning homotopy classes in the state space—a typical combinatorial problem. Of course, we must acknowledge that homotopy classes are trivial in 3D space, so some have proposed visibility deformation, which essentially does the same thing. We can coarsen the state space, losing local information. A key approach to coarsening is discretization.

There are two discretization strategies: one discretizes the control space, where, over a fixed time step, the discretized action space applied to the state yields a finite (or countable) set of motion primitives, called **forward discretization**; the other discretizes the state space, then from the current initial state, we find finite (or countable) motion primitives connecting to the discrete (goal) state space, called **reverse discretization**. Search-based and sampling-based path planning correspond to special cases of these two discretizations. Search-based methods often simply discretize the control space into four- or eight-connected moves, i.e., control inputs allow only movements in these directions, constructing a graph; sampling-based path planning essentially randomly discretizes the state space. Of course, more technical approaches exist for discretizing the state space, such as constructing Voronoi diagrams or decompositions.

Don't think that forward discretization always requires building grid maps, or that state-space discretization always requires random sampling. These are just **two different discretization ideas for transforming high-dimensional complex continuous state-space planning into graph problems or graph search problems**.

The above discretization + graph search methods can solve homotopy class partitioning. However, too-high dimensionality makes any simple graph search algorithm nearly non-real-time, necessitating pruning operations to eliminate clearly non-optimal paths by introducing geometric or kinematic constraints. Those working on global planning nowadays, like Sven Koenig and Maxim Likhachev, publish less in this area. There are also other methods for searching homotopy classes, such as gradient-based ones that set two (p,v) pairs with opposing vectors on an obstacle to guide generated trajectory primitives toward different local minima. From my limited understanding, searching homotopy classes as a combinatorial problem lacks systematic methods beyond discretization; most approaches are highly technique-driven.

### (IV) Local Planning

For local problems, we need to consider differential constraints, dynamic constraints, and so on. In such cases, the problem is typically transformed into trajectory optimization methods. These scale well with the state space and are less affected by the curse of dimensionality. However, even setting aside combinatorial issues, non-convexity remains a rather awkward problem. After obtaining the homotopy class partition, performing trajectory optimization within a given homotopy class still faces intrinsically non-convex problems. In other words, if we "crudely" convert the problem into an optimization problem, we will invariably end up with a high-dimensional, non-convex, complex optimization problem with difficult hard constraints. Current approaches to tackle non-convexity mainly include:

1. **Converting hard constraints to soft constraints**

   Under non-convex constraints, relying solely on local derivative information can at best guarantee convergence to a local optimum, and may even fail to converge. Furthermore, hard constraints can introduce discontinuities and uncertainty due to measurement noise. If all hard constraints are converted to soft constraints, we obtain an unconstrained nonlinear optimization problem. Unfortunately, a local optimum under soft constraints does not guarantee that the hard constraints of the original problem are satisfied.

2. **Convexification**

   Here, we need to ensure both that the optimization result is sufficiently good and that real-time performance is guaranteed. This requires that the algorithm is not a trivial, inefficient "brute-force" approach—this is the motivation behind convexification. Convexification essentially transforms a non-convex problem into one or more convex problems. This requires us to exploit the structure of the optimization problem and specifically process it into a convex formulation using various techniques.

   Over a decade ago, methods using corridors of convex polytopes within each homotopy class were developed. These work well in static environments, but approximating convexity becomes difficult in dynamic environments. Moreover, in dynamic environments, the number of homotopy classes can be very large, and not all of them contain feasible solutions. Balancing safety and efficiency becomes a key issue. A 2018 paper pointed out that this method has many issues when applied to robots that require fast reaction and replanning in cluttered environments, such as small UAVs.

3. **Combining soft constraints with spline curves**

   This is essentially a combination of the previous two approaches. The convex hull property of spline curves is used to confine the trajectory within the convex hull formed by the control points. The optimization part is formulated as a soft-constrained optimization, with the control points as the optimization variables. This avoids the drawback that soft constraints do not necessarily guarantee the satisfaction of the original constraints, while also simplifying the problem into a relatively easy unconstrained optimization. Of course, polynomial curves like splines are not optimal for all dynamic situations, but they are suitable for differentially flat systems or those with simple dynamics (e.g., integrator chains).

### (V) Remaining Open Problems [\[22\]](#ref_22) [\[23\]](#ref_23)

Even with the technical treatments mentioned above within the current framework, a series of problems remains. I believe the following are some core issues, which largely correspond to currently active research directions.

1. **Limitations of convexification and more natural costs**

   To cast problems into a convex form, we actually limit the scope of problems we can solve. This is particularly evident in the construction of optimization cost functions; clearly, for many tasks, a naturally constructed cost function should be quite complex. Treating constraints as convex is also just a sequential approximation. Fortunately, the ongoing development of optimization theory has given us considerable capability to handle non-convex, non-smooth, and even stochastic optimization problems, and various black-box optimization methods have advanced significantly. We therefore need not be confined to the shackles of convexification, but rather **construct more natural, task-oriented cost functions** [\[24\]](#ref_24) [\[25\]](#ref_25) [\[26\]](#ref_26).

   Some works directly tackle combinatorial optimization, such as methods based on graphs of convex sets. After transforming into mixed-integer programming, tight convex relaxations and efficient approximation techniques can drastically reduce computation time. GCS is a well-known work in this area [\[27\]](#ref_27), and subsequent improvements have significantly reduced computation time [\[28\]](#ref_28). However, it seems that real-time application on mobile robots is still not achievable.

   But the fundamental principle is that there is no "silver bullet." For problems that are inherently NP-hard, we cannot insist on optimality. We should **embrace "acceptable suboptimality"**, which is a more effective path towards achieving ideal planning.

2. **Dynamic environments and interaction with other agents** [\[29\]](#ref_29)

   When other dynamic obstacles or agents are present, perception of the dynamic environment and the behavior of other agents introduce uncertainty [\[30\]](#ref_30). Time-varying and uncertain homotopy classes become difficult to define and compute, with high computational complexity [\[31\]](#ref_31). Some straightforward approaches currently involve **constructing task-specific cost functions oriented towards dynamic obstacles**. For example, perception-aware tasks. A more fundamental approach is to **tightly couple prediction and decision modules with the planning part**, not just considering other dynamic obstacles from an individual perspective, as other agents' trajectories will also change in response to our actions. This naturally involves **game theory**, psychology, and modeling of social norms. Many recent papers on autonomous driving actively integrate differential games with planning [\[32\]](#ref_32) [\[33\]](#ref_33) [\[34\]](#ref_34) [\[35\]](#ref_35).

3. **Complex dynamics**

   I have been more exposed to Professor Fei Gao's work, but for UAVs, differential flatness is a crucial property that allows decoupling of high-dimensional planning problems. For instance, in perception-aware tasks for quadrotors mentioned earlier, many works rely on the assumption that the field of view only depends on the yaw angle, which is based on differential flatness. For more general **nonlinear, underactuated, nonholonomic systems**, considering the robustness of planning becomes much more difficult. This relies on the development of better MPC (RHC) and other control algorithms.

4. **Completeness**

   Under current conditions, the **coupling of modules** in the "prediction-decision-planning-control" pipeline is a mainstream trend (of course, this coupling is not the "end-to-end" kind). For these coupled layers, designing the interaction and coordination between layers to ensure a certain degree of completeness is an important issue. In the ideal case of the global-local hierarchical framework in planning: the global planner provides all non-homotopic paths (enumerating all homotopy classes), and the local planner finds the (locally) optimal solution within a homotopy class. How the upper and lower layers ensure consistency of interaction with other agents is also a significant problem. However, some works do not crudely separate planning into global and local layers but instead use an integrated global optimization algorithm [\[36\]](#ref_36), which avoids the coordination problem, albeit at the cost of increased overall difficulty.

It can be said that the above problems are core issues currently facing motion planning algorithms. Of course, in practical engineering, we face many more problems. You can refer to Professor Fei Gao's answer: [What are the research directions and difficulties in motion and path planning?](https://www.zhihu.com/question/337213590/answer/764739479?utm_psn=1851451259283984385)

## IV. Task Planning, Behavior Decision, and Intelligence

A robotic system follows the classic technical chain: design — manufacturing — calibration — perception — recognition — decision — planning — feedback control — communication. Many people might think that the most intelligent tasks are visual recognition or language, but in reality, the pitfalls in the decision and planning modules are far deeper.

### (I) The "Expert Effect" of Intelligence and the Curse of Dimensionality

**Task-level planning** is extremely complex; the complexity of motion far exceeds that of vision or language modules. Decision and planning are the core of achieving intelligence. In existing legged robots, many tasks tend to be hardcoded for specific scenarios. Even disregarding this, many tasks can only achieve Pick&Place (or Pick&Place-like) operations without truly confronting the construction of task planning and behavior decision capabilities brought about by intelligence. Through the long course of human evolution, we seem to have fallen into an "expert effect" regarding intelligence; we have no way of knowing how we learned certain things—this is known as Moravec's paradox. Many tasks that humans learn easily or even possess innately seem to present an insurmountable chasm for robots under the current technological stack [\[37\]](#ref_37).

Let's start with two examples of the curse of dimensionality: [\[38\]](#ref_38) [\[39\]](#ref_39)

Example 1: Folding clothes

This requires generalization to arbitrary shapes, materials, patterns, and internal structures.

Suppose there is an image library of clothes containing 1000 images of different styles, sizes, and materials. Clearly, each piece of clothing has different geometric information and (soft-body) dynamic information, but this cannot be directly obtained—only image information is available. The table has 10000 pixels, and there is a piece of clothing on the table. When spread out, the clothing occupies at most 1000 pixels. In the action space, there are two actions: Pick and Place. Pick involves selecting a subset of the 1000 points and grasping at a certain height (height is discretized into 20 levels). As you grasp part of the clothing, the pixels occupied change because the clothing is deformable. Place involves selecting arbitrary pixels on the table and releasing. One operation consists of a set of Pick&Place pairs. The observed effective information is an RGB image and a depth image. Suppose the task provides you with the flat state A of the clothing and the folded states Bi (i=1,…,n). How can you reach the ideal state Bi from a messy clothing state C in fewer than 10 operations?

Example 2: Knitting a sweater

From a ball of yarn to knitting a sweater, an LVM can only recognize the yarn and the sweater; an LLM can only search its database for knitting-related knowledge through enumeration and combination. So how do we plan the 100,000 complex actions involving the 10 motors and 1000 parameters of a robot to knit a sweater? Moreover, different action combinations can lead to completely different overall plans. The entire state space is combinatorially explosive, with astronomically large numbers of states. This becomes one of the biggest challenges on our path to "artificial intelligence"—dynamic planning for complex, high-level tasks.

Clearly, the combinatorial explosion of state points here is unimaginable. One of the original motivations for reinforcement learning, or approximate dynamic programming, was to accelerate search or planning under combinatorial explosions so that planning can proceed [\[40\]](#ref_40) [\[41\]](#ref_41) [\[42\]](#ref_42) [\[43\]](#ref_43). However, for these complex tasks, reinforcement learning cannot guarantee effective "exploration" of the state space, let alone solve the planning problem. Even planning is difficult; let alone real physical-world control. Even if we don't discuss the curse of dimensionality, task planning and decision-making themselves are inherently difficult to solve.

### (II) Task Planning and Decision Making [\[44\]](#ref_44)

I asked a friend in computer science: in recent years, learning has swept through image processing, then signal processing, followed by large models and various modalities... where will the next "explosion point" be? He said he only works on small-scale signal processing, but he felt the next step for the learning community is advancing into LLM reasoning and similar inference capabilities. Personally, I don't quite believe what those American companies claim about AI learning to reason within five years. I personally think reasoning is also a problem that decision and planning should consider—that is, for a task, we need to determine a sequence of actions, which is the concept of task planning.

Without reasoning, tasks cannot be decomposed, nor can generalization be achieved (indeed, I believe that before AI truly learns to reason, claims of generalization are essentially false). One could say that reasoning should be integrated into decision and planning, and conversely, the development of decision and planning enhances reasoning capabilities. In the motion planning section (Part III), I mentioned coupling prediction, decision, and planning, and using game theory based on task understanding (e.g., prior knowledge like traffic rules, human psychology) to construct interaction—this is a reasoning process. This work nicely demonstrates the mutual promotion of reasoning and decision/planning.

Returning to the current classic framework, one cannot help but ask: can we construct "task-level" cost functions to solve an optimization problem? Superficially, we can propose some reasonable, task-related cost functions. We rely on human understanding of the task to construct a so-called "cost." Going in circles, we seem to return to the issue of missing states discussed in Part I. For tasks more complex than driving—where "complex" means complex for robots—we have long since lost the ability, due to the "expert effect," to capture the crucial states in these tasks. When will we be able to distill, from certain tasks and experiments, a complete set of states that are crucial for the successful completion of one or more tasks?

## V. Me and Planning

### (I) Why I Wrote This Article

I believe a major purpose of this type of article is similar to the Bourbaki school's structural approach to mathematics: dissecting the underlying logic of planning. This allows us to overview the development trajectory of planning, which I find quite helpful for research—facilitating the understanding of existing frameworks and even proposing one's own work. This more general perspective, this style of "building solid camps and fighting stubborn battles," helps cultivate powerful, problem-solving thinking like Faltings' approach to mathematics—clearing obstacles wherever they appear.

Moreover, whether in academia or industry, having this kind of thinking can prevent repeatedly reinventing work already done years ago. While reinventing the wheel can fool outsiders, to insiders it is merely laughable. Furthermore, so-called rule-based approaches that only handle problems case-by-case are at best expert systems and cannot guarantee true problem-solving. Writing this article is itself a form of problem-solving through planning. However, many people's mental models of planning do not conform to the constraints of logic; instead of being steady and methodical, they sway like a drunkard—naturally making problem-solving quite difficult (laughs).

### (II) Why I Do (Motion) Planning

Regarding planning, to put it nobly, it is a cornerstone in humanity's current pursuit of understanding "what is intelligence." Pragmatically speaking, pursuing traditional control theory as commonly understood today offers little in terms of academic novelty or financial prospects (of course, one can pursue an academic career in control theory, or work in precision motion control and other fields requiring deeper theory, but that is not my aspiration). Planning, however, still has a thriving landscape. In a sense, it's a "strategic shift" for a theorist.

I have always upheld a spirit of rationality and have a deep love for mathematics, especially control theory, optimization theory, and nonlinear-related branches of mathematics (Hamiltonian systems, nonlinear analysis, etc.). Planning has given me a broader perspective than traditional control. I appreciate this perspective (for specific reasons, see the "Why I Wrote This Article" section).

### (III) Response to a Friend's Critique

He said: "Does your branch have any deep intellectual innovation? It feels like your innovations aren't in your field, but in optimization."

If one thinks that formulating a problem as an optimization problem means the innovation lies in optimization, then one might as well dismiss most of applied mathematics. Even your own field of information fusion—the so-called Bayesian methods—ultimately boils down to optimization.

If everyone is doing optimization, what constitutes innovation? The answer lies in my article above.

First, there are many essential and non-essential problems that haven't been solved well, leaving much to be desired both theoretically and practically. Second, task planning and behavior decision-making are also huge problems; we cannot always rely on a learning-based black box to tell us what to do. Instead, we must uncover the hidden state variables associated with real-world tasks and behaviors, incorporate them into our state space, which in turn requires a deep analysis of tasks and behaviors in the physical world. Then, we use scientific methods to approximate or even describe ambiguous concepts like tasks and behaviors. Finally, I believe that only when we truly achieve good planning can we truly touch the edge of "intelligence."
