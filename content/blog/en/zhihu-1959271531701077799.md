This article aims to systematically introduce [model-based methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%89%E6%A8%A1%E5%9E%8B%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity). As the name suggests, model-based methods require a [surrogate model](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BB%A3%E7%90%86%E6%A8%A1%E5%9E%8B&zhida_source=entity). Incidentally, zero-order optimization has become a highly vibrant branch of [applied mathematics](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%BA%94%E7%94%A8%E6%95%B0%E5%AD%A6&zhida_source=entity). In recent years, there has been clear evidence of deep cross-fertilization between distribution geometry ([information geometry](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BF%A1%E6%81%AF%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity), optimal transport and [Wasserstein geometry](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Wasserstein%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95&zhida_source=entity)), Bayesian statistics, [sampling theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%87%87%E6%A0%B7%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity), zero-order optimization, statistical physics, [mean-field theory](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B9%B3%E5%9D%87%E5%9C%BA%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA&zhida_source=entity), energy-based methods in learning, model-based diffusion, and other applied mathematics directions. These fields are thriving and mutually reinforcing. **The vitality of a research direction is reflected in its rich intersections with multiple fields, its abundance of high-value core problems, and the rich structures it embodies.**

## 1. Gradient Estimation Methods

The core idea is to approximate the gradient $\nabla f$, then substitute the approximate gradient $g$ for the true gradient in gradient-based [optimization methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%98%E5%8C%96%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity). The gradient is local information, and differentiability is also a [local property](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%B1%80%E9%83%A8%E6%80%A7%E8%B4%A8&zhida_source=entity). This requires us to sample local zero-order information.

### 1. [Finite Difference Method](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9C%89%E9%99%90%E5%B7%AE%E5%88%86%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity)

$$
g_{i}=\frac {f(x+\alpha e_{j})-f(x)}{\alpha}e_{j}
$$

 Using finite differences to approximate the gradient, different step sizes are applied across different dimensions for one-point or two-point differencing. Some of the stochastic gradient estimators below generally serve as better alternatives.

Adding a random index $j=random\{1,...,n\}$ yields [random coordinate](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9A%8F%E6%9C%BA%E5%9D%90%E6%A0%87&zhida_source=entity) descent search. When gradient estimation is accurate, for [coordinate descent](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9D%90%E6%A0%87%E4%B8%8B%E9%99%8D%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity)[\[1\]](#ref_1)—the Gauss-Southwell method—it can be shown that for a local (convex) problem, the bound obtained by a gradient step is a lower bound on the bound obtained by Gauss-Southwell with n-1 iterations. Roughly speaking, when gradients are available, n-1 coordinate descent steps have a search effect similar to one gradient step. In fact, this depends on the degree of Hessian decoupling: when the problem variables are largely independent (the Hessian is nearly diagonal), coordinate descent performs slightly better than a gradient step. For certain specific functions, it achieves the same number of steps as [gradient descent](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A2%AF%E5%BA%A6%E4%B8%8B%E9%99%8D&zhida_source=entity) while reducing function evaluations (zero-order oracle calls) by a factor of n.

### 2. Simultaneous Perturbation [Stochastic Approximation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9A%8F%E6%9C%BA%E8%BF%91%E4%BC%BC&zhida_source=entity) (SPSA)

$g=\frac {f(x+\alpha \Delta)-f(x-\alpha \Delta)}{2\alpha}\Delta$, where the components of $\Delta$ follow a [Bernoulli distribution](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Bernoulli%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83&zhida_source=entity) taking values in $\{±1\}$. Under certain reasonable assumptions, SPSA achieves performance comparable to finite difference methods while reducing function evaluations by a factor of n.

### 3. Stochastic Smoothed Gradient Estimation[\[2\]](#ref_2) [\[3\]](#ref_3)

Since finite difference methods sample different dimensions with different step sizes, a single gradient estimate requires O(n) zero-order oracle calls. Stochastic gradient estimation overcomes this issue, achieving O(1) sampling complexity per gradient estimate, independent of problem dimension. The following article details stochastic gradient estimation methods in zero-order optimization, including Gaussian smoothing and uniform smoothing on the sphere, along with numerous references. I will not elaborate further here.

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/709395175](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/709395175)

It is easy to implement, more efficient in approximation, and achieves convergence rates comparable to first-order algorithms. I believe the most crucial insight is recognizing that optimizing the one-point [estimator](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1%E9%87%8F&zhida_source=entity) is essentially computing the gradient of a smoothed approximation $f_{r}$ of $f$, so stochastic gradient estimation actually takes a stochastic gradient step with respect to $f_{r}$.

$$
f_{r}(x)=\mathbb E_{z\sim \mathcal N(x,\Sigma)}[f(x+rz)]
$$

$$
\nabla f_{r}(x)=\mathbb E_{z\sim \mathcal N(x,\Sigma)}[\frac {f(x+rz)-f(x)}{r}\Sigma^{-1}z]=\mathbb E_{z\sim \mathcal N(x,\Sigma)}[f(z)\nabla_{x}log(p_{x}(z))]
$$

Of course, [variance reduction](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%96%B9%E5%B7%AE%E7%BC%A9%E5%87%8F&zhida_source=entity) techniques come into play here. By using mini-batch techniques, we obtain the estimator. The above article also presents proofs of convergence rates.

Besides zero-order stochastic gradient descent, the estimated gradient can similarly be applied in other algorithms, such as zero-order Adam and accelerated first-order methods.

## 2. Bayesian Optimization

There is abundant material on Bayesian optimization online (although much of it is translated or repetitive), so I will not elaborate further here.

[https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/705362267?share_code=a4uYujPLtpe2&utm_psn=196576262182847049](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/705362267?share_code=a4uYujPLtpe2&utm_psn=196576262182847049)

Bayesian optimization is essentially a more scientific sequential search. The key assumption is that each zero-order oracle call is expensive, so the core goal is to maximize sample efficiency, solving the problem with the fewest sampling iterations ([number of iterations](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%AD%E4%BB%A3%E6%AD%A5%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity)). Since it is a sequential search, addressing sample efficiency requires balancing exploration and exploitation. On one hand, already explored regions can be used to roughly identify areas where lower costs may be achieved—this is exploitation; on the other hand, the locations already explored indicate which regions remain underexplored and need further information—this is exploration.

The core components are the surrogate model and the [acquisition function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9B%86%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity). The surrogate model captures both the current understanding/estimation of the problem and the uncertainty in that estimation, commonly represented by Gaussian processes (GPs). The acquisition function must leverage the mean and variance from the surrogate model to trade off between low-cost points and high-uncertainty points, while also being easy to optimize (since each BO step requires solving the acquisition function subproblem).

The advantages of this method are its efficiency—significantly reducing zero-order oracle calls—and its flexibility and robustness, as it not only suits zero-order optimization but can even handle noisy oracle queries. The drawbacks are that performance degrades in high dimensions: [nonparametric regression](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9D%9E%E5%8F%82%E6%95%B0%E5%9B%9E%E5%BD%92&zhida_source=entity) (GP) becomes difficult, the multimodal nature of the acquisition function complicates optimization, and sparsity in high-dimensional spaces drives GP predictive variance toward the prior variance, making it unable to capture function variations with limited samples. Additionally, model selection affects performance, and the choice of initial samples also has an impact.

## 3. Distribution-Based Optimization Methods

In zero-order optimization, besides population-based evolutionary methods, there is a large class known as **[Estimation of Distribution Algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity)** (EDA). These algorithms were proposed in the early 1990s, building upon population-based evolutionary algorithms.

During the execution of [genetic algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%81%97%E4%BC%A0%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity), according to the [schema theorem](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A8%A1%E5%BC%8F%E5%AE%9A%E7%90%86&zhida_source=entity), short, low-order "schemas" with high fitness values grow exponentially in the population. However, due to inappropriate [fitness function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%80%82%E5%BA%94%E5%BA%A6%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) selection, premature convergence in early stages and stagnation in later stages can occur, resulting in poor optimization—this is known as the deception problem. Different arrangements of [genes](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%9F%BA%E5%9B%A0&zhida_source=entity) alter the "schemas," changing their growth rates and thus affecting [convergence speed](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E6%94%B6%E6%95%9B%E9%80%9F%E5%BA%A6&zhida_source=entity). [Crossover operators](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BA%A4%E5%8F%89%E7%AE%97%E5%AD%90&zhida_source=entity) often disrupt "schemas," scrambling useful information—this is known as the linkage problem. To address these issues, researchers attempted to avoid "schemas" altogether by instead extracting information from a set of promising solutions, using that information to construct a suitable [probability distribution](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A6%82%E7%8E%87%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83&zhida_source=entity) (capturing global rather than individual information), and then sampling new solutions from this distribution to achieve global optimization. Consequently, EDAs are also referred to as [probabilistic model](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A6%82%E7%8E%87%E6%A8%A1%E5%9E%8B&zhida_source=entity)-based genetic algorithms.

In 1994, Baluja at CMU proposed the Population-Based Incremental Learning (PBIL) algorithm [\[4\]](#ref_4) [\[5\]](#ref_5) for [binary](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BA%8C%E8%BF%9B%E5%88%B6&zhida_source=entity)-encoded optimization problems, widely regarded as the earliest EDA. In 1996, Mühlenbein in Germany introduced the Univariate Marginal Distribution Algorithm (UMDA)[\[6\]](#ref_6). Other EDAs include the compact Genetic Algorithm (cGA)[\[7\]](#ref_7), the [Cross-Entropy](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E4%BA%A4%E5%8F%89%E7%86%B5&zhida_source=entity) Method (CEM)[\[8\]](#ref_8), [Evolution Strategies](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E8%BF%9B%E5%8C%96%E7%AD%96%E7%95%A5&zhida_source=entity) (ES)[\[9\]](#ref_9), [Covariance](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8D%8F%E6%96%B9%E5%B7%AE&zhida_source=entity) Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES)[\[10\]](#ref_10), and Natural Evolution Strategies (NES)[\[11\]](#ref_11). Most of these methods, at their inception, were as heuristic-driven as population-based [evolutionary algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=2&q=%E8%BF%9B%E5%8C%96%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity). **However, recent developments in stochastic optimization have provided a solid theoretical foundation for this class of algorithms.**

Specifically, for an unknown complex [objective function](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E7%9B%AE%E6%A0%87%E5%87%BD%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) $f：X\rightarrow \mathbb R$, discrete points can be sampled from $X$, and a continuous distribution $\mu$ can be defined on the discrete variable space, effectively assigning a probability (weight) to each discrete point in the solution space. The original problem then becomes optimizing the function $E_{x\sim \mu}\left[ f\left( x \right) \right]：\mathcal P\rightarrow \mathbb R$ over the [probability space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%A6%82%E7%8E%87%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity), where the distribution becomes the optimization variable. This is known as **stochastic relaxation**. The idea is to look at the problem from a different angle: rather than measuring the weight of a stone directly, place it in a net and observe the deformation of the net. Distribution optimization can be parametric or non-parametric, but ultimately must return to finite-dimensional optimization. These two approaches correspond to two different strategies for converting an infinite-dimensional optimization into a finite-dimensional one—parameterization and particle-based approximation. Remarkably, behind this lie several distinct geometric structures introduced by different probability distribution geometries.

### 1. Parametric Methods (Information Geometry)

If we restrict the distribution used in stochastic relaxation to a parametric [distribution family](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83%E6%97%8F&zhida_source=entity) $p_{\theta}$ [\[12\]](#ref_12) [\[13\]](#ref_13), then the objective function becomes $E_{x\sim p_{\theta}}\left[ f\left( x \right) \right] : \Theta \rightarrow \mathbb R$ , denoted as $\mathcal L\left( \theta \right)$. The original problem is transformed into an optimization problem over the [parameter space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8F%82%E6%95%B0%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) of the distribution. After parameterization, since the infinite-dimensional distribution space is approximated by a finite-dimensional information manifold, the optimization problem naturally becomes finite-dimensional as well.

The family of probability distributions

$$
\mathcal M=\left\{ p\left( x,\theta \right) \right\}
$$

constitutes an information manifold. Being a Riemannian manifold, one can naturally introduce the natural gradient step

$$
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta_{t}G^{-1}\left( \theta_{t} \right)\nabla \mathcal L\left( \theta \right)
$$

or its continuous counterpart, the natural gradient flow

$$
\dot \theta\left( t \right)=-\tilde{\nabla_{\theta}} \mathcal L\left( \theta \right)=-G^{-1}\left( \theta_{t} \right)\nabla \mathcal L\left( \theta \right)
$$

where

$$
G=\left( g_{ij} \left( \theta \right)\right), \quad g_{ij} \left( \theta \right)=E_{x\sim p_{\theta}}\left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_{i}}\log p_{\theta} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_{j}} \log p_{\theta} \right]
$$

is the Riemannian metric on the information manifold. This idea is conceptually consistent with the Newton step: Newton's method achieves local quadratic convergence by exploiting the local curvature information of the objective function, while the natural gradient accelerates convergence by similarly accounting for the [geometric structure](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%87%A0%E4%BD%95%E7%BB%93%E6%9E%84&zhida_source=entity) (Riemannian metric) of the solution space.

Furthermore, one can show that the natural gradient step satisfies

$$
\delta \theta^{*}= \frac{\tilde\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal L}{\parallel \tilde\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal L \parallel} = \underset{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0}{\lim}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\underset{KL\left( p_{\theta+\delta \theta}||p_{\theta}\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}/2}{\arg\max}\mathcal L\left( \theta+\delta \theta \right)
$$

That is, it corresponds to the steepest descent direction subject to a small KL divergence constraint. Moreover, for small $t$ we have

$$
\theta \left( t\right)=\underset{\theta}{\arg\max}\left( t\mathcal L\left( \theta \right) + H\left[ p_{\theta}\right]\right)+o\left( t\right)
$$

where $H\left[ p \right]$ denotes the Shannon entropy, indicating that early on this approach balances exploration and exploitation to a certain extent.

By further introducing [quantile](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%88%86%E4%BD%8D%E6%95%B0&zhida_source=entity) transformations, this method becomes highly general, arguably unifying the Estimation of Distribution Algorithms (EDAs) while providing theoretical explanations for certain heuristic components. Through a key lemma involving proximal operators and an expansion of the KL divergence, it can be shown to be equivalent to smoothed [maximum likelihood estimation](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%9E%81%E5%A4%A7%E4%BC%BC%E7%84%B6%E4%BC%B0%E8%AE%A1&zhida_source=entity). The Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) shares a similar philosophy and becomes exactly equivalent under the expectation parameterization of [exponential families](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%8C%87%E6%95%B0%E6%97%8F%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83&zhida_source=entity). Under the [Gaussian distribution](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%AB%98%E6%96%AF%E5%88%86%E5%B8%83&zhida_source=entity) family, we naturally recover CMA-ES and NES methods; under the Bernoulli distribution, with appropriate settings, we obtain PBIL, cGA, UMDA algorithms, and so on.

### 2. Non-parametric Methods with Wasserstein Geometry

Parameterization inherently involves selecting a specific family of distributions, which effectively constrains the solution space. Is it possible to optimize directly over the space of probability distributions without parameterization? The answer is yes: the optimization of the functional

$$
\mathcal L\left[ \mu \right]=E_{x\sim \mu}\left[ f\left( x \right) \right] : \mathcal P \rightarrow \mathbb R
$$

can also be carried out using gradient-type algorithms.

Let us choose $P_{2}\left( \mathbb R^{d} \right)\subset \mathcal P$ as the set of distributions on $\mathbb R^{d}$ with finite variance, and endow it with the Wasserstein distance, specifically the 2-Wasserstein distance $W_{2}$. This yields the metric space $\mathcal W_{2}=\left( P_{2}\left( \mathbb R^{d} \right) , W_{2}\right)$, on which we define the Wasserstein gradient flow. The resulting optimization dynamics is a PDE. For a deterministic system whose state variable is a distribution $\mu$, the [convergence](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E6%94%B6%E6%95%9B%E6%80%A7&zhida_source=entity) of the optimization can be characterized through the convergence of the distribution itself during iteration.

$$
\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-\nabla_{\mathcal W_{2}}\left( \mathcal L\left[ \mu \right] \right)
$$

This involves defining the derivative of a distribution with respect to time and the notion of gradient within $\mathcal W_{2}$ (which requires techniques from optimal transport to endow $\mathcal W_{2}$ with a Riemannian manifold structure). According to the continuity equation, we have:

$$
\partial_{t}\mu_{t} = -\nabla \left( \mu_{t}v_{t}\right) =-\nabla \left( \mu_{t}\nabla_{\mathcal W_{2}}\left( \mathcal L\left[ \mu \right] \right)\right) =-\nabla \left( \mu_{t}\nabla\delta \mathcal L\left[ \mu \right] \right)
$$

It can be shown that when $\mathcal L\left[ \mu \right]=KL\left( \mu||q \right)=E_{x\sim \mu}\left[ \log\frac{\mu}{q} \right]$, the above dynamics reduces to the Fokker-Planck equation (the “trajectory-distribution” dual form of Langevin diffusion). As is well known, discretizing Langevin diffusion via the Euler-Maruyama method yields the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (also known as the Langevin Monte Carlo algorithm), a variant of the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm. The sequence of $X_{n}$ generated by LMC provides biased samples of $\mu$, while incorporating a Metropolis-adjustment step at each iteration yields unbiased sampling. Correspondingly, applying forward-flow splitting discretization to the Wasserstein gradient flow for the KL divergence yields the distributional form of LMC.

Similarly, when the cost function is more complex, the above formulation leads to a more general PDE governing the distribution evolution, potentially a generalized FPK equation. More general diffusion equations can be obtained via the “trajectory-distribution” duality. Furthermore, the system is perturbed by [stochastic errors](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E9%9A%8F%E6%9C%BA%E8%AF%AF%E5%B7%AE&zhida_source=entity) arising from random sampling estimation. For such infinite-dimensional [dynamical systems](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%8A%A8%E5%8A%9B%E7%B3%BB%E7%BB%9F&zhida_source=entity), many operations must be performed through stochastic sampling, and here we naturally introduce “trajectory-distribution” dual sampling. This is essentially approximating the solution of the PDE via particle-based ODEs or SDEs (generalized diffusion equations).

### 3. Non-parametric Methods via RKHS Embedding

Beyond defining a Wasserstein distance to induce a Riemannian metric on the distribution space, we can also embed distributions into a [Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%86%8D%E7%94%9F%E6%A0%B8Hilbert%E7%A9%BA%E9%97%B4&zhida_source=entity) (RKHS) $\mathcal H_{k}$, via the kernel mean embedding ${f}[\mu]=E_{x\sim \mu}[k(x,\cdot)]:\mathcal P\rightarrow \mathcal H_{k}$, where $k(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the kernel function of the RKHS. We then compute gradient flows using [Stein's method](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=Stein%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) and kernel techniques. Recall the PDE dynamics for distribution optimization discussed earlier:

$$
\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-\nabla_{\mathcal H_{k}}\left( \mathcal L\left[ \mu \right] \right)
$$

According to the continuity equation:

$$
\partial_{t}\mu_{t} = -\nabla \left( \mu_{t}v_{t}\right) =-\nabla \left( \mu_{t} \phi^{*}\right)
$$

The solution of this PDE can be approximated by particle ODEs. For $\mathcal H_{k}$, the associated gradient flow is given by $\dot x = \phi^{*}(x)$, yielding the variational gradient step $x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}\phi^{*}(x_{k})$.

The construction of the gradient flow requires Stein's method, and Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) is a prototypical (and arguably the currently most mature) algorithm of this class, where $\phi^{*}$ is derived via Stein's method. It can be shown that when $\mathcal L\left[ \mu \right]=KL\left( \mu||q \right)=E_{x\sim \mu}\left[ \log\frac{\mu}{q} \right]$, we have

$$
\phi^{*}(x)=\underset{\parallel \phi\parallel_{\mathcal H_{k}}\leq 1}{\arg\max}\left(-\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}KL\left(\mu_{\varepsilon}||q\right)\right)=E_{y\sim\mu}[\mathcal A_{p(y)}k(y,x)]
$$

where $\mu_{\varepsilon}$ is the distribution after a variational gradient step, and $\mathcal A$ is the Stein operator. The core theory here involves Stein operator theory and the reproducing property of kernels in the RKHS. The action of the Stein operator consists of two terms: the Stein-score term $s_{q(y)}k(y,x)=\nabla _{y}\log q(y)k(y,x)$, which acts as a tangent vector on the distribution manifold, driving particles toward the target distribution; and the diffusion term $\nabla _{y}k(y,x)$, which maintains repulsion between particles. These two terms together balance exploration and exploitation.

Of course, because non-parametric methods operate on the distribution space rather than a parameter space, their cost function currently always involves both the current optimizing distribution and a target distribution. This requires computing gradients of the target distribution $q(x)$, which in problems such as trajectory optimization typically involves setting $q(x)$ in relation to the cost function—equivalent to requiring differentiability of the original cost. In such cases, other zero-order methods must be incorporated.

## IV. Consensus-Based Optimization

The model-based methods described above all involve a single particle searching within the feasible space. In contrast, population-based [metaheuristic algorithms](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%85%83%E5%90%AF%E5%8F%91%E5%BC%8F%E7%AE%97%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) such as Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), along with the vast array of subsequent heuristic designs, draw inspiration from the collective behavior of biological swarms (e.g., bird flocks, ant colonies) to obtain zero-order optimization algorithms. However, these [heuristic methods](https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=263915631&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=%E5%90%AF%E5%8F%91%E5%BC%8F%E6%96%B9%E6%B3%95&zhida_source=entity) lack theoretical support.
